## What this page solves

Remote trail risk is not just “injury”; it is injury plus hours before anyone finds you. Rescue distance, changing weather, signal dead zones and group condition force a sequence of correct decisions in the field.

This page does not teach advanced medical procedures or replace rescue teams. It gives a field framework: what to do first, what not to do, and when forward progress must stop.

## Three priorities: person, insulation, position

For any abnormality, stop and check in order: is the person alert, speaking, walking normally; are they cold, wet, exposed to wind; do they know where they are and which retreat point is closest? Do not manage while running, and do not leave the group while hunting signal.

| Order | Check | Immediate action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Person | Consciousness, breathing, speech, gait | Stop; sit/shelter |
| 2 Insulation | Wet clothes, wind, temperature, shivering | Dry layer, warm layer, insulating mat |
| 3 Position | Last certain point, retreat, route | Offline/paper map |
| 4 Help | Signal, SOS, contact | Send location and plan; stay put |

## Hypothermia: stop heat loss before progress

Early signals include continuous shivering, clumsy movements, slurred speech and poor judgement. Do not “run to get warm”: running increases heat loss and fall risk. Shelter, dry clothes, insulation, carbohydrate and gradual rewarming come first. Severe hypothermia (shivering stops, altered consciousness, cannot walk) is an emergency requiring rescue and professional rewarming.

| Stage | Presentation | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mild | Shivering, stiff hands, still walking | Shelter, dry clothes, warmth, warm drink, carbs |
| Moderate | Slurred speech, clumsy, poor judgement | Stop, insulate, call for help |
| Severe | No shivering, altered consciousness, cannot walk | Emergency; minimise rough movement; rescue |

Do not feed someone with altered consciousness; do not use alcohol to “warm up”; do not use intense exercise or hot water for rapid rewarming.

## Heat illness: cool first, not just drink

Early heat illness is dizziness, nausea, headache, stopping sweating and weaving gait. Move to shade, remove extra clothing, cool the neck, armpits and groin with cold water/ice, and sip fluids. Altered consciousness, seizures or inability to walk is a heat-stroke emergency: call rescue and keep cooling.

| Signal | Action |
| --- | --- |
| Dizziness, nausea, heavy sweating | Shade, fluids, cooling, rest |
| Sweating stops, hot skin | Aggressive cooling, stop racing |
| Altered consciousness, seizure | Emergency; rescue; continuous cooling |
| Vomiting, cannot drink | Stop; seek medical care |

Do not gamble “one more kilometre to the aid station”; heat stroke worsens fast.

## Altitude: recognise HAPE/HACE

Headache, nausea and poor sleep can be mild altitude illness, but resting breathlessness, cough, pink frothy sputum, altered consciousness or inability to walk suggests HAPE/HACE. The core treatment is descent and help, not painkillers or “one more night to acclimatise”.

| Presentation | Interpretation | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Headache, nausea, poor sleep | Mild altitude illness | Stop ascent, fluid, rest |
| Resting breathlessness, cough | Suspect HAPE | Descend, call help |
| Altered consciousness, unsteady gait | Suspect HACE | Descend/rescue immediately |
| Cannot walk | Emergency | Call rescue; do not leave alone |

## Lost: return to the last certain point

The danger is not extra kilometres; it is entering steep ground, cliffs or valleys while searching. Stop and identify the last certain point. In poor visibility, contour back to known terrain; do not cut a new line. Darkness, dropping temperature or a deteriorating teammate means retreat nearby or shelter until daylight.

| Situation | Decision |
| --- | --- |
| Off-route <200 m, visible terrain | Return to last certain point |
| Off-route >200 m, fog/night | Stop cutting; contour back |
| Dark, no direction | Shelter, send help, wait for daylight |
| Injury/hypothermia | Retreat nearby; abandon plan |

## Calling for help: say it once, clearly

Include location (coordinates or landmark), number of people, injury/symptoms, weather, kit, remaining water and food, planned route, and whether helicopter/stretcher is needed. After sending, stay put and conserve battery and energy; do not climb into dangerous ground “for better signal”.

Without signal, use whistle, light, mirror and bright clothing. With two or more people, assign roles: one stays with the injured person, one seeks signal, with an agreed return time.

## Two worked examples

**Winter 50K, runner shivering and slowing at 35 km.** The group stops, changes to dry clothes, adds a down layer and insulating mat, gives a warm drink and carbohydrate. If there is no improvement in 10 minutes or consciousness changes, send for help and retreat nearby. Do not continue to the 40 km aid station.

**Summer 30K, runner dizzy, no longer sweating, weaving.** Move to shade, remove clothing, cool neck/armpits/groin, sip fluids. If consciousness does not recover, treat as heat stroke and call rescue. “The next station” is not the goal.

## Boundaries

This page covers field recognition and sequence management only; it does not provide drug doses, injections, reductions or wound closure. In remote terrain the correct decision is often “stop, call, wait”, not “try once more”.

## Help-message template

Save this template in a phone note and fill in race contact and companions before starting; it can be read to a passer-by or written on paper without signal.

| Field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Location | 30.1234 N, 102.5678 E; or “3 km from CP5, left side of the valley” |
| People | 2; one can walk, one cannot weight-bear |
| Symptoms | Shivering, slurred speech, low temperature; or dizzy, no sweating |
| Weather | Strong wind, rain, visibility 20 m |
| Kit | Emergency blanket, headlamp, 1 L water, no satellite device |
| Plan | Was heading to CP6; now retreating to CP5 |
| Need | Rescue/stretcher/insulation |

After sending, do not repeatedly resend long messages; use a three-part “status—location—need” text to save battery.

## Team roles

With two or more people, assign roles: one stays with the injured person and insulates; one seeks signal/help; a third, if present, records time, symptoms and decisions. Do not send everyone to find signal, and do not leave the injured person alone in the wind.

Agree a return time: if the signal-seeker is not back in 15–20 minutes, the remaining person treats help as unsuccessful and starts the backup plan (whistle, light, descend).

## Common errors

- Walking onto a ridge or cliff to find signal.
- Feeding or medicating someone with altered consciousness.
- Using alcohol, hot water or intense exercise for rapid rewarming.
- Continuing to an aid station with heat illness.
- Cutting a new line when lost and getting steeper.
- Leaving the injured person alone while the whole group seeks help.

The correct order often feels slow: stop, insulate, locate, call, wait. Slow is how rescue finds you.

## Night and sudden weather

Night rescue is exponentially harder. If someone is deteriorating, the route is uncertain or weather is turning before dark, shelter nearby early rather than “one more hour to the next point”. At night every person needs a primary and backup light; the group stays together; river crossings, steep descents and scree slow down. Twenty extra minutes beat a fall in the dark.

When weather turns, leave ridges, valleys and isolated trees first and reduce exposure; do not raise poles or stand on the high point in hail, lightning or strong wind. Mark retreat points on the map in advance and execute when weather changes, not “wait and see”.

## Three minutes before starting

Write the route, retreat points, weather window and contact on paper, put it in a waterproof bag; tell a companion “if I am not back in 20 minutes, treat it as a call for help”. Three minutes can save hours of searching.
