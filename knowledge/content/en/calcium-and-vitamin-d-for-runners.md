## Why runners care about these two

What runners fear is not the word "deficiency" but a **bone stress injury** — it directly interrupts training and racing. In its explanation of medial tibial stress syndrome, the AAOS warns that pain on the inner shin can also come from a **stress fracture**, tendinitis or chronic exertional compartment syndrome, and needs to be differentiated rather than treated as generic "shin pain".

Calcium is the main mineral component of bone and teeth; vitamin D takes part in calcium absorption and bone metabolism. The NIH ODS calcium fact sheet states explicitly that net absorption of dietary calcium falls markedly when **vitamin D status is low**. Another runner-relevant thread is energy: the 2023 IOC consensus on relative energy deficiency in sport (REDs) states that **problematic low energy availability is associated with reduced musculoskeletal health and increased injury risk**.

Put the three together and the conclusion is: **calcium and vitamin D are underlying conditions for bone health, not a treatment for bone stress injury.** Persistent bone pain should first be assessed for stress fracture and load problems, not answered with supplements.

## Tolerable upper intake levels (do not exceed)

The calcium UL covers all sources — food, drink and supplements (NIH ODS):

|---|---:|
| 0–6 months | 1,000 mg/day |
| 7–12 months | 1,500 mg/day |
| 1–8 years | 2,500 mg/day |
| 9–18 years | 3,000 mg/day |
| 19–50 years | 2,500 mg/day |
| Over 51 years | 2,000 mg/day |
| Pregnant/lactating adolescents | 3,000 mg/day |

| Age | Upper level |
|---|---:|

The UL is "the highest daily intake unlikely to cause adverse effects", not a target. A doctor treating a deficiency may prescribe a dose above the UL for a short period; that is a medical act and outside this page.

About **72% of calcium intake in the United States comes from dairy products and foods containing dairy**. Non-dairy sources include sardines or salmon with bones, kale, broccoli and bok choy. Fortified foods include juices, plant milks, tofu and ready-to-eat cereals (NIH ODS).

Few foods naturally contain vitamin D; in the US diet, **fortified foods provide most of the vitamin D** (NIH ODS). That is why it is hard to reach 15–20 mcg reliably from natural foods alone.

## Absorption: the same label does not mean the same amount reaching bone

Calcium absorption is not "eat it, absorb it":

- Dairy and fortified foods absorb at about **30%**; spinach only about **5%**, because oxalate forms poorly absorbed salts; milk about **27%** (NIH ODS).
- Supplement absorption is highest at **single doses below 500 mg**; a 300 mg dose absorbs about **36%** and a 1,000 mg dose about **28%** (NIH ODS).
- Caffeine and phosphorus slightly reduce net calcium absorption; **low vitamin D status reduces it markedly** (NIH ODS).

The practical meaning for runners: **spreading calcium across meals works better than one large dose**; high-oxalate foods such as spinach and beet greens are not efficient calcium sources, and "I ate spinach" does not cancel a dairy shortfall.

## Sunlight and vitamin D: the boundary

Key NIH ODS points:

- The RDA assumes **minimal sun exposure**; sunlight is the main source for some people, but not everyone can reach the RDA from sunlight.
- Skin limits how much vitamin D is synthesised, so **you cannot become vitamin D toxic from sun exposure**; however, **sunscreen, dark skin, increasing age and spending little time outdoors** all reduce synthesis.
- Ultraviolet radiation from the sun carries its own health risks; this page gives no universal "minutes in the sun" figure, because skin colour, latitude, season, cloud cover, clothing and time of day all change the result.

Practical boundary (this site's suggestion, not a research finding): do not aim for "sunburn"; if you already train outdoors, count food intake first, then decide whether supplementation is needed; if you train mainly indoors, live at high latitude or in winter, or use sunscreen consistently, discuss testing with a doctor.

## Deficiency and excess

**Calcium deficiency** (NIH ODS): can cause osteoporosis (weak, fracture-prone bone), rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults. An analysis of US NHANES 2007–2010 found that **39% of people aged 4 and over** had calcium intake from food and supplements below the estimated average requirement; mean intake was 1,083 mg for adult men and 842 mg for women.

**Vitamin D deficiency** (NIH ODS): blood 25(OH)D below **30 nmol/L (12 ng/mL)** is associated with deficiency and can cause rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults; **30–50 nmol/L (12–20 ng/mL)** is generally considered insufficient; **≥50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL)** is sufficient for most people; **>125 nmol/L (50 ng/mL)** may be associated with adverse effects. About **one quarter of the US population** has low or insufficient vitamin D status.

**Excess**:

- Vitamin D blood levels **above 375 nmol/L (150 ng/mL)** can cause nausea, vomiting, muscle weakness, confusion, pain, loss of appetite, dehydration, polyuria and thirst, and kidney stones; extremely high levels can cause kidney failure, irregular heartbeat and even death, and **almost always come from supplement excess** (NIH ODS).
- High blood or urine calcium produces poor muscle tone, poor kidney function, low phosphate, constipation, nausea, weight loss, extreme fatigue, polyuria and arrhythmia, but this is usually caused by conditions such as parathyroid problems or cancer rather than by "eating too much calcium" (NIH ODS).

## Should healthy people be tested routinely?

The NIH ODS vitamin D fact sheet states that blood 25(OH)D reflects vitamin D status, but the **Endocrine Society does not recommend routine 25(OH)D testing in healthy people**. Who is more likely to be low: people who get little sun or keep their skin covered, people with dark skin, older people, people with fat malabsorption (for example Crohn's disease, coeliac disease, ulcerative colitis), and breastfed infants (NIH ODS).

Runners can use this to ask "am I someone who should discuss testing with a doctor", but **should not self-increase doses based on "ideal values" circulating online**. Whether to test, when to test and how to interpret the result should be decided by a doctor together with symptoms, medication and diet.

## Runner scenarios

- **High mileage plus indoor training**: both sunlight and food sources may fall short; first meet the RDA for calcium and vitamin D each day, then discuss supplementation.
- **Rehabilitating a bone stress injury**: nutrition is one part; it does not replace load management, shoe and surface assessment, or imaging and orthopaedic assessment. Persistent localised bone pain should be assessed first (the AAOS notes that stress fracture must be differentiated).
- **Before a race**: do not start a large-dose supplement at the last minute; there is no evidence that a short-term high dose improves performance, and it may cause gastrointestinal upset.
- **Taper or low energy availability**: if energy intake has been low for a long time, address energy availability first (IOC REDs consensus), not just calcium tablets.

## Common misconceptions

- **"More calcium is better"**: there is a UL; above it there is no extra bone benefit and there may be gastrointestinal upset and other risks.
- **"Calcium alone is enough"**: low vitamin D status markedly reduces calcium absorption; look at both together.
- **"Sun exposure always covers it"**: skin synthesis is limited by sunscreen, skin colour, age, season and clothing; sunlight is one source, not a guarantee.
- **"Normal blood test means diet is fine"**: a normal test does not mean intake is adequate; conversely, healthy people are not advised to test routinely.
- **"Calcium supplements treat bone stress injury"**: calcium and vitamin D are underlying materials, not treatment; bone stress injury needs load management and medical assessment.
- **"Supplements can replace food"**: food provides protein, phosphorus, potassium and many other nutrients; a supplement adds a single component.

## When to seek medical care

- Localised bone pain that persists, night pain, a precise point of tenderness, swelling or inability to bear weight → seek assessment for stress fracture (the AAOS explicitly lists stress fracture among the causes of shin pain that must be differentiated).
- Constipation, nausea, polyuria, extreme fatigue or arrhythmia suggesting high blood calcium → seek medical care.
- After long-term high-dose vitamin D supplementation, nausea, vomiting, muscle weakness, confusion, thirst and polyuria → stop and seek medical care.
- If you have Crohn's disease, coeliac disease, ulcerative colitis, thyroid or parathyroid disease, or take medication that affects calcium absorption long-term → discuss dose and testing with a doctor.

## Scope and boundaries

This page is nutrition education. It provides no diagnosis, prescription, brand or individualised supplementation plan. The recommended intakes and ULs come from NIH ODS dietary reference intakes for healthy people; specific plans for people who are ill, pregnant, lactating, children or older adults must be set by a doctor or registered dietitian. Food amounts are label values per serving; actual content varies with brand, processing and cooking.

## How to schedule supplements: split doses, review, one example

Scope: this arithmetic is for topping up an ordinary diet, not for treating osteoporosis, kidney stones or hypercalcaemia, which need individual medical management.

A worked example (hypothetical): target 1000 mg/day, diet supplies about 600 mg, gap 400 mg. A calcium label usually states the compound weight, not elemental calcium: calcium carbonate is about 40% elemental, calcium citrate about 21%. A tablet labelled 500 mg calcium carbonate supplies about 200 mg elemental calcium, so two tablets are needed; common advice is to keep a single dose at or below about 500 mg elemental calcium and take it with meals.

Review rhythm: check intake against a food record every 2–3 months; if you also take vitamin D, review dose and sun exposure on the same cycle rather than treating them as separate projects.
