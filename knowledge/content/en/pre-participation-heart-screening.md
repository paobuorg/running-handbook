## Definitions and scope

**Pre-participation cardiovascular screening** is the process of looking, before training or competition, for cardiac conditions that raise the risk of sudden death during exercise. It uses personal history, family history, symptoms and physical examination, with a 12-lead ECG added in some systems. It is not a full medical check-up and it is not a diagnosis.

This page is for runners preparing for a race, or wondering whether they need a heart check. It summarises what the public documents of major organisations say and where their limits lie. It does **not** make clinical judgments and does **not** turn specific tests into a prescription; those decisions belong to qualified clinicians.

## Verification result: common starting points, but three organisations disagree

Checking whether a citable common starting point exists gives a qualified yes:

- **AHA (American Heart Association)**: annual screening with a 14-element personal history, family history and physical examination. It does not recommend a mandatory 12-lead ECG or echocardiogram; these are used after a positive finding.
- **ESC (European Society of Cardiology)**: screening of competitive athletes should include family history, symptoms, physical examination and a **resting 12-lead ECG** (Class IIa, Level C).
- **IOC (International Olympic Committee)**: its position is close to the ESC, advocating screening that includes a resting 12-lead ECG (as summarised in a 2016 review).

This page therefore covers **differences between organisations, symptoms that require assessment, and the fact that screening does not eliminate risk**. It does not decide which organisation is right.

## What the three organisations say

| Organisation and document | Population | First round | Place of the ECG |
|---|---|---|---|
| AHA, 2007 statement (12 elements) and policy guidance (14 elements) | Young competitive athletes | Annual personal history, family history and physical examination (12 elements in 2007; policy guidance later expanded to 14) | **Not a mandatory screen**; ECG and echocardiography follow positive findings |
| ESC, 2020 sports cardiology guidelines | Competitive athletes | Family history, symptoms, examination + resting 12-lead ECG | **Recommended** (IIa, C) |
| IOC (as summarised in a 2016 review) | Competitive athletes | History, examination + resting 12-lead ECG | **Recommended** |

The AHA's 14 elements cover exertional chest pain, unexplained syncope, exertional dyspnoea or fatigue out of proportion to training, a previous murmur or high blood pressure, prior restriction from sport, and premature family death or cardiomyopathy. The design intent is not "test everything" but "identify cheaply who needs further assessment".

## Where the disagreement lies: should the ECG be in the first round?

- **The AHA's concerns**: low prevalence in the target population limits positive predictive value; both false positives and false negatives exist; there are too few clinicians who can read athlete ECGs accurately; mandatory testing raises access and equity problems.
- **The ESC and IOC rationale**: for cardiomyopathies and channelopathies, the ECG is more sensitive than history and examination alone.
- **The evidence is not one-sided**: Williams et al. (2019) studied high-school athletes and found the AHA 14-point evaluation produced many false positives, poor sensitivity and very low positive predictive value, while ECG interpreted by experienced clinicians outperformed it on every statistical measure. The AHA policy document cites this trial but stresses that the comparison assumes the 14 elements are used alone rather than followed by further testing after a positive finding.

That is what "common starting points but inconsistent criteria" means in practice.

## The numbers: benefit, false positives and false negatives

| Measure | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Estimated annual incidence of sudden cardiac death in high-school athletes | **1 in 23,000 to 1 in 300,000** | AHA policy guidance |
| False-positive rate of physician-read ECG | Generally **1.3%–2.8%**; up to **6.8%** in Arab and Black athletes | AHA policy guidance (citing a 2020 systematic review) |
| False-positive rate of computer-read ECG | **2.3%–5%** | Same |
| False-negative rate of ECG for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy | **10%** | Same |
| After the Veneto screening programme in Italy | Annual sudden cardiovascular death in young competitive athletes fell **89%** (3.6 to 0.4 per 100,000 person-years, 1979–1980 to 2003–2004); unscreened non-athletes showed no significant change | Corrado et al., JAMA 2006 |
| Proportion asymptomatic before sudden death | Up to **80%** | MacLachlan & Drezner 2020 review |

Two conclusions follow: screening can produce a population-level benefit (the Italian experience), but any single screen can miss (about 10% false negatives for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and most victims have no symptoms) and can also over-call (false positives of 1.3%–6.8%).

## Symptoms that require assessment

Warning signs listed across the international screening documents:

- **Chest pain, tightness or pressure** during or after exercise
- **Syncope or near-syncope**, especially during exercise
- **Palpitations or an irregular heartbeat**
- **Breathlessness or fatigue clearly out of proportion** to the workload
- A family member who died suddenly from heart disease **before age 50**, or has cardiomyopathy, Marfan syndrome, long QT syndrome or a related diagnosis
- A previous **heart murmur, high blood pressure**, or restriction from sport for cardiac reasons

Any one of these should prompt stopping vigorous exercise and seeking medical assessment. **What tests follow is decided by a clinician**; this page provides neither a test list nor drug or treatment recommendations.

## Screening does not eliminate risk

- **Asymptomatic is the norm.** The review reports that up to 80% of young athletes have no symptoms before sudden cardiac arrest or death, so history-based screening cannot identify them.
- **A normal ECG does not exclude structural disease.** The false-negative rate for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is about 10%, and some changes are intermittent.
- **The 14 elements alone have limited accuracy.** Williams 2019 found poor sensitivity and very low positive predictive value.
- **Abnormal does not mean diseased.** Physiological athlete's heart produces ECG changes that mimic pathology; without athlete-specific interpretation, false positives rise.

"I had a check-up and it was fine" is therefore not a reason to ignore symptoms during exercise.

## Recreational runners and competitive athletes are not the same pathway

For individuals at low to moderate cardiovascular risk, the ESC 2020 guidelines consider participation in all recreational sports without further cardiovascular evaluation (IIa, C); the ECG-inclusive recommendation applies to **competitive athletes**. This does not mean recreational runners should ignore cardiac risk. It means the strategy changes with prevalence, exercise intensity and the rules of the sport.

For most readers, the practical question is not "which competitive-athlete protocol should I follow" but "do I have warning signs, and do I have risk factors a clinician should assess".

## Runners over 35 and masters athletes

The ESC 2020 guidelines note that evidence for cardiovascular screening in athletes over 35 is limited and that screening in this group must target **atherosclerotic coronary artery disease**. They also state that **routine exercise testing to screen for ischaemia in asymptomatic adults is not recommended**, because positive predictive value is low and false positives are common.

A treadmill ECG is therefore not a universal safety certificate, and should not be treated as a mandatory pre-race purchase. Risk stratification, symptom assessment and the need for further investigation belong to clinicians.

## A worked example

A 45-year-old preparing for a first half marathon asks whether a heart check is needed. A defensible framework:

1. **Symptoms first**: chest pain, syncope, palpitations, unusual breathlessness? If yes, seek medical care before entering the race.
2. **Then risk factors**: family history, smoking, high blood pressure, diabetes, abnormal lipids. These inform a clinician's risk assessment; they are not a self-diagnosis tool.
3. **The screening method is a clinical decision**: history and examination, resting ECG, further tests if indicated. AHA, ESC and IOC differ, and local practice differs too.
4. **A normal result still requires vigilance**: passing a screen does not mean nothing will happen during exercise; symptoms still mean stop and seek care.

## Common misconceptions

- **"There is one global screening standard"**: there is not. The AHA does not mandate ECG; the ESC and IOC recommend it.
- **"A normal ECG means nothing is wrong"**: the ECG false-negative rate for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is about 10%, and a normal result cannot exclude every structural problem.
- **"More tests must be safer"**: the ESC explicitly does not recommend routine exercise testing for ischaemia in asymptomatic adults; there is no evidence for unlimited add-ons in low-risk people.
- **"An abnormal athlete ECG means disease"**: physiological changes are common and require athlete-specific interpretation.
- **"Recreational runners need not think about this"**: the ESC advice is that low-risk recreational sport needs no further evaluation, not that symptoms can be ignored.
- **"Screening replaces emergency response"**: it does not. Screening identifies risk beforehand; emergency response handles an event when it happens.

## Checklist

- [ ] Have I had chest pain, syncope, palpitations or unusual breathlessness during exercise?
- [ ] Is there a family history of sudden death before 50, or of cardiomyopathy, Marfan syndrome or long QT?
- [ ] Do I know whether my local race or sport requires pre-participation screening?
- [ ] Am I treating "my check-up was normal" as a reason to ignore symptoms?
- [ ] Do I understand that organisations disagree about the ECG?
- [ ] If a warning sign appears, will I stop and seek care rather than continue training?

## Limits of this page

This page is health education and source summarisation. It does not provide clinical judgment, test prescriptions, drugs or treatment. Screening strategy should be decided by qualified clinicians according to local guidelines. Stop exercise and call emergency services if chest pain, syncope, persistent palpitations, unusual breathlessness or collapse occurs during or after exercise.
