## What this page solves

Multi-day races are rarely won on the fastest day; they are lost when the slowest day leaves you unable to start again. Course, fuelling, sleep and foot damage accumulate. Copying a one-day ultra plan into three days usually collapses on the second night.

This page separates a stage race into four ledgers: pacing, fuelling, sleep and damage. It does not replace race medical staff or a clinician, and no pace table fits everyone.

## Three variables: distance, climb, recoverability

Every day asks: after today, can I stand on tomorrow's start line? Recoverability depends on distance and climb, technical descent, and how many hours you have to eat and sleep. Arrange hard–moderate–hard rather than “all out every day”.

| Day type | Goal | Pace | Fuel | Next-day impact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | Build margin | 10–15% slower than ability | Hourly intake, early | Low |
| Middle | Hold position | Steady, no chasing | Solid/liquid alternate | Moderate |
| Key | Hardest terrain | Conservative start, finish strong | High carb, early caffeine | High |
| Final | Finish | By accumulated fatigue | Easy to digest, low fibre | Low |

## Daily pacing: repeatable, not explosive

Day-one pace should let you walk, eat breakfast and tie your shoes the next morning. If the last 10 km already required pure willpower, day two probably will not start on plan.

A workable start: 15–25% slower than your best one-day pace, walk-run the climbs, do not sprint the descents. Every 10 km check breathing (can you speak in short sentences?), gait and new hot spots. If two of three are wrong, downgrade the rest of the day.

## Fuelling by segment, not by day

The unit is “to the next aid station or sleep stop”, not “24 hours today”. Split each day into 3–5 segments with one carbohydrate and one fluid target; execute inside the segment, do not recalculate.

| Window | Carbohydrate | Fluid | Sodium | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Morning start | Previous night's load; small amount 1–2 h before | 300–500 mL | By sweat | Do not fill empty |
| Morning | 50–70 g/h | Thirst and weather | Electrolyte included | Little solid |
| Afternoon | 60–80 g/h | Small frequent sips | More in heat | Avoid high concentration |
| Night | 40–60 g/h | Warm drink | By sweat | Cautious caffeine |
| Before sleep | 20–30 g protein + carb | 300–500 mL | Not a large load | Eat first |

Gut problems often come from “the same gel every day”. Carry at least two carbohydrate sources and one savoury solid; when nauseated, change flavour and temperature before increasing concentration.

## Sleep: write the debt into the plan

There is no catching up in a stage race, only borrowing less. Aim for 4–6 hours per 24 hours and prioritise 6 hours on key nights; sleep loss amplifies pain, suppresses appetite and raises hypothermia and navigation risk.

| Sleep chance | Duration | Priority | Do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Night 1 | 5–6 h | High | Wash, dry clothes, elevate feet |
| Night 2 | 4–5 h | Highest | Treat feet, carb, two alarms |
| Night 3 | 4–6 h | High | Do not organise kit; sleep |
| Day nap | 20–30 min | Medium | No longer than 30 |

Do not use large caffeine doses to “cover” sleep; caffeine hides fatigue but does not restore judgement. At night, assess speech, gait and finger dexterity every two hours; any abnormality is treated as hypothermia or low blood glucose.

## Kit: carry only today's needs

Pack weight grows every day. Split kit into “daily”, “weather window” and “sleep bag”; the sleep bag travels by race transport, not on your back. Spare shoes half a size larger for swollen feet after blisters.

| Category | Items | Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daily | Headlamp, batteries, emergency blanket, phone, waterproof | Before start |
| Weather | Warm mid-layer, gloves, hat, sunscreen | Each morning |
| Sleep bag | Dry clothes, sleeping bag, sandals, foot care | At sleep stop |
| Spare | Dry socks, spare shoes, second lamp | Each night |

## Medical and withdrawal: three hard lines

Withdrawal is not decided by “can I still walk”. Stop and seek race medical help for any of: altered consciousness or unable to wake, resting breathlessness, persistent chest pain, inability to urinate, repeated vomiting, obvious hypothermia, spreading infection, or a red-hot swollen foot extending into the calf.

Do not mask fever or infection with painkillers; do not treat withdrawal as a character failure. Finish rate depends on stopping at the right time.

## Two worked examples

**Three days, 120 km, 6,000 m climb.** Day 1: 45 km and 2,000 m at conversational effort, walk-run the last 5 km. Day 2: 35 km and 2,500 m, start 10% slower, fuel every 45 minutes in the afternoon. Day 3: 40 km and 1,500 m, let foot status decide walk breaks. Sleep 5 hours nightly, 6 on night two.

**Five-day desert race, 35°C.** One hour before start, drink 500 mL electrolyte; sip every 20 minutes in the morning; replace by sweat rate in the afternoon; about 60 g/h carbohydrate, savoury solids. Reduce effort in the hottest two hours; check toes and blisters nightly and switch to shoes half a size larger on day two.

## Common errors

- Racing day one like the final day and failing to start day two.
- Gels only until day three, when the sight of another gel makes you nauseous.
- Caffeine through the night and poor judgement the next day.
- Organising kit before sleeping and getting two hours.
- Ignoring blisters until shoes no longer fit.
- Continuing with fever, vomiting or altered consciousness past the treatable window.

## The 30-minute end-of-day routine

At the sleep stop, spend 30 minutes closing the day before touching the phone or reorganising kit. Order: wet clothes off, feet dry, blisters and hot spots checked, 300–500 mL fluid plus a carbohydrate/protein portion, two alarms, sleep. Do not wash kit, post updates or study tomorrow's route first; those can wait until after breakfast.

| Closing item | Time | Consequence of skipping |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dry clothes, dry feet | 8 min | Hypothermia, macerated feet |
| Blister/hot-spot check | 5 min | Cannot put shoes on next day |
| Fluid and small meal | 10 min | Night low blood glucose, slow recovery |
| Two alarms | 2 min | Oversleep, miss start |
| Write tomorrow's first segment | 5 min | Chaotic morning |

Tomorrow's breakfast matters more than how neatly last night's kit was arranged. Put equipment in fixed places; do not reinvent packing every day.

## Team versus solo

When solo travel is allowed, pace at “the slowest sensible speed”, not “the fastest possible”. If a teammate has blisters, sleep debt or poor appetite, the whole plan comes down. Dragging one person to withdrawal usually costs more than 30 minutes for the group.

When solo, send the daily plan, sleep stop and backup retreat to the race contact. Do not skip location updates to “avoid bothering people”; in a remote race, a check-in is an input to rescue decisions, not a formality.

## The 72 hours after

Recovery after a stage race is not “sleep for a day”. First 24 hours: fluid, carbohydrate, protein, and a foot/blister check. Hours 24–72: walking, sleep and normal meals; no deep massage, hot bath or immediate “flush” run. Fever, persistent vomiting, inability to urinate, chest pain or breathlessness is an emergency, not “wait until tomorrow”.

No quality sessions for a week after finishing; no long run for two weeks. Stage-race fatigue is cumulative; blood markers and appetite may take 2–3 weeks to return to baseline. Write the recovery week into the plan instead of waiting for the body to protest.

## The minimum completion line

Set a daily minimum: can walk, can drink, can stay warm. Below that line, stop; do not gamble medical risk for a placing or a finish.
