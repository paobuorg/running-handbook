## When to use this protocol

This protocol is for runners who already complete long training runs, want steady carbohydrate intake in a marathon or ultra, yet get stomach distress whenever they fuel. It addresses in-race gut tolerance: repeated, small-dose carbohydrate exposure in training so the stomach, gut and fluid uptake adapt, rather than opening a new gel for the first time on race day.

Confirm three premises first. You already have a regular long-run habit and a basic sense of [long-run fueling](/en/knowledge/long-run-fueling/) and [race-day fueling](/en/knowledge/race-day-fueling/); you have no untreated gastrointestinal condition, food allergy or recent GI infection; and you understand that the g/h figures below are population ranges and editorial examples, not a personal prescription — your own tolerance governs the final plan.

It does not apply to beginners without a long-run base; anyone in an active episode of diarrhoea or abdominal pain; people with diagnosed reflux, IBS or other digestive conditions not discussed with a clinician; or anyone treating gut training as a reason to skip normal meals and force sugary drink. For overall eating structure and energy intake, see [Daily fueling](/en/knowledge/daily-fueling/).

## If/then decision table

| Your current situation | Branch | Target carbohydrate | Watch-outs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hardly fuel on long runs, or get bloated as soon as you do | Start at Level 0; practise "being able to hold fuel" | 30 g/h in small repeated doses | Move up only after 2–3 long runs without distress |
| Already tolerate 30–45 g/h over 1–2.5 h | Start at Level 1 or 2 | 45–60 g/h | Fix one form (gel or drink) before adding variety |
| Preparing for an ultra or event over 2.5–3 h | Progress toward Level 3–4 | 75–90 g/h | 90 g/h only for long events, validated in training |
| Bloating, diarrhoea, reflux or cramping at any level | Stop progressing; step back a level | Return to last tolerated dose | Retry after 7–10 days; recurrent distress refers to clinical care |
| Race within 2 weeks | Do not raise dose or change brands | Keep the validated recipe | No first-time fuel on race day |

Position-statement figures: the ISSN nutrient-timing stand advises about 30–60 g/h carbohydrate for high-intensity exercise over 60 minutes, as a 6%–8% carbohydrate-electrolyte solution every 10–15 minutes, adjusted to individual tolerance and event duration. The ACSM/AND/DC 2016 joint statement frames 30–60 g/h for 1–2.5 h and up to 90 g/h beyond 2.5 h. The IOC nutrition guide likewise puts ultra-endurance intake up to 90 g/h, and stresses it must be practised in training.

## Level checklist: entry conditions, dwell time, failure step-back

The levels below are an editorial example; completing the whole ladder usually spans 4–8 weeks, and the week counts and dose steps show how to arrange the work. You may stay longer at a level, but do not skip one, and do not compress the whole sequence into two weeks to force 90 g/h. The Costa 2017 trial used ten sessions over two weeks, each one hour of running with 30 g carbohydrate every 20 minutes (about 90 g/h), as repeated exposure; it reduced GI symptoms and improved running distance. This protocol splits that strong stimulus into a gentler ladder for recreational runners.

| Level | Target carbohydrate | Entry condition | Dwell and sessions | Failure step-back |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Level 0 | 30 g/h | Can complete a 60–90 min easy run; no marked dizziness or hypoglycaemia after fasting long runs | 2–3 long runs or 3–5 days; one small dose every 15–20 min | On distress: cut or stop non-fluid fuel; next time restart at about half dose (15 g/h) |
| Level 1 | 45 g/h | Level 0 passed two straight sessions without bloating, diarrhoea or reflux; fluid rhythm stable | 2–3 sessions; about 15 g every 20 min | Return to Level 0 for 7 days, then re-enter in +5–10 g/h steps |
| Level 2 | 60 g/h | Level 1 well tolerated; runners in 1.5–2.5 h events often stay here | 3–4 sessions; introduce one solid or semi-solid form | Back to 45 g/h; record weather, pace, breakfast and symptoms |
| Level 3 | 75 g/h | Level 2 stable; event over 2.5 h; validated in a full long session | At least two ≥2 h long sessions | Back to 60 g/h; do not trial 75 g/h in race week |
| Level 4 | 90 g/h | Level 3 passed two long sessions; ultra or very long endurance; familiar with multiple carbohydrate sources | Repeated long sessions and a simulation | Back to 75 g/h; 90 g/h should not be forced in a 1–2 h race |

Dose split example: 60 g/h can be 15 g every 15 min, or 20 g every 20 min. Reaching 90 g/h usually requires multiple carbohydrate types; a single source often cannot deliver that ratio, and the combination must be validated in training — this page does not promise a fixed recipe.

## Two exposure lines: small daily doses and session trials

Gut training runs on two parallel exposure lines:

1. **Training trials:** add the target dose later in a long run or tempo session, 30–60 minutes in, to mimic late-race pressure. Validate first at easy pace, then once in a slightly harder session. The same fuel (brand, flavour, form) must appear in at least two training sessions before it counts as "trained".
2. **Daily small-dose groundwork:** arrange regular carbohydrate in training-day meals and snacks; avoid long fasting windows. The Costa 2017 trial used both gel and whole-food forms, so gut adaptation is not tied to one gel brand. For everyday carbohydrate basics, see [Carbohydrate loading](/en/knowledge/carb-loading/) and [Pre- and post-run fueling](/en/knowledge/pre-and-post-run-fueling/).

A weekly example (editorial assumption): Tuesday easy run, little or no fuel; Thursday tempo, half the Level target; Saturday long run, full target dose; Sunday rest or low-impact cross-training. Level upgrades happen only on Saturday's long run; mid-week sessions validate rather than trial anything new.

## Intolerance signals and stop rules

The Baart 2023 survey of 1,993 runners found 57% reported abdominal discomfort during running or within 3 hours after; reported GI-symptom prevalence in runners ranges from 30% to 90% in the literature, and about a third of symptomatic runners said discomfort affected their running. Common signals and handling:

| Signal | Meaning | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Upper/lower abdominal bloating, belching | Dose or form beyond current tolerance; excess swallowed air | Step back a level; swallow slowly, gel with small water |
| Diarrhoea, urgent bowel need | Gut perfusion or osmotic intolerance | Step back; check breakfast fibre, fat and caffeine; do not cut fluids in heat |
| Reflux, heartburn | Gastric emptying pressure, fasting or overfull stomach | Lower single dose; avoid a big pre-run meal; recurrent episodes see care |
| Abdominal cramping, worsening nausea | May be tolerance shortfall, but could be something else | Stop fueling and running that session; seek care if it does not settle |
| Discomfort lasting 24 h post-run | The stimulus was too large | Drop volume next week; do not progress on schedule |

Stop rules: chest pain, dizziness, fainting, persistent vomiting, bloody stool or severe abdominal pain are outside "gut training" and are treated as emergencies or care-seeking items; see [Side stitch and GI](/en/knowledge/side-stitch-and-gi/).

## Common misuse

1. **Opening a new gel on race day.** Every fuel form, brand, flavour and dose must have been validated in a training long run; race day executes the proven combination only.
2. **Pushing 90 g/h for everyone.** 90 g/h is the upper figure for very long events; forcing it in a 1–2 h race carries more gut risk than benefit.
3. **Loading the whole daily dose into one session.** Adaptation comes from repeated small doses, not one large bolus; a single meal beyond gastric emptying only manufactures a failed experience.
4. **Ignoring weather and pace.** Heat, steep climbs and faster pace all change gastric emptying and sweating; tolerance in a cool easy run does not guarantee race-day tolerance.
5. **Treating symptoms as willpower.** Recurrent diarrhoea or reflux calls for plan adjustment and, when needed, referral, not "run through it".

## Applicable boundaries

This protocol is endurance nutrition education, not a medical plan, and does not replace a sports dietitian or clinician. The g/h ranges come from position statements and guidance; individual tolerance, energy needs and medical conditions differ. People with GI disease, food allergy, glucose metabolism issues or regular medication should consult a clinician first. The levels, dwell times and weekly arrangement are editorial examples; no public source fixes a uniform "reach level X in Y weeks" standard for recreational runners. For in-race field judgment, see [Race-day fueling](/en/knowledge/race-day-fueling/).

## What would change this protocol

- **Event duration and type:** 10K/half marathon, marathon, ultra and self-supported trail set different carbohydrate targets and forms; trail also depends on aid-station gaps and carried weight.
- **Environment:** heat and humidity lower tolerance and speed sweating; dose and fluid need re-trial; see [Heat and humidity](/en/knowledge/heat-and-humidity/).
- **Digestive history:** IBS, reflux and food intolerance restrict viable forms and may call for low-fibre, low-fat pre-race meals.
- **Evidence update:** carbohydrate ratios, multi-sugar combinations and gut adaptation mechanisms remain active research; this page adjusts if later high-quality reviews differ.
- **Training phase:** taper and recovery weeks do not hold the top dose; tolerance may dip slightly after taper and is reconfirmed in pre-race long runs.
