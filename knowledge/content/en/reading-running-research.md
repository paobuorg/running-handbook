## What reading running research means, and who it is for

Reading running research means tracing a claim back to its question, population, design and measurement, then asking whether it supports your decision. This page is for runners and coaches with stable training. It applies on the premise that evidence differs in strength and no single paper is a command. One condition comes first: identify the question and population before the conclusion.

## First, ask who was studied

Find the population: elite athletes, recreational runners, students, or people with injury or chronic disease? How large was the sample, and how close were sex, age, training level and surface to yours? A study of 30 male rear-foot strikers cannot be generalised to all runners.

## Then look at design and comparison

A randomised trial is better for causal questions, though training studies are hard to blind. Observational studies show association, not causation; cross-sectional studies say nothing about sequence; reviews remain limited by the studies they pool.

| Evidence type | Can answer | Cannot answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Randomised trial | Whether an intervention changes an outcome | Real-race effects |
| Observational study | Whether variables are associated | Which caused which |
| Cross-sectional study | Distribution at one time point | Sequence or causation |
| Systematic review | Overall direction across studies | Flaws in included studies |

Was there a placebo or usual-training group, and were groups otherwise comparable? If everyone did the same training plus one movement, the result cannot be credited to it.

## How measurement and outcomes were defined

Pace, heart rate, effort, pain, injury and performance all need definitions. Is "injury" self-reported or diagnosed? Is "performance" a time trial or a race? Different measurements cannot be compared. VO2max, lactate threshold and running economy are commonly studied determinants, but measurement depends on protocol, so values from different methods are not interchangeable.

## Effect size and uncertainty

Look at effect size and uncertainty, not only "significant". A significant result may be a few seconds in practice, and a wide interval means an unstable estimate. Rewrite the conclusion as a testable sentence; if it already sounds unrelated to your training, the finding is background.

## From one study to the body of evidence

A systematic review of 36 studies covering 23,047 runners found 26.2% reported a running-related injury; it also found conflicting evidence on weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity and recent change, and warned against one "optimal" progression for everyone.

A meta-analysis of tapering included 14 studies and found significant improvements in time-trial and time-to-exhaustion performance. A taper cutting volume by 41-60%, keeping intensity and frequency and lasting up to 21 days was associated with better time-trial performance, though the authors note that the optimal taper duration varies between individuals. The first review means "no universal formula"; the second means "the direction has support".

## How to read one study in three steps

1. Read the abstract and population, then write one sentence on who was studied, what was asked and what it was compared with.
2. Check design and measurement: list the outcome, comparison and follow-up, and mark where they differ from your training.
3. Read the discussion and limitations, note the uncertainty admitted, and decide whether the evidence is to trial, background only, or not adopted.

## A worked example: applying a taper range to weekly volume (hypothetical)

Assume a runner runs 60 km per week and uses the taper range above. At the middle value, 50%, the tapered week becomes 30 km. Assume 3 weeks (21 days, inside the study window): total volume falls from 180 km to 90 km. The 60 km, 50% and 3 weeks are hypothetical; only the 41-60% range and 21 days come from research.

## Common misconceptions

- "Statistical significance means the method works": it means the result is unlikely to be chance, not that the effect matters.
- "A meta-analysis is always more reliable": its quality depends on included studies.
- "Elite subjects mean it applies to me": training level, age and recovery differ.
- "One study overturns years of practice": a single paper usually justifies a small adjustment.

## How often to revisit and update your view

Revisit the evidence you rely on every 12 weeks (about 3 months) and keep only 1 to 2 conclusions relevant to current training. When new evidence conflicts with practice, first ask whether its population and setting are closer to yours. Change 1 variable, observe for 4 weeks, then reassess.

## When to stop and when to seek help

If reading research keeps overturning your plan, stop and return to stable training. For persistent pain, night-time worsening, joint swelling or a change in walking, do not let a paper talk you into running through it; stop and seek medical or physiotherapy advice.

## Checklist

- You can state the population, question and comparison.
- You have marked at least 1 way the setting differs from yours.
- You have recorded the limitations the authors admit.
- You have decided whether the evidence is "trial", "background" or "not adopted".
- You change 1 variable at a time and have scheduled a review in 4 weeks.

## Scope and limits

This page is not a statistics course, an individual training prescription or a quality appraisal of a specific paper. Research findings do not replace clinical diagnosis, coaching judgement and personal testing.
