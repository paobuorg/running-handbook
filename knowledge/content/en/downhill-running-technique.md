## Bottom line

This page is for intermediate and advanced runners who already run steadily on the flat and want to practise controlled descents on hills and trails. It does not apply when you have acute knee or ankle pain, limited balance, or when a steep descent is your first ever hill session. The technique has three parts: keep the stride compact, land under the body, and look ahead to pick footholds early. The limit on a descent is set first by your quadriceps' ability to brake eccentrically, not by heart rate or metabolic cost. Downhill looks easy but is whole-body eccentric work; muscle damage markers and soreness can peak 24 to 96 hours after the run, so practise technical descents while fresh and keep the dose conservative. This is a technique and loading guide, not individual coaching or medical advice; stop descending and seek care for knee pain, giving way, or inability to bend the knee normally.

## Definition and preconditions

Downhill running means moving with gravity while lower-limb muscles contract eccentrically to absorb impact and control speed. Compared with level running, descents usually show longer flight time, lower cadence and more rear-foot striking. This page assumes a repeatable slope, a surface that does not require hands-on scrambling, and no acute joint pain that day. All three preconditions must hold: the slope can be repeated in training; there are no technical climbing moves; knees, ankles and hips are pain-free with normal walking and stair use. If any fails, choose a gentler slope, shorten the repeats, or stay on flat ground and fast walking.

## The mechanism: why eccentric load costs

A descent is not free momentum. A review describes downhill running as a whole-body eccentric exercise model used to study exercise-induced muscle damage (EIMD); slope, duration and speed are the main factors that determine how much damage occurs, and the runners least accustomed to descents generally show the most damage. The cost does not appear in today's heart rate but afterwards: reduced muscle force, raised blood creatine kinase, quadriceps swelling and delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS). In a treadmill study of ten recreational runners, a 30-minute downhill run at 10 km/h on a -20% (-11.3 degree) grade produced moderate decrements in knee-extensor maximal voluntary isometric force; CK, quadriceps swelling and soreness all returned to baseline within four days in this sample. The late phase of rate of force development was impaired and recovered over 3 to 4 days; the early phase was not affected. The sample was small, all male and treadmill-based, so "four days" is not a universal recovery deadline, but it shows that the cost arrives delayed: feeling fine right after the run does not mean the load was light.

## Stride, cadence, hips and centre of mass

The most common downhill error is suddenly opening the stride so the foot lands far ahead, concentrating braking and impact on the knee and shin. The controlled alternative is to keep steps light, never fully open the stride, land under the body, and keep the torso upright or slightly forward from the ankles rather than folded at the waist. Keep the hip under the centre of mass instead of letting it trail behind. On cadence, adjusting cadence can reduce per-step load and energy expenditure; the reviewed evidence does not establish a universal target cadence, and the benefit comes from shortening the stride and cutting forward reach, not from chasing a steps-per-minute number. On foot strike, forefoot striking reduces peak impact forces compared with rearfoot striking, especially downhill, but strike choice also depends on shoes, surface and individual technique; do not force a new strike pattern on a fatiguing section.

## Reading the terrain: scree, roots, steps and wet rock

Look several steps ahead and choose footholds in advance rather than staring at the foot under you. On scree and loose gravel the feet slide, so land light with small steps and avoid long reaches across loose rock. Read a root before stepping: plant on its base rather than bridging over it. On continuous stone steps, do not run a road pace down; confirm each landing, and move sideways or walk when the edges are slick. On wet rock and moss, grip drops sharply and walking is usually the right call, using hands for a stable point when needed. If the next section is hidden by leaves or water, slow down or stop to confirm rather than guessing. A terrain-by-terrain breakdown of mud, roots, steps and rock is in the companion page on [technical trail skills](/en/knowledge/technical-trail-skills/).

## Decision table: when to run, when to walk

| Condition | Recommended action | Do not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gentle slope, dry firm surface, clear sightline | Controlled running with compact steps, landing under the body | Open the stride fully or chase flat pace |
| Steep but stable, rhythm under control | Shorten stride, raise cadence, keep hip position stable | Lean back and brake with the foot |
| Scree, loose dirt, fallen leaves | Slow down, shrink steps, pick each foothold | Leap across or watch only the foot under you |
| Roots, tangled ground, continuous steps | Slow to fast walk or side-step, confirm each landing | Run down at road cadence |
| Wet rock, moss, slick mud slab | Walk; use a support point if needed | Bet on grip and run down |
| Quads burning, steps heavy, foothold choices slowing | Switch to walking early and keep remaining control | Push to the finish on willpower |

## Worked example: 10 km with 800 m of descent (assumptions stated)

All numbers below are a hypothetical demonstration, not a route recommendation or performance prediction. Assume a 10 km segment with 800 m of total descent, an average grade of about -8%, and a runner whose flat easy pace is 6:00 per km (360 s/km). Using the Minetti 2002 running-cost polynomial behind the site's [grade-adjusted pace tool](/en/tools/grade-adjusted-pace/), the metabolic cost at -8% is about 0.655 of flat cost; the downhill pace matching that same metabolic cost is roughly 360 x 0.655 = 236 s/km, or 3:56 per km. Conversely, if the runner actually holds 5:00 per km (300 s/km) on the descent, the model converts that to a flat-equivalent pace of about 7:38 per km.

Three caveats matter. First, the model comes from treadmill running and captures metabolic cost only; it excludes braking, eccentric damage, technical terrain and fatigue. Second, the real constraint on a descent is quad control and safe footfall, not metabolism; scree, roots and wet ground cannot be run at 3:56 per km. Third, the model assumes a constant grade while real courses vary continuously, so the equivalent pace only illustrates that descents look cheap on paper; it is not a race pace. On race day, downhill pace is set by staying controlled and preserving form for the sections that follow.

## Training dose and the DOMS boundary

Descent adaptation builds through prior exposure, which the review identifies as the preventive strategy with the strongest supporting evidence. A sample plan (hypothetical): at most one hill session per week, reviewed every 4 to 6 weeks; start downhill repeats on a gentle slope with 4 x 30 seconds, extending to 60 seconds only after adaptation, always when fresh and on dry ground. The dose ceiling is "the last repeat still holds form"; repeats that fall apart under fatigue are no longer technique practice. For the soreness boundary, the lab study above had markers resolve within days, but individual differences, slope, duration, speed and prior adaptation all change the response. If knee pain or quad soreness worsens markedly more than 24 hours after a session, or swelling or giving way appears, cut the next session or return to the flat; symptoms that do not ease within 48 to 72 hours warrant medical assessment. The review notes that evidence for compression garments and specific footwear limiting downhill damage remains unconfirmed, so gear is not a substitute for training dose.

## Common misconceptions

- **Riding the descent on inertia.** More momentum means a heavier eccentric braking load and faster loss of control as fatigue sets in; a descent is a controlled release, not free fall.
- **Holding flat pace downhill.** Gradient changes the cost of work; lower metabolic cost does not mean lower mechanical load, and flat pace on a descent burns the quads early.
- **Chasing a fixed cadence number.** Cadence adjustment helps by shortening the stride and reducing forward reach; reviewed studies were small and varied, with no universal target.
- **Practising steep descents late in a long run.** Form breaks first when tired and eccentric risk peaks; technical descents are practised fresh.
- **Expecting gear to offset downhill load.** Shoe and compression evidence for limiting downhill muscle damage is limited; systematic descent adaptation, strength and balance are the main line.

## Failure modes and stop rules

1. **Forward-reaching stride, foot landing ahead.** Early signs: heavier footfall, tight calves, increased force felt through the front of the knee. Shorten the stride and bring the landing under the body immediately; walk if needed.
2. **Slower foothold choice under fatigue, dragging steps.** Early signs: unable to read the next landing, reduced arm support, persistent front-thigh burn. Leave the section rather than keep running.
3. **Joint or tendon warning.** Persistent front or lateral knee pain, a knee that gives way, or inability to bend normally: stop descending immediately. After a fall, persistent pain, marked swelling, inability to bear weight or a head impact is an acute injury; do not test it by running on it.
4. **Excessive delayed soreness.** Unusually severe soreness the next day, difficulty on stairs: no further descent or hard session for the next two days. Seek care if pain persists, worsens at night, or comes with swelling or numbness.

## Scope and limits

This page gives general downhill technique and loading principles, not individual coaching, navigation training or medical advice. Gradient, surface, altitude, weather, fatigue and prior injury all change the response; lab recovery timelines come from small treadmill samples and are not personal recovery promises. Technical sections and race routes follow the latest information from the organizer and land manager. Anyone with cardiovascular, joint or balance conditions should consult a professional before hill sessions.

## What changes this advice

- **Slope and terrain.** A smooth dirt descent and a steep scree or wet-rock descent make different demands on footfall and eccentric load, so the technique trade-off changes.
- **Training background and prior adaptation.** Regular hill runners show smaller damage responses; novices or runners returning after a break start with a lower dose and a longer recovery window.
- **Gear and surface conditions.** Lug depth, shoe drop, surface wetness and vegetation cover change grip and therefore whether running is appropriate.
- **Evidence updates.** Downhill biomechanics, damage adaptation and gear interventions keep evolving; this page is updated when higher-quality evidence changes the guidance.
