## Definition and preconditions

Treadmill running means running on an indoor belt at a set speed and incline. It applies to runners limited by weather or time, beginners, and experienced runners wanting controllable intensity. It holds when the machine works, the safety clip is available, the room is ventilated, and the runner can stop the belt in an emergency. The value is fewer variables, not an easier session.

## One percent incline is a convention, not a rule

Outdoors a runner pushes through air resistance; indoors the belt gives a little back, so the same speed is not the same effort. Setting 1% incline is a common compensation convention for belt drift and missing wind resistance. It is a training-circle convention, not a validated rule for everyone. Beginners can start at 0–1%, adapt, then raise it. Incline does not copy a hill one-to-one; prepare for a hilly race on its actual gradients.

## Using the talk test to set intensity

The CDC description gives a simple rule: moderate means you can talk but not sing; vigorous means you cannot say more than a few words without pausing. The talk test is a rule of thumb, not a precise measure, but indoors it works well without wind or changing scenery. Heart rate is a second clue, though room temperature, sleep, caffeine and dehydration shift it. Record the full-sentence speed as an easy baseline and the short-phrase speed as a tempo reference.

## Handrails, safety clip and indoor heat

Using the handrail to carry body weight transfers load to the arms, changes gait and hides training volume. Let the arms swing and touch the rail briefly for balance. Attach the safety clip where a fall pulls it free and stops the belt, start slow, slow down gradually and step off only after the belt stops. Indoors, lack of headwind slows sweat cooling, so ventilate, use a fan and keep water close.

## How to do it: three steps

1. Warm up with a few minutes of walking or jogging, set a speed that allows full sentences, and start incline at 0–1%, changing one variable at a time.
2. Adjust with buttons rather than jumps; recheck the talk test and stop for dizziness or chest tightness.
3. Slow down gradually, stop the belt, remove the clip, and log speed, incline, duration and perceived effort.

## Session types

Easy running builds aerobic time; tempo accumulates short-phrase effort; incline holds speed and raises gradient; recovery sits below easy pace.

| Session | Intensity cue | Structure | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Easy run | Full sentences possible | Continuous or short breaks | Aerobic base |
| Tempo run | Only short phrases | Accumulated segments | Threshold stimulus |
| Incline run | Speed steady, gradient rising | Short repeats | Hill adaptation |
| Recovery run | Clearly below easy | Short duration | Circulation between hard days |

## Worked example: converting 6:00 per km to treadmill speed

The numbers are hypothetical. Assume an outdoor easy pace of 6:00 per km. As speed, 60 divided by 6 gives 10.0 km/h, so 30 minutes at 10.0 km/h covers about 5.0 km. Assume 1% incline; effort at the same speed usually rises slightly. Do not copy the outdoor pace: confirm full sentences with the talk test, then decide whether to drop to 9.5 km/h. This is a conversion, not a climbing equivalence.

## Setup, safety and the indoor environment

Set up before you press start: clip the safety key to your clothing, stand on the side rails while the belt reaches speed, and check that the belt is centred and dry. A treadmill session is fixed in place, so a stumble becomes a fall onto a moving surface; keep the clip on even for easy running. Learn where the emergency stop is and reach it without looking down.

Indoor air is often warmer and stiller than outdoors, especially in a gym without a fan. A small fan and a bottle within reach change how the same pace feels, and a towel prevents sweat from reaching the belt and your hands. If a session feels much harder than the outdoor equivalent, check room temperature, airflow and whether you are gripping the rails, since holding on changes posture and effort.

## Common misconceptions

- The treadmill must be easier. Less air resistance saves effort, but heat and monotony add load.
- Hold the handrails throughout. It transfers weight and hides gait.

## When to stop and when to seek care

Stop the belt and seek help for chest pain, marked dizziness, difficulty breathing, confusion or loss of gait control. NHS advice is direct: do not run while in pain; seek medical or physiotherapy advice when pain is severe, persistent or swollen; unsupportive or worn-out shoes can contribute to heel pain.

## Frequency and progression

NHS Couch to 5K is three runs a week with rest days between, over nine weeks or longer; it starts with one-minute runs and 1.5-minute walks and builds to 30 minutes continuous; go at your own pace and speak to a GP if worried. Adult guidelines call for 150–300 minutes of moderate activity weekly, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity, or an equivalent combination, plus muscle-strengthening on at least two days. Stabilise frequency, extend duration, then intensity; review every four hypothetical weeks.

## Before-you-start checklist

- Safety clip attached where a fall pulls it free.
- Water, towel, fan or ventilation.
- Today's speed, incline and plan set.
- Laces tied, shoes dry, nothing loose near the belt.

## Scope and limits

This page gives no individual prescriptions, rehabilitation plans or repair advice, and does not judge treadmill work as race preparation. Conversions are estimates with wide variation. People with cardiovascular, respiratory or musculoskeletal conditions should consult a clinician.
