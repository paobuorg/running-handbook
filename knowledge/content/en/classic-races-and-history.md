## Definition and who this applies to

A classic race is a road marathon or trail race with a long history, stable culture and substantial public record, such as Boston, Berlin, London, New York, Tokyo and Chicago, or UTMB, Western States and Hardrock. “Classic” marks history and cultural position, not certified difficulty or guaranteed performance.

This applies to runners, coaches and editors who read race material, choose events or review results. The condition: any comparison of editions, or quotation of a rule, must name the race, edition, category and course version; otherwise the conclusion is background only.

## Event definition: the marathon distance

World Athletics defines the marathon as a road event over 42.195 kilometres, also expressed as 26 miles and 385 yards. The definition belongs to the event, not a course; start, finish, turnarounds and measurement are edition-specific.

## Records, times and ratification

World Athletics publishes world records by event and category and marks records pending ratification. Road record tables distinguish mixed-gender from women-only races. A listing is governing-body recognition, not simply the fastest time reported anywhere.

## Conditions and comparability

Weather, wind, temperature and field density differ between editions. Records depend on event rules and course measurement; recreational times also reflect the day’s conditions. Label race, edition, category and course version, and add distance, cumulative climb, surface and altitude across races.

## What history adds

History explains tradition, difficulty and cultural position: why a climb becomes a landmark, why an event has a particular entry culture, why a course is called fast or hard. It also shows that today’s route evolved over time. History does not replace the current notice; an old map is not navigation.

## How to build version awareness in three steps

1. **Lock the version.** Record race, edition, category, course version and rule-document date.
2. **Separate three layers.** Keep the event definition (42.195 kilometres), current rules (entry, conduct, cut-offs, kit) and historical background apart.
3. **Label comparison conditions.** Put weather, surface, cumulative climb, altitude and ratification status beside each time.

## Worked example: context around a finish time

Assume a runner finishes 42.195 kilometres in 4 hours 30 minutes at an even pace. Average pace is 270 minutes ÷ 42.195, about 6 minutes 24 seconds per kilometre. Assume one edition added a 200-metre turnaround; at that pace it takes about 77 seconds. Side-by-side gun times would misread those 77 seconds as fitness change. All figures are hypothetical.

## Common pitfalls

- **Treating “classic” as “right for me”**: reputation says nothing about difficulty, travel, cut-offs or recovery.
- **Sorting times across editions or categories**: conditions, not ability, may explain the gap.
- **Using an old map for navigation**: routes change for permits, weather or safety.
- **Lending a bib**: bibs are non-transferable, and unregistered runners should not join the course.

## Reading frequency, race cycle and progression

Race information is not read once. An illustrative rhythm: with a goal race 12 weeks away, check eligibility and rules at entry, course and cut-offs 4 weeks out, transport and kit one week out, then record conditions a week after. This is an editorial example, not an organiser requirement. Progression means judging which version a fact belongs to.

## When to stop and seek help

In a trail race, stop with hypothermia, dehydration, confusion or inability to walk with a load; find the nearest checkpoint or rescue and tell your partner and the organiser. Do not continue with chest pain, fainting, unusual palpitations or persistent vomiting. If the route conflicts with official documents or markings are missing, stop and follow the organiser.

## Pre-race checklist

- [ ] Race, edition, category, course version and rule date are recorded.
- [ ] Eligibility, cut-offs, aid and mandatory kit match the latest notice.
- [ ] Bibs are not transferred, and no unregistered runner joins.
- [ ] Headphones are used only while maintaining awareness.
- [ ] The current course file is loaded, with an offline backup.

## Scope and limits

This page does not decide entry, eligibility, route access or ratification, and does not judge whether a race suits you. Rules and routes change; the organiser, World Athletics and the local land manager govern.
