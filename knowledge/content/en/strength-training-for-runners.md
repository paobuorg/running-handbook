## Conclusion first

The first thing to remember about adding strength work to a running week is its role: **it supports running ability, it is not a second main training block.** In current evidence, regular strength and stability work is among the strongest protective measures against sports injury; a middle- and long-distance strength review found that adding two to three varied strength sessions per week improved running economy by about 2%–8% in most studies, without a negative body-composition effect. For recreational runners, the starting point is not heavy weight: master movement patterns, build glute, hip and single-leg capacity first, and progress load later.

Three actions first. First, schedule two to three strength sessions per week covering hips, glutes, knees, ankles, core and single-leg stability — not calves alone. Second, place strength on easy, rest or cross-training days, never on the same day as a quality session or long run. Third, progress load by movement quality and the 24-hour response; joint sharp pain, swelling or instability means stepping back and seeking professional assessment.

This page is general training education, not a diagnosis or a prescribed weight. Anyone with prior fractures, spinal problems or chronic disease should consult a doctor or physiotherapist before starting.

## Definition and prerequisites

The strength work on this page means resistance and stability exercise that complements running: bodyweight, bands, dumbbells or machines. Its aims are lower-limb three-joint strength, hip abduction and core control, calf-raise capacity and single-leg stability, supporting tissue tolerance and running economy. It is not bodybuilding-style heavy hypertrophy training, and it does not replace running itself.

It suits runners who already train regularly and want to build a strength base or reduce recurring niggles; intermediate and advanced runners keeping strength through a race block; and coaches arranging auxiliary training. It does not apply during acute injury, post-surgical rehab, or with an established joint, tendon or spinal condition — those situations require exercises set by a clinician.

## Key mechanisms: what strength gives runners

Running alternates single-leg support, and every stride produces force and stability across the hip, knee and ankle in stance. Strength supplements running in three directions:

1. **Tissue tolerance:** tendons, ligaments, muscle and bone adapt progressively to controlled resistance, reducing repetitive-strain injury. The Lauersen 2014 meta-analysis covered 25 RCTs with 26,610 participants and 3,464 injuries: strength training reduced sports injuries to RR 0.315 (95% CI 0.207–0.480), about one third of the control rate; proprioception training RR 0.550 (95% CI 0.347–0.869). Note only four studies fed the strength estimate, and the authors say evidence across broader injury types still needs to grow.
2. **Running economy:** in the Blagrove 2018 systematic review, 20 studies measured economy; most showed a 2%–8% improvement over control, though not every study agreed. The review notes methodological differences and confound control may explain the spread.
3. **Movement control:** single-leg stability, hip abduction and core control improve alignment and force production in stance, reducing compensatory movement.

Strength is not the only lever on pace: aerobic base, volume, pacing rhythm and recovery also determine performance. See [Training volume and progression](/en/knowledge/training-volume-and-progression/) and [Periodization](/en/knowledge/periodization/).

## Exercise menu: three-joint lower body, hip abduction, core, calf raises and single-leg

The table groups exercises by joint and function, listing common exercise types; the exact choice and load depend on movement quality and injury history. Fixed rep counts are not prescribed here; ranges follow in the next section.

| Group | Function | Exercise types (examples) | Common cues |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hip / posterior chain | Glute and hamstring force, hip-hinge pattern | Glute bridge, single-leg bridge, hip hinge, controlled Romanian deadlift | Fold at the hip first; keep a neutral back, no lumbar collapse |
| Knee / single-leg support | Quadriceps and single-leg control | Split squat, lunge, step-up | Track the knee over the toes; stride and speed set by control |
| Ankle / calf | Achilles and calf tolerance, raise capacity | Double-leg calf raise, single-leg calf raise, step-off | Slow and controlled; no bouncing; cut back if soreness persists |
| Hip abduction / glute medius | Pelvic stability, control of hip-adduction compensation | Side-lying raise, clamshell, banded lateral walk | No forward trunk lean, no pelvic tilt |
| Core | Intra-abdominal pressure and anti-rotation in stance | Plank, dead bug, anti-rotation hold | Do not hold your breath; no lower-back sag |
| Single-leg balance | Landing stability and proprioception | Single-leg stand, controlled single-leg squat, single-leg balance deadlift | Start supported; progress difficulty gradually |

The Royal Osteoporosis Society organizes strength work by movement category as well: each session includes one hinge, one push, one pull and one squat, plus a brace and either a lunge or step once or twice weekly. Runners can simplify push and pull to general upper-body and back work; the emphasis stays on lower body and stability.

## Sets, reps and frequency ranges

The ranges below come from existing guidelines and reviews, not a uniform prescription. Start below the lower end and progress once movement is stable.

| Item | Range | Source basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly frequency | 2–3 sessions, with at least one rest day between | Blagrove 2018: two to three strength sessions per week; ROS: two to three days, at least a day apart |
| Session length | 20–30 minutes | ROS: 20–30 minutes working legs, arms, back and abdominal muscles |
| Sets and reps | Build toward 3 sets of 8–12 reps per exercise | ROS: build up to 3 sets, 8–12 repetitions |
| Load choice | Good technique for 8 reps; if 12 feels easy, add load | ROS: lighter load if you cannot manage 8 with technique; heavier if 12 reps feel easy |
| General recommendation | At least two muscle-strengthening days per week for adults | CDC and ODPHP Physical Activity Guidelines: ≥2 strengthening days weekly |

ROS also advises: people with a history of spinal fractures, many broken bones, or recovering from a broken bone should speak to a doctor or physiotherapist before starting.

Progression order: phase one learns the pattern with bodyweight; phase two builds to three sets; phase three adds load or single-leg difficulty without changing the pattern. Do not add load, sets and a new exercise in the same week.

## Decision table: placing strength so it does not interfere with key sessions

| Week arrangement | Where to put strength | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Easy run Tuesday, quality Wednesday, long run Saturday | Thursday rest/cross day, or after Tuesday's easy run | The day before a quality session, or on long-run day |
| Three easy runs, no quality session | Rest day between runs, or after an easy run | The day after a strength session, a long run |
| Race-specific phase | Keep one to two low-to-moderate sessions, reduce sets | Turning strength into a new high-intensity main session |
| Pre-race taper | Cut strength volume, keep a small maintenance dose | New movements or heavy load in taper (see [Strength and taper](/en/knowledge/strength-and-taper/)) |
| Post-injury recovery | Rehabilitation exercises prescribed by the physiotherapist | Self-returning to pre-injury heavy lifts |

Whether strength interferes with running shows up in the next day's easy run: pace, perceived effort and muscle soreness should return to baseline. If easy-run quality drops after two consecutive strength sessions, adjust the load or placement.

## Worked example: a beginner's two sessions over two weeks (assumed)

Example runner: three runs per week, about 25 km total, no current pain, wanting to build a strength base. Exercises and sets below are an assumed example, not a prescription.

**Session A (Thursday rest day, about 25 min)**

- Glute bridge: 2 × 10
- Split squat: 2 × 8 per leg
- Calf raise: 2 × 12
- Side-lying hip abduction: 2 × 12 per side
- Plank: 2 × 20–30 s

**Session B (after Tuesday's easy run, about 20 min)**

- Step-up: 2 × 8 per leg
- Single-leg stand: 2 × 20 s per leg
- Hip hinge: 2 × 10
- Dead bug: 2 × 10 per side
- Calf raise: 2 × 12

After two stable weeks with no marked next-day soreness, build each exercise to three sets; progress load only after week four. If an exercise causes joint pain or breaks down, return to the previous difficulty level.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Strength training will bulk my legs and add weight that hurts performance."** The Blagrove 2018 review concludes strength intervention did not negatively affect body composition; its text reports weight unchanged in 18 studies and a rise of about 2% in one. The distinction matters: endurance-oriented moderate-load strength work is not bodybuilding hypertrophy; heavy, low-rep lifting in a calorie surplus can add muscle mass. The ranges on this page do not chase hypertrophy.
- **"Calves alone are enough."** Recurring niggles often trace to weak glutes and single-leg stability; calf raises alone do not address the posterior chain and pelvic control.
- **"Heavier is always more effective."** For runners, movement quality, tissue tolerance and stability outrank absolute load; heavy load under poor technique is an injury source.
- **"Strength can share a hard day with the long run."** Both draw on recovery; stacking them degrades the long run and adds next-day fatigue.
- **"Stop all strength before a race."** Taper can cut volume, but dropping strength entirely loses the maintenance stimulus; see [Strength and taper](/en/knowledge/strength-and-taper/).
- **"Watch or weight numbers judge whether strength works."** Improvements show in movement quality, single-leg stability, easy-run state and long-term injury pattern, not a single weighing.

## Common failure modes and early signals

1. **Local-only training:** lots of calf work, no glute or core in the plan. Early signals: pelvic sway on long runs, knee discomfort on descents.
2. **Heavy lifting in a fatigued state:** compensation and added load on the back or knee. Early signals: wobble at the end range, visible posture breakdown on mirror review.
3. **Strength eating into running recovery:** easy runs slow after strength, soreness lingers. Early signals: next-day state drops after two consecutive sessions.
4. **Copying a gym hypertrophy plan:** sets and load bias muscle gain, weight and fatigue rise. Fix: return to the 8–12 rep, moderate-load range here.
5. **Treating pain as normal burn:** muscle burn differs from joint sharp pain, local heat or swelling; the latter are not adaptation signals.

Stop rules: joint sharp pain, swelling, giving way or locking, or pain that keeps worsening after scaled-back exercise — stop strength work and seek clinical assessment. With a history of spinal fractures, multiple fractures or a fracture in recovery, consult a doctor or physiotherapist first. For weight and energy-balance questions, see [Weight and running](/en/knowledge/weight-and-running/).

## Applicable boundaries

This page is general training education, not a personal strength prescription, diagnosis or medication advice. Review RR and economy figures come from specific study populations and cannot be converted into a personal injury rate or performance gain; some studies are small with methodological differences. Exercise choice should account for injury history, joint range and movement background; pregnancy, post-surgical and chronically ill people are assessed first by a clinician or qualified coach. This site does not replace a sports physician, physiotherapist or strength coach.

## What would change this advice

- **Phase:** base phase leans pattern learning and stability; specific phase keeps maintenance strength; taper cuts volume; sets and load differ across stages.
- **Population:** novices, older runners, women and previously injured runners need individualized exercise choice and progression speed.
- **Evidence update:** effects of strength on economy and injury remain active research; the page adjusts if later high-quality reviews reach different ranges.
- **Health status:** osteoporosis, joint replacement, tendon disease or cardiovascular problems require professional assessment first.
- **Sport:** trail and ultra demands more glute-leg and downhill stability; see the [Trail and ultra training plan](/en/knowledge/trail-ultra-training-plan/) for terrain-specific additions.

## An 8-week progression: from isometrics to load

Strength training is not “go to the gym and lift”. Running needs single-leg stability, hip extension, calf and foot stiffness, and trunk anti-rotation. This is an eight-week, twice-weekly template for runners who can already perform basic movements without pain.

| Week | Focus | Movements | Dose | Progression |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1–2 | Isometrics and technique | Wall sit, bridge, single-leg calf raise, dead bug | 2–3 sets, 30–45 s | Pain-free, symmetrical |
| 3–4 | Single-leg strength | Split squat, single-leg deadlift, side-hip, calf raise | 3 sets, 6–8 reps | Control knee and pelvis |
| 5–6 | Load and power | Goblet squat, Romanian deadlift, step-up, box jump | 3 sets, 4–6 reps | Technique holds |
| 7–8 | Max strength/maintenance | Squat, deadlift, weighted calf raise | 3–4 sets, 3–5 reps | Halve in race week |

Rest 60–120 s between sets. Put maximal strength after a quality session or at least six hours away; never the day before a long run.

## Dose: reps, load, when to add

- **Isometric:** 30–45 s × 2–3 sets, pain ≤2/10.
- **Hypertrophy/base:** 6–10 reps × 3 sets, last two challenging but clean.
- **Max strength:** 3–5 reps × 3–4 sets, heavy but not slow.
- **Power/plyometric:** 3–5 reps × 3 sets, quiet landing, knee not collapsing.
- **Progress:** two consecutive sessions stable at the same load, then add 2.5–5%; not every session.

## Three conflicts with running volume

**8–12 weeks before a race:** maintain twice weekly; reduce to one light session ten days out; only isometrics and mobility three days out. Do not test a new max load near a goal race.

**Peak volume week:** strength drops to once weekly, keep hips and calves, remove heavy squats. The goal of peak week is running, not a parallel strength personal best.

**Amber pain:** stop movements that increase pain, keep pain-free isometrics; cut running first as described in the pain article, then rebuild strength. Strength does not replace diagnosis.

## Two worked examples

**Base-phase runner, 45 km/week, no strength history.** Weeks 1–2: isometrics and bridges only, twice weekly, 20 minutes. Weeks 3–4: add split squats and single-leg deadlifts. Weeks 5–6: add goblet squats. Weeks 7–8: add light weighted calf raises. After eight weeks, maintain once or twice weekly without chasing big load increases.

**Three weeks before a marathon, 55 km/week.** Keep one 30-minute session: bridge, single-leg calf raise, side-hip, dead bug; remove heavy squat and deadlift. Ten days out, isometrics and mobility only; no lower-body strength three days out.

## Common errors

- Training upper body and core but not calves and outer hips.
- Putting strength the day before a long run and degrading the long run.
- Taking every set to failure and spoiling the next easy run.
- Using strength to replace running volume; race-specific work is still missing.
- Continuing heavy loads while painful, turning a load problem into a structural one.
