## What this page covers

This page is for runners travelling to altitude for training, trekking or a race. It answers four questions: how to recognise altitude illness, how fast to ascend, what to do in the first 24-48 hours, and when to descend. Conventional bands are low altitude 500-2000 m, moderate 2000-3000 m and high 3000-5500 m. Unacclimatised lowlanders travelling above 2500 m are at risk of acute altitude illness, but individual susceptibility varies widely and symptoms can appear lower.

This is not a diagnostic tool and does not replace a doctor. Diagnosis depends on symptoms, functional status and ascent history, not on a watch oxygen reading or on feeling fine.

## Three forms of altitude illness

| Type | Typical clues | Timing | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acute mountain sickness (AMS) | Headache with loss of appetite, nausea, fatigue or dizziness; reduced function | Hours to 3 days after ascent | Stop ascending; rest, hydrate, monitor; descend if worsening |
| High-altitude cerebral oedema (HACE) | Ataxia (often the earliest sign), altered mentation, unusual behaviour | Worsening AMS | Descend immediately; oxygen if available; do not wait until morning |
| High-altitude pulmonary oedema (HAPE) | Breathlessness at rest, cough, sudden loss of performance; pink frothy sputum is a late sign | Often 1-4 days after ascent | Descend at least 1000 m or until symptoms resolve; oxygen if available |

Boundary: AMS is usually not life-threatening, but it is the warning line for HACE and HAPE. Ataxia, altered mentation or breathlessness at rest is an emergency, not a bad day.

## Ascent rate: two sets of numbers

| Source | Recommendation | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Wilderness Medical Society 2024 | Above 3000 m, do not increase sleeping elevation by more than 500 m per day; take a rest day every 3-4 days | One night at an intermediate altitude lowers risk; population guidance, not a guarantee |
| UIAA Medical Commission | Above about 2700 m, climb no more than 400 m above the previous night's sleeping altitude; a rest day after every two days of gain may help | Climb high, sleep low may help, but do not overexert |

The numbers differ, which shows there is no single acclimatisation dose. Use the more conservative set and adjust to symptoms; no schedule replaces listening to your body and making a descent decision.

## The first 24-48 hours

- Take one or two days of rest and easy activity before regular training at moderate altitude; this is the IOC's usual recommendation for high-level athletes, not an hour-by-hour prescription.
- No quality sessions, long runs or intensity in the first 24 hours. Treat day one as a check of how you respond to altitude, not as a chance to catch up on training.
- Avoid alcohol and sleeping pills: both can depress breathing and interfere with acclimatisation; alcohol also worsens dehydration and mimics AMS.
- Do not force large volumes of fluid. WMS states that forced or over-hydration has not been shown to prevent altitude illness; drink normally and check urine colour.
- Fitness is not protection. The UIAA states that there is no evidence that fitness prevents altitude illness; very fit people may be more susceptible because they push harder, marathon runners included.
- There is no single evidence-based training volume for the first days (unknown). This page gives a sequence, not a kilometre target.

## How early to arrive before a race

| Arrival strategy | Evidence | Practical use |
|---|---|---|
| About 2 weeks before at moderate altitude | IOC consensus: typical recommendation; most athletes acclimatise within 2 weeks | Preferred when logistics allow; rest 1-2 days before regular training |
| 5-7 days before | IOC: partial acclimatisation; Foss 2017 cites studies showing worse performance than 14-19 days | Better than nothing, but do not treat partial acclimatisation as full |
| Only the evening before or a few hours before | Foss 2017: 14 hours of overnight hypoxia versus 2 hours before a 20-km time trial showed no one-sided advantage | If travel forces this, there is no clear gain from delaying arrival to just before the start; no evidence that later is better |
| More than 19 days before | Foss 2017 cites marginal additional gains beyond 19 days | Relevant to long stays, rarely practical for amateur runners |

The claim that "arriving late is better than arriving early" was not supported by the sources we could verify, so it is marked unknown. What is supported: about 2 weeks is the usual target; if you can only arrive the evening before, delaying to two hours before the race does not clearly help.

## When to descend or seek emergency care

- AMS of any severity: stop ascending; consider descent if symptoms worsen or do not settle with rest.
- Suspected HACE: ataxia, altered mentation or abnormal behaviour — descend immediately, oxygen if available, do not leave the person alone.
- Suspected HAPE: breathlessness at rest, cough, sudden loss of performance — descend at least 1000 m or until symptoms resolve; oxygen if available.
- If descent is not feasible, WMS suggests supplemental oxygen or a portable hyperbaric chamber, but do not delay necessary descent while waiting for equipment.
- Chest pain, fainting, loss of consciousness, persistent vomiting or inability to walk: call emergency services; do not drive yourself down.

## Medication: the practice exists, doses do not belong here

The WMS 2024 guideline discusses acetazolamide and dexamethasone for prevention and treatment, including boundaries such as sulfonamide allergy; UIAA notes that people flying directly to high altitude without time to acclimatise may consider prophylactic acetazolamide after medical assessment. Whether to use medication, the dose, timing and contraindications must be decided by a doctor. This page gives no drug doses and does not treat medication as a substitute for a safe ascent rate.

## Common misconceptions

- "Fitness prevents altitude sickness." No. There is no evidence that fitness protects, and very fit people may push harder.
- "Drinking more prevents it." Forced hydration has no supporting evidence and over-drinking can cause hyponatraemia.
- "A few hits of oxygen fix it." Short-term commercial oxygen does not replace acclimatisation or descent; WMS does not recommend relying on portable oxygen canisters.
- "Once acclimatised, HAPE cannot happen." Acclimatisation lowers risk but does not guarantee protection; fast ascent, overexertion and respiratory infection change the picture.
- "Pre-acclimatisation gadgets replace arriving early." WMS does not endorse a specific pre-acclimatisation protocol; hypoxic tents need sufficiently long and regular exposure to be plausible.

## A sample training week (not a prescription)

| Timing | Session | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Rest or 20-30 minutes very easy | Observe symptoms; avoid extra load |
| Day 2 | Easy run, no quality work | Check sleep, appetite and headache |
| Days 3-5 | Return gradually to normal training; quality only when symptoms are stable | Use feel and recovery, not the original plan |
| 2-3 days before race | Taper with short sharp touches | Keep sharp, do not add volume |
| Race day | Start around 95% of sea-level goal pace (practical suggestion, not a research finding) | Conservative start, adjust by feel |

The last row is practical advice, not a research conclusion. A safer approach is to run by perceived effort and breathing rather than chasing a sea-level pace.

## Scope and limits

This page does not provide an individual training plan, drug doses or a diagnosis. Pregnant runners, children, people with chronic heart or lung disease, recent respiratory infection or a history of severe altitude illness should consult a doctor before travel. If altitude illness worsens, descend and seek care rather than gambling on one more day of acclimatisation.

## Arrival schedules: three options

Altitude racing is not “breathe oxygen on race day”; it is the rhythm of arrival and adaptation. Choose among three options by race altitude and time budget; none suits everyone.

| Option | Arrival | Suitable | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Late | 24–48 h before | Short race, 2,000–2,500 m | Poor sleep, acute mountain sickness |
| Staged | 3–7 days at intermediate altitude, then race site | 2,500–3,500 m, ultra | Complex travel and lodging |
| Early | 10–14 days before | Above 3,500 m, key race | Long leave, disrupted training |

“Late” does not mean “as late as possible”. Racing straight off a flight, with overnight travel and dehydration, magnifies risk; keep at least one day for easy walking, fluid and sleep.

## How much pace drops per 1,000 m

There is no universal formula. Use perceived effort and breathing as primary signals: at 2,000 m easy pace may be 20–40 s/km slower than sea level; at 3,000 m, 40–80 s/km; above 4,000 m, more. Individual variation is large; the table is for budgeting, not rigid prescription.

| Altitude | Easy-run adjustment | Race effort | Monitor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1,500–2,000 m | 10–30 s/km slower | One notch below sea-level feel | Breathing, sleep |
| 2,000–2,500 m | 20–40 s/km slower | Do not chase sea-level pace | Morning HR, urine |
| 2,500–3,500 m | 40–80 s/km slower | Finish and safety first | Headache, nausea, gait |
| Above 3,500 m | Time and feel, not pace | Stable finish only | Consciousness, cough, breathing |

## Two examples: 2,300 m half and 3,000 m trail

**2,300 m half, sea-level 1:45.** Arrive 48 hours early; day one is 20 minutes walking, fluid and early sleep. Target 1:55–2:00, do not chase pace in the first 5 km, sip water every 15 minutes. Persistent headache, nausea or weaving means stop; do not mask it with painkillers.

**3,000 m 50K, sea-level 7-hour finish.** Arrive 10 days early, stay four days at about 2,000 m, then go to 3,000 m; cut training 30–40% and only walk-run easy. Target 8–9 hours, carry insulation and a backup light; assess breathing, cough and consciousness every two hours.

## Medication and boundaries

Acetazolamide and other drugs can prevent or treat altitude illness, but that is a medical decision; dose and contraindications must come from a clinician. This page gives no dose. Masking headache with painkillers can delay recognition of HAPE/HACE. Resting breathlessness, cough, pink frothy sputum, altered consciousness or inability to walk means descend and seek help immediately.
