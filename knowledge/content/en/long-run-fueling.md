## Conclusion first

The core message of long-run fueling is: **beyond about 1 h of hard running, stored glycogen alone is not enough; you need to train a stable carbohydrate–fluid–electrolyte rhythm in training, and every choice on race day comes from a list validated in training.** The joint ACSM/AND/DC 2016 position statement frames in-exercise carbohydrate at 30–60 g/h for 1–2.5 h of exercise, rising to up to 90 g/h beyond 2.5 h of ultra-endurance effort. The ISSN carbohydrate-timing stand recommends about 30–60 g/h for high-intensity exercise over 60 min, taken in a 6%–8% carbohydrate-electrolyte solution every 10–15 min, adjusted to personal tolerance and event duration.

Three actions come first. First, set the carbohydrate range by duration, then use long runs to find the dose and form you tolerate. Second, every gel, drink and salt tablet is tried in training; race day is not the first exposure. Third, persistent nausea, cramp, reflux, diarrhea or dizziness means slow or stop intake, and drop out or seek care if needed.

This page is exercise nutrition and coaching education, not diagnosis or a personal nutrition prescription. People with diabetes, gastrointestinal disease, food allergies or those taking medication should have a fueling plan assessed by a clinician or sports dietitian first.

## Definition and premises

- **Carbohydrate (CHO):** the fastest available running fuel; internal glycogen stores are limited and external intake is needed during long, hard efforts.
- **g/h:** grams of carbohydrate per hour, the core in-exercise dosing metric; total intake = g/h × duration.
- **Glycogen stores:** carbohydrate stored in liver and muscle; pre-event reserve sets the opening state of a long effort.
- **Gut tolerance:** the ability to digest and absorb carbohydrate and fluid during running without upper-abdominal discomfort, reflux, cramp or diarrhea; it can be improved with repeated training.
- **Energy density:** calories per unit of fuel; ultras often require balancing calories, carbohydrate and fluid within limited gastric emptying.

This page covers training and race fueling for long runs, road marathons, trail running and ultras. Easy runs under 60 min usually need no in-exercise fuel. Daily eating is in [Daily food and recovery](/en/knowledge/daily-fueling/); fluid and sweating are in [Hydration](/en/knowledge/hydration/); sodium and cramps are in [Electrolytes and sodium](/en/knowledge/electrolytes-and-sodium/).

## Why long runs need in-exercise carbohydrate

The body's mobilizable glycogen stores do not cover sustained hard running for long: the ISSN nutrient-timing stand notes glycogen is the predominant fuel for up to a few hours, and carbohydrate availability matters for prolonged endurance performance; when internal carbohydrate runs low, performance drops before subjective fatigue does. The IOC 2010 nutrition guide gives daily carbohydrate ranges by activity: about 5–7 g/kg body weight per day for moderate activity, and about 7–10 g/kg body weight per day beyond 90 min or in heavy sessions.

Two things must be separated: **glycogen reserve the day before** sets the opening stock, and **per-hour carbohydrate during the run** sets whether you hold up late. One cannot replace the other. ACSM 2016 notes that events beyond 90 min may benefit from higher glycogen stores achieved by increasing carbohydrate intake and reducing training load; 36–48 h before the event at 10–12 g/kg body weight per day is the reference carbohydrate-loading figure.

## Duration–carbohydrate tier table

| Exercise duration | In-exercise carbohydrate range | Typical forms | Source and boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Under 60 min | Usually no in-exercise carbohydrate; plain water | Plain water | ACSM/ISSN: short low-intensity effort relies on internal stores |
| 1–2.5 h | 30–60 g/h, divided doses | 6%–8% carb drink, gels, soft food | ACSM 2016: 30–60 g/h; ISSN: every 10–15 min; adjust to tolerance |
| Beyond 2.5–3 h ultra | Up to 90 g/h; ISSN ultra range starts 30–50 g/h, raised to taste and tolerance | Gels plus real food and drinks; note multiple transportable carbs | ACSM 2016: up to 90 g/h >2.5 h; ISSN ultra stand: 150–400 kcal/h, carb 30–50 g/h, protein 5–10 g/h; high dose needs prior gut training |
| 36–48 h before a >90 min event | 10–12 g/kg body weight/day (ACSM loading); IOC daily 7–10 g/kg body weight/day | Rice, pasta, potatoes, bread and familiar carbs | ACSM 2016: loading may benefit >90 min events; wide individual variation, consult a sports dietitian |

Read the table this way: 90 g/h is not a default starting dose; it belongs to trained individuals, after gut training, in very long events. Most runners find their stable point in the 30–60 g/h band. Dose increases must happen step by step in long runs, not maxed out on race day.

## Daily carbohydrate, protein and pre-event reserve

- **Daily carbohydrate:** the IOC 2010 guide suggests about 5–7 g/kg body weight/day for moderate training and about 7–10 g/kg body weight/day beyond 90 min or in hard sessions; the ISSN nutrient-timing stand likewise lists carbohydrate availability as a key variable for prolonged endurance.
- **Protein:** the ISSN protein stand suggests 1.4–2.0 g/kg body weight per day for most active people; about 0.25 g/kg or 20–40 g of high-quality protein per serving, every 3–4 h. Endurance performance rests first on adequate carbohydrate; protein mainly offsets muscle damage and supports recovery and cannot replace in-exercise carbohydrate.
- **Post-exercise recovery:** the ISSN nutrient-timing stand notes that the timing and amount of post-exercise carbohydrate plus protein affect recovery and glycogen resynthesis; the IOC guide suggests 1.0–1.2 g/kg body weight per hour of carbohydrate over the 4–6 h after exercise. Rest-day arrangement is in [Recovery day](/en/knowledge/recovery-day/).
- **Pre-event meal:** familiar, easy-to-digest, low-fat, low-fiber carbohydrate with enough digestion time; do not try a new food before the event.

## Worked example: a 70 kg runner, 2.5 h long run (teaching assumption)

Assumptions: 70 kg runner, 2.5 h long run, moderately hard pace, mild weather, no GI history.

**In-exercise carbohydrate:**

- In the 1–2.5 h band at 30–60 g/h. At the midpoint 45 g/h: total carbohydrate = 45 × 2.5 = 112.5 g.
- With 30 g gels: 112.5 ÷ 30 ≈ 3.75 gels, i.e. about one every 40 min, 3–4 gels over the run; do not rigidly take one every half hour.
- At the 60 g/h upper bound: total 150 g; this dose needs a gut-training base and tends to cause bloating and cramp in untrained runners.
- Ultra comparison: ISSN ultra stand 30–50 g/h gives about 75–125 g over 2.5 h; 150–400 kcal/h, protein 5–10 g/h.

**Fluid and electrolytes:** reference ISSN ultra range 450–750 mL/h, about 1.1–1.9 L over 2.5 h; the exact figure follows sweat rate, measured by weighing in the [sweat-rate tool](/en/tools/sweat-rate/).

**Pre-event reserve:** at 7–10 g/kg body weight/day, a 70 kg runner takes about 490–700 g carbohydrate the day before; with carbohydrate loading, 10–12 g/kg/day over 36–48 h is about 700–840 g/day, arranged around personal gut tolerance rather than copied as a total.

**Post-run:** 20–40 g high-quality protein with a carbohydrate meal; glycogen resynthesis completes over the hours after exercise.

These figures are a teaching example, not a personal prescription; the real dose follows long-run feel, weighing and gut tolerance.

## Decision table: choosing a fueling strategy by state

| Condition (if) | Action (then) | Evidence boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long run under 90 min, easy pace | Plain water; no gels needed | ACSM/ISSN: short efforts rely on internal stores |
| 1.5–2.5 h long run, fading late | Start at 30 g/h, raise stepwise in long runs to your tolerance ceiling; record per-hour carbs and feel | ACSM 2016: 30–60 g/h; ISSN: adjust to tolerance and duration |
| Ultra or >3 h race | Start 30–50 g/h; trained, gut-conditioned runners may move toward higher intake; rotate gels, food and drinks | ACSM: up to 90 g/h >2.5 h; ISSN ultra: 30–50 g/h, 150–400 kcal/h; high dose needs prior adaptation |
| Upper-abdominal fullness, reflux, cramp during a long run | Slow the intake rhythm, lower concentration and single dose; switch to a more dilute solution or low-osmolar gel; record triggers | Baart 2023: high carbohydrate intake and hyperosmolar solutions relate to GI distress; wide individual variation |
| Recurring GI history, or female, younger, high-intensity runner | Start gut training several weeks ahead; raise in-exercise carbohydrate frequency and dose gradually; use only validated forms on race day | Costa 2017: a two-week repetitive gut challenge reduced symptoms; Baart 2023: female sex, younger age and higher intensity associate with abdominal discomfort |
| Persistent vomiting, diarrhea, dizziness or collapse in a race | Stop fueling and exercise, seek medical care; do not push through to finish | Same red-flag boundary as [Hydration](/en/knowledge/hydration/); this site does not diagnose |

## Gut tolerance and gut training

GI complaints are common in runners. In Baart 2023's cross-sectional survey of 1,993 runners, 1,139 (57%) reported abdominal discomfort during running or within 3 h after; upper abdominal discomfort 15%, lower 56%, both 14%; about one third of symptomatic runners said the complaints affected their running. Across studies, GI symptom prevalence in distance runners ranges 30%–90%, highest in ultramarathoners. Female sex, younger age and higher running intensity were positively associated with abdominal discomfort.

The gut can be trained. In Costa 2017's exercise trial, runners completed ten sessions over two weeks of 1 h running while consuming 30 g carbohydrate every 20 min (about 90 g/h); after two weeks, the two carbohydrate-training groups reduced GI symptoms while the placebo group showed no comparable improvement. In the 1 h distance test, the gel group improved 5.2%, the real-food group 4.3%, and the placebo group fell 2.1% (trial × time p = 0.009). The authors conclude a two-week repetitive gut challenge improved GI symptoms and running performance; this is a trainable adaptation.

Practical steps: start with small carbohydrate late in long runs, then move the intake earlier and raise the dose session by session; repeat one form several times before changing it; record gel/food/drink, dose, timing and symptoms. Detailed methods are in [Gut training](/en/knowledge/gut-training-for-races/). Note: the trial was short and limited in sample; the optimal frequency and dose of gut training remain unsettled, and training does not guarantee everyone reaches high intake.

## Combining fluid, electrolytes and carbohydrate

Fueling combines three separate jobs that cannot substitute for one another:

- **Fluid:** replaced in divided doses by sweat rate; see [Hydration](/en/knowledge/hydration/). Sports drinks also provide some carbohydrate and a small amount of sodium.
- **Electrolytes:** consider electrolyte drinks in long, hot/humid sessions or for high sweat sodium; sports drinks carry about 10–38 mmol/L sodium and cannot prevent hyponatremia when fluid intake is excessive. Detail is in [Electrolytes and sodium](/en/knowledge/electrolytes-and-sodium/).
- **Carbohydrate:** the core in-exercise energy source; an overly concentrated (hyperosmolar) drink delays gastric emptying and worsens discomfort, one reason for the 6%–8% solution figure.

Order: confirm sweat rate and drinking rhythm in training first, then layer in carbohydrate dose, then decide on electrolytes from salt streaks, cramp history and conditions. Do not change fluid, carbohydrate and electrolyte all at once.

## Gels and food: common forms and selection conditions

| Form | Strengths | Limits | Best fit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Energy gel | Clear dose, easy to carry, quick acting | Tiring to repeat; concentrated gels may cause reflux | Mid-marathon/trail; check carb per gel on the label |
| Carbohydrate drink | Fuels and hydrates together, sipped in doses | Limited gastric emptying; high concentration worsens discomfort | Long runs and heat; use the 6%–8% concentration reference |
| Real food (rice balls, banana, bars, rice cakes) | Variety, some protein and micronutrients | Slower to carry and digest; fat/fiber may burden the stomach | Long ultra aid stations; must be validated in training |
| Protein supplement | Aids recovery, eases some hunger | Cannot replace in-exercise carbohydrate; excess protein/fat may slow emptying | Mainly post-run recovery, small amounts in-race |

Selection: the longer the race, the more you need variety late; but every form is first used in training. The full race-day list is in [Race-day fueling](/en/knowledge/race-day-fueling/); the planning tool is [Long-run fueling planner](/en/tools/fueling-planner/).

## Failure modes and early signals

- **Chugging concentrated gels or large food amounts in a long run or race:** early signals are upper-abdominal fullness, reflux, nausea, cramp. Rule: divide doses, lower concentration, cut single serving size; hyperosmolar solutions and high carbohydrate intake are recorded GI-distress triggers.
- **Trying new fuel only on race day:** early signals are pre-race nerves plus an unfamiliar food, bloating soon after the start. Rule: gels, drinks, salt tablets and food all come from the training-validated list.
- **No carbohydrate in a long run:** early signals are heavy legs, unexplained pace drop, low mood late. Rule: plan external carbohydrate for hard long runs beyond about 1 h.
- **Replacing in-exercise carbohydrate with protein or fat:** early signals are energy shortfall, delayed recovery. Rule: endurance performance rests on adequate carbohydrate; protein serves recovery.
- **Copying someone else's gels-per-hour:** early signals: they tolerate it, you do not. Rule: record your own g/h range, gut-training progress and duration; do not copy another's dose.
- **Skipping post-run recovery eating:** early signals are next-day tiredness, slow recovery after long sessions. Rule: arrange carbohydrate plus high-quality protein within hours after the run.

## Red flags: stop and seek care

Persistent vomiting, diarrhea with dizziness, collapse, altered consciousness, chest pain, severe breathlessness, or symptoms worsening after fueling: stop exercise and fueling, seek medical care, do not push through to finish. With suspected hyponatremia, do not self-administer dilute salt water; acute GI symptoms that persist after dropping out should be assessed medically. Those with diabetes, GI disease, disordered eating history or pregnancy should have a pre-race fueling plan confirmed by a clinician or sports dietitian.

## Applicable boundaries

This page is general exercise nutrition and coaching education, not a diagnosis, treatment plan or medication advice. g/h, g/kg and kcal/h figures are population reference ranges from stands and studies; they cannot be converted into a precise personal prescription. Cross-sectional surveys, exercise trials and position statements are flagged separately by evidence type. Diabetes, gastrointestinal disease, food allergy or intolerance, disordered eating, pregnancy/lactation or medication use call for clinician-assessed fueling plans. This site does not run blood chemistry tests or replace individualized sports dietitian or medical guidance.

## What would change this advice

- **Duration and distance:** under 60 min usually needs no in-exercise carbohydrate; 1–2.5 h enters the 30–60 g/h band; high doses and food rotation only beyond 2.5 h.
- **Conditions and intensity:** heat and humidity reduce gastric emptying and change sweat and electrolyte loss; higher intensity depletes glycogen earlier, and the dose adjusts accordingly.
- **Individual state:** GI history, body weight, training age, heat acclimation, sleep and energy status all change tolerance; female, younger and high-intensity runners reported abdominal discomfort more often in the survey.
- **Evidence update:** gut-training protocols, multiple-carb transport ratios and ultra protein use remain under study; the page adjusts if later high-quality RCTs or consensus statements differ.
- **Disease and medication:** diabetes, GI disease or drugs change the safe carbohydrate and fueling range; clinical assessment takes priority over the general figures here.
