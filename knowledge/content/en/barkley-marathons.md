## What this page covers

The Barkley Marathons takes place in Frozen Head State Park, Tennessee, and is routinely called the hardest ultramarathon in the world. Most Chinese-language information about it is second-hand, and the numbers contradict each other: finisher counts appear as 14, 20 or 26, and James Earl Ray's escape distance appears as 8 miles, 12 miles or 13 kilometres.

This page answers four questions: where the race came from, why its rules are designed the way they are, why there is no official course to find, and why it cannot be copied.

The boundary comes first: **the organisers do not publish a course, and this page contains no course data.** It lists no kilometre figures, no elevation table and no cut-off table, and gives nothing that could be used for navigation. That is not an omission: the race has no publicly released route. Within the scope of this review, no route file published by the race was found, and any GPX file claiming to be the complete Barkley course cannot be traced back to the organisers.

This is also not an entry guide. The entry process is not public, the race date moves, and the venue is managed by a state park, so any "how to enter" or "how to train" checklist would not hold.

## Definition and premises

This page uses "Barkley Marathons" for the invitation-only race in Frozen Head State Park, Tennessee: five loops of about 20 miles each, a 60-hour limit, an unpublished route, no conventional waymarks and no GPS. It is not an event you can enter through a public lottery, download a GPX for, or train on as a fixed course. The page is for readers who want to understand the history, rules and culture, not a "how to replicate the race" guide. Every figure about distance, climb and finisher counts is constrained by the absence of an official course file and an official results database, so none should be treated as a reproducible training parameter.

## The short version

It began with a failed prison escape in 1977. James Earl Ray moved a very short distance in roughly 54 hours, and organiser Gary Cantrell — later known as Lazarus Lake — thought he could run at least 100 miles (Knox TN Today). The first edition was held in 1986 and named after Cantrell's neighbour Barry Barkley (RUN247, Knox TN Today).

Today it is usually described as five loops, 60 hours, no waymarks, no GPS and no conventional aid stations. Every source consulted describes it as a 100-mile-class race, but nobody can supply a measured figure — the reason is in the next section.

## Origins: one escape and one neighbour's name

On 10 June 1977, Ray and five others escaped from Brushy Mountain State Penitentiary; by 13 June they had been recaptured (Knox TN Today). Knox TN Today says he covered "between 8 to 12 miles" in "54.5 hours", and that Cantrell considered the distance pitiful and thought he could have covered at least 100 miles himself. CGTN gives "approximately 12 miles over 54 arduous hours"; Runraid's French-language race history says "13 km in 54 hours".

The three figures do not agree, and a later section deals with what that means. What is clear is the design motive: the race was not conceived as a longer ultramarathon, but as a mocking reply to a failed escape.

The name and the founders are not in dispute. RUN247, CGTN and Knox TN Today all name Gary Cantrell and Karl Henn as the creators; RUN247 and Knox TN Today both say the race is named after Barry Barkley.

## Why there is no official course

This is the key to understanding the Barkley, and the point most second-hand accounts get wrong.

RUN247's retrospective states it plainly: there is "no website, no route maps", and the route is altered every year. The Bitter Southerner's on-site report says the race is "entirely unmarked", with books hidden across the course by the organiser and his colleague to serve as checkpoints. CGTN says there are no course markings and competitors must commit the route to memory. RUN247 adds that GPS is not allowed.

Put those three together and the result is: there is no stable, downloadable, measurable route file. No map means no quotable distance; no GPS means nobody can reconstruct the true mileage from a track; a route that changes every year means last year's track has no navigational value this year.

So "100 miles" is the organiser's and the media's shorthand, not a survey result. RUN247 notes that some claim the true distance is closer to 130 miles; that figure likewise has no first-hand measurement behind it. This page therefore gives no definitive course length or elevation total.

Two things should be kept apart: **no official course** does not mean **total secrecy**. Runners are given route information to memorise before the start, and books on the ground act as checkpoints; but none of that is a published route file, and none of it is course data a third party can verify.

## Rule design: five loops, 60 hours, and no credit for unfinished business

The sources agree on the basic structure: five loops of about 20 miles each, with a 60-hour limit (RUN247, CGTN). Runners return to the start area after each loop and begin the next. Completing three loops is recorded as a "Fun Run", an acknowledged shorter form of completion (the barkley.ferrett.io results tables for 2025 and 2026 mark the third loop as FUN).

A full finish requires all five loops inside 60 hours. There is no official list that treats four loops as a result. RUN247 quotes Frozen Ed Furtaw, the first finisher, writing in 1996 that the Barkley is "intentionally set up to minimise the number of finishers while staying within the limits of possibility". That sentence explains the core of the design: it does not chase a finish rate, and it offers no gradation for those who stop.

The tracker records that loop cut-offs exist and states that "you can't start a loop after its cutoff", but it does not publish the times, and the organisers publish no cut-off table (barkley.ferrett.io 2025). This page therefore includes no cut-off table.

## Books and page numbers: how a checkpoint is read

The Barkley's checkpoints are not timing mats. They are books.

The Bitter Southerner's report describes the system: books are hidden around the course; a runner reaching a book tears out the page matching their race number and carries it back to the start as proof of completing that loop; if a runner goes missing, searchers can use the last torn page to narrow the search area. The report adds that the organisers typically will not go looking for someone until the full 60 hours are up.

The page numbers are not arbitrary. Asked why books, Laz replied: "What else has page numbers?" (Bitter Southerner). The Bitter Southerner gives the count for the edition it covered — 13 books — and that number should not be applied to other years.

The design solves three problems at once: it proves a runner reached a point, it leaves physical evidence without electronic timing, and it provides a last known location if someone disappears. It also means the "checkpoints" cannot be exported as a standard map.

## The start: a conch, a cigarette and an unpublished window

The Bitter Southerner reports that the race begins at some point between midnight and noon on a Saturday, with nobody knowing exactly when; Laz blows a conch shell, giving runners one hour to reach the start gate; the race starts when he lights a cigarette.

That "midnight to noon" description should not be treated as a fixed rule. barkley.ferrett.io records a 2025 start at 11:37 on 18 March, inside the window; the 2026 start was at 19:00 on 14 February, outside it. Both records exist, and this page sets them side by side rather than deciding which one is the "correct rule".

The date itself moves. RUN247 says the race is usually held in March or early April, and that the 14 February 2026 start was the earliest by a wide margin. Planning travel around past dates is therefore unreliable.

## Entry: 1.60 US dollars, an essay and a number plate

There is no public entry page. RUN247 says applicants have to work out when and where to send an essay, along with the 1.60 US dollar entry fee, and that first-time runners must bring a number plate from their home town. CGTN says applicants must write a "Why I should be allowed to run in the Barkley" essay and that successful applicants receive a letter of condolence.

The commonly quoted field size is "35 runners a year" (CGTN, Knox TN Today). That is not a published start list: the runner pages on barkley.ferrett.io are fan-maintained records, each marked "confirmed" with a link to a social post, not a list issued by the race. This page therefore does not treat 35 as the actual number of starters in any given year.

## Why the finisher numbers do not agree

Three sources, three figures:

- The Bitter Southerner's on-site report says that "out the 1,000 or so runners who've attempted the race, only 14 people have ever finished".
- CGTN, writing in 2024, says that since the race was extended to 100 miles in 1989, "only 20 people" have completed it inside 60 hours.
- Knox TN Today, writing after the 2024 edition, says "only 26 have ever finished the 100-mile course".

None of these is an official results database. The sources also do not spell out the moment and counting rule behind each figure: the 14 comes from an on-site report dated 27 April 2017, which says "ever finished" and does not state whether Fun Runs are included; the 20 comes from CGTN on 24 March 2024, which explicitly says "20 people", meaning distinct individuals; the 26 comes from Knox TN Today on 26 March 2024, which also says "ever finished" and does not say whether repeat finishes by the same person are counted. Runraid separately says "20 different people", supporting the distinct-individual reading. None of the three states whether Fun Runs count, and none publishes a counting rule; no official definition could be found.

| Source | Report date | Number | Original wording | Fun Run explained |

This table only records each source's wording and date; it does not harmonise them. The 14, 20 and 26 cannot be added together or treated as an official count at one point in time.

One plausible explanation is that the denominators differ: 14 is the count at the time that article was written; 20 explicitly counts people (distinct individuals); 26 may count finishes rather than finishers, since five people finished in 2024 alone. But that is an explanation, not an official definition, and this page does not declare which number is correct.

Runraid's history says "40 editions, about 1,000 starters, only 20 different people" have finished, matching CGTN's 20 and explicitly using "personnes différentes" (distinct people). That supports the finishers-versus-finishes explanation, but it is still not official data.

The conclusion: any "total number of Barkley finishers" should carry its source and its counting rule. A number without a rule should not be cited as fact.

## Ray's mileage: a claim with no first-hand source

This is the most widely repeated detail about the Barkley, and the one with the least first-hand support.

- Knox TN Today: 8 to 12 miles, 54.5 hours.
- CGTN: approximately 12 miles, 54 hours.
- Runraid: 13 km, 54 hours.

Thirteen kilometres is about 8.1 miles, near the lower bound given by Knox TN Today; 12 miles is its upper bound. The three figures do not necessarily contradict each other, but there is no single agreed value. More importantly: **all three come from second-hand accounts. The race has never published a primary investigation of the 1977 escape, so the claim has no first-hand source.**

What can be confirmed is the outline: Ray escaped on 10 June 1977 and was recaptured on 13 June (Knox TN Today). As for how far he actually travelled and whether he was out for 54 or 54.5 hours, this page says "roughly 8 to 13 kilometres and about 54 hours", and labels that as a second-hand range rather than a precise figure.

## Why it is hard: terrain, weather and sleep

The official Frozen Head State Park page says the park covers more than 24,000 acres of rugged wilderness in the Cumberland Mountains and is named for the 3,324-foot Frozen Head peak. The Bitter Southerner's description: the race is held in early spring, there are no grand views, only a "wintry and barren" landscape and "blankets of blinding fog"; the slopes are covered in wet leaves and broken branches, and it is "no stretch to say it's a certain kind of prison".

The difficulty is not one factor but several at once: no waymarks, so navigation depends on map and memory; no GPS; no conventional aid stations (CGTN mentions only two locations offering water); repeated climbing and descending across 60 hours; and early-spring weather that can turn from cold rain to fog, thunderstorms or sleet within hours.

CGTN puts the total climb and descent at about 60,000 feet; the Bitter Southerner gives 59,100 feet and translates it as "Everest twice". The two figures are close, but both are media conversions, not official course measurements. This page uses them only as an order of magnitude, not as quotable facts.

## Culture: Taps, nicknames and a documentary

The race comes with a set of rituals. RUN247 says a sombre rendition of "Taps", the US military funeral call, is played each time a runner drops out. The Bitter Southerner lists fixed nicknames for sections of the terrain, including Big Hell, Rat Jaw, Bird Mountain, The Bad Thing, Son of a Bitch Ditch and Leonard's Butt Slide.

The race reached a wider audience after a 2014 documentary (Bitter Southerner). That attention also produced a great deal of second-hand retelling, and many of the numerical disagreements cited on this page are the result of retellings layered on retellings.

Another part of the culture is a refusal to scale. RUN247 notes there is no website and no route map; the Bitter Southerner describes runners who "train for years, for decades, to vie for a spot they usually don't get". That is not marketing language but an organising method: no public entry, no official website, no official results database.

## 2024: 59:58:21 and 99 seconds

2024 is the year the Barkley became widely known to Chinese readers.

CGTN reports that Jasmin Paris finished in 59 hours, 58 minutes and 21 seconds, with 99 seconds to spare before the 60-hour cut-off, and that five runners finished that year. CGTN also says she reached the fourth loop in both 2022 and 2023, the first woman to do so since 2001.

Those two figures — 59:58:21 and 99 seconds — are consistent across several reports and can be used as a reliable record. They still come from media reporting, not an official results database. CGTN also reports that Mark Williams became the first runner to finish inside 60 hours, in 1995, and that Brett Maune set the fastest finish of 52 hours, 3 minutes and 8 seconds in 2012 — again, media records.

## 2025 and 2026: two years without a finisher

The fan-maintained tracker records:

- 2025: the conch was blown at 10:37 on 18 March, the race started at 11:37 and ended at 23:37 on 20 March, with no finishers; John Kelly completed the three-loop Fun Run, with the third loop recorded at 39:50:27 (barkley.ferrett.io 2025).
- 2026: the conch was blown at 18:00 on 14 February, the race started at 19:00 and ended at 07:00 on 17 February, with no finishers; Sébastien Raichon completed the three-loop Fun Run, with the third loop recorded at 38:05:46 (barkley.ferrett.io 2026).

Two things follow. Even at the shorter three-loop distance, very few people finish in most years; at the full five-loop distance, some years produce nobody. And the date really does move: March in 2025, February in 2026.

## Why it cannot be copied

Taken apart, every component looks easy to reproduce: a trail loop, a book, a 60-hour clock, a bugle call. Put together, the race cannot be copied, for five reasons.

**First, it depends on one specific piece of land.** Frozen Head State Park covers more than 24,000 acres in the Cumberland Mountains and is named for the 3,324-foot Frozen Head peak (Tennessee State Parks). The venue is managed by the state park, not by the race; on 8 November 2026 the park page still listed several campgrounds, the visitor centre and a number of trails as closed for renovation, with only the Chimney Top Trail accessible. Move the race elsewhere and the terrain, weather, permits and rescue arrangements all change.

**Second, it depends on an organiser who does not scale.** There is no public entry, no official website and no route map (RUN247). The field is kept in the dozens, not the thousands. A copier who scales up must publish a route, add aid stations, and introduce timing and medical systems — exactly the things the Barkley removes.

**Third, its core product is unmeasurability.** The route changes every year, GPS is banned, and there is no official course file, so results cannot be compared across editions or certified. Any copy that wants to be rankable, certifiable and commercial would lose that core in its first year.

**Fourth, it has no official results database.** The fact that finisher counts appear as 14, 20 and 26 shows that records are maintained by media and fan trackers, not issued by the race. A copier who builds an official database would make the race standardisable, and therefore stop it being the Barkley.

**Fifth, its rituals are rooted in local culture.** The conch, the cigarette start, "Taps", the page numbers, the terrain nicknames — these are not an "event experience" that can be packaged and exported, but locally accumulated knowledge (Bitter Southerner, RUN247). Move them somewhere else and they become a theme park.

What can be copied is the form. What cannot be copied is the absence of an official course.

## Common misconceptions and misreadings

- **"The 100 miles was measured."** It was not. There is no official route file and GPS is banned, so runners cannot measure the true distance; 100 and 130 miles are conventions, not survey results.
- **"There is an official finisher count."** There is no official results database. The figures 14, 20 and 26 come from different sources, at different times, with different counting rules.
- **"Entry is by lottery."** It is not a lottery. Applicants must find out how to apply, pay 1.60 US dollars and send an essay (RUN247, CGTN).
- **"The course is fixed, so you can scout it."** The route changes each year and is not published (RUN247).
- **"Four loops counts as a finish."** A full finish requires five loops; three loops is the Fun Run, a separate acknowledged shorthand.
- **"60 hours is the only cut-off."** The tracker records loop cut-offs as well, though the times are not published; "you can't start a loop after its cutoff" (barkley.ferrett.io 2025).
- **"You can book flights around last year's date."** The date moves: March 2025, February 2026, and 2026 was the earliest start on record (RUN247, barkley.ferrett.io).

## Risk and stop signals

The risk is not "not being fit enough" but navigation failure, hypothermia, sleep deprivation and thunderstorms. With no waymarks and no GPS, a wrong turn can leave you circling wet, cold forest for hours; early-spring weather can turn from cold rain to fog, thunder or sleet within a short time. The Frozen Head State Park page also lists construction closures and limited trail access, so the venue itself changes.

In a similar unmarked long-distance setting, stop and seek help on any of these signals: continuous shivering or clearly degraded coordination (hypothermia), confusion or incoherent answers (hyponatremia or hypoglycemia), chest pain or fainting, exposure on a ridge during a thunderstorm, or being unable to place yourself on the map before dark. Do not treat hypothermia or altered consciousness with "one more mile"; the Barkley has no conventional aid stations or embedded medical team, and rescue depends on the organiser, other runners and park management. This page is a culture and rules explainer, not an entry guide; with no official course file, nobody should use it to replicate the route.

## Three numbers that get conflated

| Commonly quoted number | What it actually means | What it cannot be used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "About 20 miles per loop" | A five-loop format; loop distance varies with year, route and shortcuts, and no fixed official GPX is published | Treating 20 miles × 5 as a precise 100-mile course |
| "60-hour cutoff" | The race closure counted from the start window, not a fixed 60:00 clock time on a calendar day | Back-calculating per-loop pace from a road-race cutoff table |
| "$1.60 entry fee" | An application fee; entry also depends on the essay, licence plate, experience and draw | Assuming that paying the fee guarantees a place |

## A worked example: splitting 60 hours across five loops

Assume a 58-hour target over five full loops: 11 hours 36 minutes per loop on average. The 2024 finish of 59:58:21 works out at about 11 hours 59 minutes 40 seconds per loop. Example: if loop one takes 10 hours 30 minutes, the remaining four can average 11 hours 52 minutes; if loop one takes more than 13 hours, every later loop must stay under 11 hours 15 minutes, squeezing sleep and book-finding time. The example is for sleep, fueling and pacing only, not an official split; weather, night navigation and route versions move each loop substantially.
