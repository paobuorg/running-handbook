## What this page covers, and when it applies

Caffeine is a central stimulant available as coffee, tea, energy gels, caffeinated drinks and tablets. This page covers the endurance evidence, common dose and timing, and how to test it safely in training. It assumes you have no cardiovascular disease, arrhythmia, severe anxiety or reflux, and are not pregnant or breastfeeding; those situations need medical advice first. This page is general nutrition and training education, not an individual dosing plan.

## What the evidence roughly says

The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand states that caffeine can acutely enhance performance in many but not all studies, with aerobic endurance showing the most consistent moderate-to-large benefits. Common doses are 3-6 mg per kg body mass, usually about 60 minutes before exercise; optimal timing depends on the source and individual absorption. Higher doses usually bring more side effects: palpitations, anxiety, tremor, stomach upset and insomnia.

For someone who normally avoids caffeine, a sudden moderate-to-high dose on race day usually carries more risk than benefit. Caffeine also does not remove fatigue; it changes perceived effort and arousal. Relying on it while hot, dehydrated or sleep-deprived is not a safe strategy. Findings are group averages and cannot predict an individual response.

## What it changes, and what it does not

Caffeine acts mainly on the central nervous system: it raises arousal and changes perceived effort, so the same pace feels easier. It provides no carbohydrate, adds no fluid, and does not replace pacing discipline. Fatigue does not disappear; some of it is temporarily masked.

Individual variation is the most important takeaway from the evidence. The position stand notes that adverse effects on sleep or anxiety, as well as differences in performance response, may relate to genetic variation, and habitual intake also plays a role. The same dose can be helpful, neutral or clearly uncomfortable for different people, so a protocol that worked for someone else cannot be copied directly.

## How to test it: three steps

1. Choose a quality session or long run and simulate the race timing, dose and source; change only one variable at a time.
2. Record four responses: heart rate, stomach discomfort, anxiety level and the time you fall asleep that night.
3. Only consider race-day use after 2-3 repetitions at the same dose produced no clear side effects.

During testing, record the milligrams per serving on the label, not "a cup" or "a packet". Concentrations vary widely: brewed, espresso and ready-to-drink coffee can differ several-fold in caffeine content.

## Dose and timing quick reference

| Body mass | Low dose 3 mg/kg | High dose 6 mg/kg | Common timing | Suitable context |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 55 kg | 165 mg | 330 mg | 45-60 min before | First test at the low dose |
| 70 kg | 210 mg | 420 mg | 45-60 min before | Reuse a protocol already validated in training |
| 85 kg | 255 mg | 510 mg | 45-60 min before | Higher side-effect risk at the high dose |

The milligram figures are arithmetic conversions, not intake recommendations; always read the per-serving content on the label. Conversion: dose in mg = body mass in kg × mg per kg.

## Worked example: a race-day plan for a 70 kg runner

Suppose a 70 kg runner has tolerated 3 mg/kg in training, giving 70 × 3 = 210 mg. If one gel contains 75 mg caffeine, 210 ÷ 75 = 2.8 gels, close to three gels to reach that dose, bringing extra carbohydrate and stomach load. A more practical plan is a cup of brewed coffee containing about 200 mg 60 minutes before the start (200 ÷ 70 ≈ 2.9 mg/kg), then caffeine-free gels during the race. If the race is in the afternoon and good sleep that night matters, move intake at least 8 hours earlier or skip it. Product contents are hypothetical examples; follow the label.

## Second worked example: why a 55 kg runner cannot count "per gel" (hypothetical)

Assume a 55 kg runner has tolerated the low 3 mg/kg dose: 55 × 3 = 165 mg. If each gel contains 75 mg, 165 ÷ 75 ≈ 2.2 gels, and 0.2 gel cannot be split out. A 200 mg cup of coffee would give 200 ÷ 55 ≈ 3.6 mg/kg, already on the high side of the common range, which someone who rarely uses caffeine should not take directly. The calculation shows why you first work out milligrams from body mass, then choose the product and number of servings, rather than estimating by "a packet" or "a cup".

## Decision table: whether to use caffeine on race day

| Condition | Advice | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Same dose tolerated 2-3 times in training with no clear side effects | Reuse the validated protocol | Response is known; risk stays controlled |
| Normally avoids caffeine or uses it only occasionally | Do not start above 3 mg/kg on race day | Response is untested; acute high doses carry more side effects |
| Afternoon race, normal sleep needed that night | Take it at least 8 hours earlier, or skip it | Caffeine can affect sleep; response varies |
| History of insomnia, anxiety, palpitations or reflux | Consult a clinician before use | Outside the scope of this page |

## Common misconceptions

- Assuming stronger is better. Above 6 mg/kg side effects rise without a matching gain; the position stand links very high doses to a higher incidence of side effects.
- Estimating dose as "a cup" or "a packet". Concentrations vary widely; check milligrams per serving and convert by body mass.
- First trying it on race day. An unvalidated combination of dose, product and timing turns a controlled training session into an experiment.
- Treating caffeine as a cure for fatigue. It changes arousal and perceived effort only; it does not replace fluids, carbohydrate or pacing.

## When to stop

Stop and seek medical help for chest pain, marked palpitations, tremor that interferes with movement, severe anxiety or vomiting. People with hypertension, arrhythmia, anxiety disorders or insomnia should consult a clinician before use. Caffeine does not replace fluids, carbohydrate or pacing; pushing on through heat, dehydration or poor sleep with caffeine is not a safe strategy.

## Frequency and progression

Caffeine does not need to be used daily. Reserve it for quality sessions and races and keep habitual intake low to limit tolerance. Track sleep and resting heart rate over 4 weeks; if insomnia persists or resting heart rate rises by 5 bpm or more, stop and reassess. Record changes in daily coffee and tea intake too; otherwise race-day response cannot be attributed to dose or habitual level.

## Race-day checklist

- Confirm milligrams per serving, not the marketing wording.
- Take it about 60 minutes before the start, not at the line.
- Carry caffeine-free options so the second half can switch.
- If the race is in the local afternoon, assess the effect on that night's sleep.
- In heat, treat caffeine as one part of hydration and cooling, not a standalone boost.

## This page's limits: no diagnosis or individual dosing advice

This page does not diagnose anxiety disorders, arrhythmia, reflux or sleep disorders, assess drug interactions, or prescribe an individual dose. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, arrhythmia, hypertension, anxiety disorders, reflux, insomnia or current medication all warrant prior advice from a clinician or registered dietitian. Whether caffeine suits you, how much and when, should be decided by a professional who knows your history.
