## Definition and scope

Race-day fuelling means the timing and amount of carbohydrate, fluid, electrolytes and optional caffeine from the last meal to the first food after finishing. It applies to races and training days over 60 minutes when products and intervals are rehearsed, gut response is known and conditions match.

## The last pre-race meal

The pre-race meal tops up glycogen without loading the stomach. Use habitual foods, not race-week discoveries, and leave digestion time before the gun; no single number fits. Assume a main meal three hours out and a small snack in the final hour.

## During the race

The ISSN position stand suggests about 30–60 grams of carbohydrate per hour for high-intensity exercise longer than 60 minutes, often as a 6–8% carbohydrate-electrolyte drink in small amounts every 10–15 minutes. Easier events sit near the lower end; heat or intensity can make the upper end hard to tolerate.

## Converting products

Packets differ in grams per serving, so "one gel every 20 minutes" cannot replace the label. Find grams per serving, divide the target by that number, then place the result on the clock. Assume a 500 ml bottle at 7%, containing 35 grams: one bottle per hour supplies about 35 grams. A 22-gram gel every 30 minutes is about 44 grams per hour.

Station spacing has to match the intake rhythm. Assume stations every 5 km at a 5:00 per km pace; the gap is about 5 × 5 = 25 minutes, longer than the 10–15 minute small-snack rhythm, so a self-carried gel or drink has to cover that gap rather than waiting for the station. Spacing, supplies and what you may carry follow the race guide.

## Worked example

Figures are hypothetical: 70 kg, a 3:30 target (210 minutes), 45 grams per hour, 22-gram gels.

- Total: 45 g/h × 3.5 h = about 158 grams.
- Gels: 158 ÷ 22 ≈ 7; first at 30 minutes, then every 30 minutes.
- Check: 7 × 22 = 154 grams, about 44 grams per hour.
- Caffeine, if used: 3–6 mg/kg at 70 kg is about 210–420 mg, about 60 minutes before exercise; a conversion, not a recommendation.

## Caffeine: optional

Caffeine can acutely improve performance in many but not all studies, most consistently in aerobic endurance; 3–6 mg/kg has consistently improved performance, while very high doses carry more side effects. Common timing is about 60 minutes before exercise. Sleep or anxiety can be affected.

## How to: three steps

1. Fix products and intervals in long runs, recording grams, weather, fluid and gut response; write the plan after two or three tolerable sessions.
2. Calculate grams and servings, put times on the bib or watch, and mark aid stations and carried supplies.
3. Rehearse near race intensity two to three weeks out and use only tested combinations.

## If the stomach stops

Bloating, nausea or lost appetite are common. Ease off, switch solids for sips, and delay the next gel. If one vomit brings improvement, a drink-only shortened plan may work; persistent vomiting, dizziness, confusion or inability to swallow means stop and seek help.

## Practising the plan before race day

A fuelling plan is only usable if your gut has met it. Use at least three or four long runs to rehearse the same products, the same hourly rate and the same timing relative to the start. Start with the amount you can tolerate and increase gradually across the block, rather than jumping to the race target in the final week. Record what you took and when, plus any stomach symptoms in the following two hours; that record, not the label, tells you what works.

Carry the plan in a form you can read while moving: a small card, a watch alarm every 20 minutes, or a written split chart. Deciding mid-race how much you have already eaten is a common reason runners under-fuel in the second half.

## Heat, hills and a slower day

Hot weather raises fluid needs and shifts blood flow toward the skin, which makes a fixed gel schedule feel heavier. Hills slow you down, so an hourly carbohydrate target translates into more frequent small intakes rather than the same number of gels at the same kilometre marks. In both cases keep the rate, change the spacing. If you are significantly slower than planned, the finish-line total matters less than maintaining a steady intake that your stomach has already accepted in training.

## Common misconceptions

- Treating "gels per hour" as standard; packets are not grams.
- Trying new products on race day; first trials belong in training.
- Drinking nothing to avoid toilets; dehydration speeds fatigue and worsens gut tolerance.
- Assuming more caffeine is better; side effects rise with dose.

## When to stop

Stop with persistent vomiting, dizziness, confusion, chest pain, fainting or palpitations; in heat, suspect heat stroke and call emergency services.

## Frequency and progression

Rehearse in a weekly long run, adjust every two to three weeks, lock the plan three to four weeks out, and add nothing new in the final two weeks. Raise hourly grams by about 10% only after two clean long runs.

## Checklist

- Grams per serving and totals calculated.
- Aid stations and self-carried rules confirmed.
- Weather checked, with hot and cold variants.
- Times written and shared with a companion.
- Caffeine, if used, tested, not disturbing sleep.

## Scope and limits

No individual prescription, fluid volume or medical clearance. People with diabetes, gastrointestinal disease, pregnancy, disordered-eating history or medication should consult a doctor or registered dietitian.

## Beyond 2.5 hours: 60 g/h is not everyone's ceiling

30–60 g/h covers most races of one to three hours. After 2.5–3 hours, a single glucose source can saturate the same intestinal transporter, so the practical range moves toward 60–90 g/h when glucose and fructose are combined and the gut has been trained. That ceiling is not a badge of professionalism; it is a tolerance-dependent range shaped by gastric emptying, sweat rate, intensity and product concentration.

| Race duration | Start here | Possible ceiling | What to rehearse |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 60–90 min | 0–30 g/h | Not needed | Pre-race meal |
| 1.5–2.5 h | 30–60 g/h | 60 g/h | Small sips every 15–20 min |
| 2.5–4 h | 60–75 g/h | 90 g/h | Multiple transportable carbs, gut tolerance, aid spacing |
| 4 h+ | 60–90 g/h | Individual tolerance | Solid/liquid mix, night and cold |

The ceiling is a range from research and practice, not a mandatory target. Going from never exceeding 60 g/h in training to 90 g/h on race day is an experiment on your gut.

## Match the plan to the race

| Event | Carbohydrate focus | Fluid and sodium | Caffeine | Common error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5K/10K | Normal meal 1–4 h before | Small amounts by thirst | Not needed | Over-drinking |
| Half | 1–4 g/kg 1–4 h before | Small, frequent in heat | Optional | Heavy solid meal too close |
| Marathon | 30–60 g/h, split by aid stations | Sweat-rate based | Optional late | Counting gels, not grams |
| 50–100K | 60–90 g/h, multi-source | Fluid + electrolytes | Optional; watch sleep | Waiting until hungry |
| 100 mi/multi-day | 60–90 g/h, solid/liquid alternate | Sodium by sweat and weather | Cautious at night | All gels, no backup |

Races shorter than a half marathon rarely need carbohydrate loading. Marathon and ultra runners need to train race fuelling as its own skill. Loading before the race and fuelling during it are separate jobs.

## Two worked examples

**Example A: 70 kg runner, 4:00 marathon.** At 60 g/h, four hours need about 240 g carbohydrate. With 22 g gels, that is about 11 gels; plan 10, one every 25 minutes, with half a gel held for the final 20 minutes. Take 150–200 mL water with each gel to avoid a concentrated slurry sitting in the stomach. If aid stations are every 5 km at 5:40/km, they are about 28 minutes apart; carry at least one portion between stations and do not assume every station stocks your product.

**Example B: 65 kg runner, 10-hour trail race.** At 70 g/h, ten hours need about 700 g carbohydrate. Split it into one 22 g gel per hour, 250 mL of 8% drink (20 g), and about 25 g of chews or banana: roughly 67 g/h. At night, switch to liquids or soft food that are easy to swallow; take a sodium-containing solid or electrolyte drink every 2–3 hours. If nausea persists, slow down and switch to small sips rather than forcing the table.

## Failure modes and when to downgrade

- **Bloating, nausea, reflux:** halve each portion, switch solids to liquids, lengthen the interval; do not simultaneously add gels and concentration.
- **One vomit but still walking:** move to carbohydrate drink only and reassess for 20–30 minutes; repeated vomiting means stop.
- **Dizziness, confusion, weaving:** consider hyponatraemia, low blood glucose, heat illness or dehydration; another gel is not the answer. Stop and seek help.
- **Plain water with heavy sweating:** include electrolytes in long races, but more sodium is not automatically better.
- **Caffeine as dinner:** caffeine does not replace carbohydrate or sleep; excess worsens heart rate, anxiety and gut symptoms.

## Put the plan into training and race day

Rehearse three long runs: one at 60 g/h, one at 75 g/h, and one using race-day aid-station spacing. Record carbohydrate grams, fluid millilitres, sodium milligrams, gut symptoms and weather each hour. On race day, copy only a combination you have rehearsed; do not try a new brand or concentration. Write the hourly plan on your bib or hand rather than relying on a phone.

Check the fuelling plan against pacing and cut-offs. If the second half slows, the same hourly intake becomes harder; lower the target pace first, then switch solids to liquids. Fuelling is not a separate spreadsheet; it has to sit inside race execution.

## Caffeine and fuelling boundaries

Caffeine reduces perceived fatigue but does not replace carbohydrate, fluid or sleep. First use on race day can bring palpitations, anxiety, stomach upset and frequent urination; rehearse it in training before racing. Large afternoon doses disturb night sleep, especially after an ultra.

Caffeine is not a “final 10 km” switch. If you are dizzy, cold or weaving, it will not fix low blood glucose, low sodium or hypothermia; stop and fuel first.

## The last 24 hours of carbohydrate

The last 24 hours are not “pasta frenzy”. Take 8–10 g/kg across meals and snacks, using familiar, low-fibre, easy-to-digest food; eat 1–4 g/kg 3–4 hours before, and only a small amount one hour before. Too much, too oily or too fibrous shifts blood flow to the gut after the start.
