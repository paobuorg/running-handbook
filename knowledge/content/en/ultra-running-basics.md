## Definition and preconditions

An ultramarathon is longer than a marathon, often a 100 km or 100 mile trail race. The page applies to runners preparing for 100 km. It holds with a stable base, semi-autonomy and mandatory kit.

## What 100 km actually demands

The core is eating, thermoregulation, navigation and fatigue for many hours; a hypothetical 12 min/km means about 20 hours. Western States medical information states participation is at the runner's own risk with numerous risks, some serious or rarely fatal; inaccessibility may make timely help difficult or impossible, and many sections are accessible only by foot, horse or helicopter; runners may develop hypothermia, hyperthermia, dehydration, hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, disorientation and exhaustion; evacuation costs fall on the runner.

## Semi-autonomy: kit carried throughout

UTMB rules require runners to handle changing mountain conditions without outside assistance: night, wind, cold, fog, rain and snow at altitude. Carry all mandatory equipment for the whole race; marshals may check packs; non-compliance can lead to disqualification.

## What aid stations provide

Aid stations provide drinks and food eaten on site; the organisation provides only water or sports drink for refilling. For UTMB, CCC and TDS, one assistant is allowed at certain stations with a bag of at most 30 litres.

## Fuelling rate

For exercise over 60 minutes, take 30 to 60 grams of carbohydrate per hour in a 6 to 8% solution, every 10 to 15 minutes; practise and adjust. Protein does not replace carbohydrate.

## Night, weather and walking

Night needs a headlamp, spare batteries, extra warmth and a conservative pace; mountain weather can turn within hours. Walk climbs, jog flats, control descents.

## Tapering

A meta-analysis of 14 studies found time-trial and time-to-exhaustion improvements after tapering; a 41 to 60% volume reduction with maintained intensity and frequency, up to 21 days, was associated with better time-trial performance. The authors also note that the optimal taper duration varies between individuals.

## How to do it: three steps

1. Build over 12 to 16 weeks with one long run weekly and a lighter week every 3 to 4 weeks.
2. Carry the full mandatory kit on at least 2 long runs, including headlamp, batteries, waterproof layer and first aid.
3. Split the race by aid stations, drop bags and escape points; write expected time and intake for each; taper for 2 to 3 weeks.

## Worked example: 100 km, hourly intake and 3 drop bags

Numbers are hypothetical. Assume 100 km, 4000 m gain, 20 hours, 12 min/km. At 60 grams of carbohydrate per hour, 20 hours needs about 1200 grams. Assume 500 ml of an 8% solution per hour gives about 40 grams, plus 20 grams from solid food. Place 3 drop bags at 30, 55, 75 km.

## Crew, drop bags and the logistics that decide the day

In a semi-autonomous race, your support is concentrated at a few points rather than spread along the course. A drop bag is useful only if it contains what you will actually want at that hour: dry layers, a specific food, spare batteries, foot care. Pack it by segment, label it, and know which bags you must collect in person and which the organisation moves for you. Do not plan to rely on a crew member meeting you at a spot that the rules do not permit.

Read the rulebook for the things that disqualify: missing mandatory equipment at a check, failing to register at a checkpoint, accepting personal assistance, sleeping outside designated areas, or leaving a life base after its exit barrier. These rules are not administrative detail; they are the conditions under which the race can exist. Knowing them also tells you where the real risk sits, because the points the organiser regulates are usually the points where help is hardest to reach.

## Fuelling on a clock, not on appetite

Appetite is an unreliable guide after several hours. Set a rate and repeat it: small amounts at fixed intervals, with solid food early while the stomach accepts it and more liquid calories later if chewing becomes difficult. Practise the same rate in long training days, because the gut adapts to what it repeatedly receives. If intake falls behind and nausea appears, slow down, switch to fluids, and treat persistent vomiting or confusion as a reason to stop rather than a problem to push through.

## Common misconceptions

- Scaling a marathon plan by distance. 100 km is not a scaled-up marathon.
- Using mandatory kit first on race day.
- Treating aid stations as restaurants; food is eaten on site.
- Believing more taper is always better.

## Overtraining, medical risk and when not to start

Overtraining syndrome is a maladapted response to excessive exercise without adequate rest; persistent fatigue and performance decline should prompt assessment and exclusion of illness; there is no single diagnostic marker; management includes rest and correcting contributing factors. On race day, disorientation, hypothermia or hyperthermia, dehydration, hyponatremia, vomiting, altered consciousness or inability to walk need medical help. Do not start with fever, persistent fatigue, an unhealed injury, incomplete kit or no escape plan.

## Frequency and progression

One long run weekly, increasing for 3 to 4 weeks then a lighter week; practise night or bad weather every 2 weeks. Taper for 2 to 3 weeks, cutting volume about 41 to 60% while maintaining intensity and frequency.

## Pre-race checklist

- Mandatory kit carried in full on at least 2 long runs.
- 30 to 60 grams of carbohydrate per hour practised.
- Aid stations, drop bags and escape points marked.
- Emergency contacts and medical information confirmed.

## Scope and limits

An introduction, not an individual plan. Ultra races carry serious, sometimes rarely fatal risk; official race information governs. People with cardiovascular, kidney or electrolyte problems, diabetes, eating disorder, severe allergy or pregnancy should seek medical assessment.
