## Conclusion: which to choose when

The first principle of traction gear is to identify what the surface is missing: soft wet mud and fallen leaves need deep lugs; packed snow and ice need metal teeth biting in; snow grains, gravel and thorns entering the collar need a gaiter. The three do not replace each other. Many winter routes pair deep-lug trail shoes with gaiters, adding slip-on microspikes only for hard ice or icy steps. NPS winter hiking guidance recommends traction devices on ice and snow, and deciding to turn back according to weather and trail conditions; this gear choice cannot replace surface judgment and route familiarity, and the broader winter plan is in [Cold-weather running](/en/knowledge/cold-weather-running/).

Short decision: hard-packed dirt, gravel, wet grass and short mud sections call for trail shoes with the right lug depth; mixed packed snow and hard ice call for removable microspikes; deep snow, scree slopes and thorn vegetation call for gaiters. Microspikes are walking and trail-running traction devices, not rigid mountaineering crampons for steep snow ridges or ice climbs. On bare hard rock, smooth slabs and deep ice faces, judge a turn-around or route change rather than relying on the teeth under your shoes.

| Surface condition | First choice | Alternative | Decision point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hard-packed dirt, gravel, wet grass | Trail shoes with matching lug depth | Deep-lug backup | Choose lug depth by surface softness; wet mud and soft ground need longer multi-directional teeth |
| Packed snow and icy trail mixed | Microspikes | Deep-lug trail shoe | Metal teeth bite into ice and snow; confirm match to your shoe model first |
| Deep snow, scree slope, thorn grass | Gaiters | Low-profile slip-on style | Block debris from the collar; add no extra grip |
| Bare hard rock, smooth slabs, deep ice face | Turn around or reroute | Do not force through | Microspikes offer limited grip on hard rock and smooth surfaces |

## Option by option

**Trail shoe lugs** are the baseline for everyday traction. The brand trail-shoe guide splits surfaces into two classes: hard-packed dirt or gravel suits shallow 2–4 mm lugs; mud, wet grass and soft fell ground suit 5 mm-plus multi-directional deep lugs. Lugs cut into soft ground and shed mud between teeth; they are not designed for hard ice, where rubber teeth cannot bite. A waterproof upper helps in cold wet or snowy conditions but is less breathable and dries slowly, while mesh suits warm weather; that trade-off is discussed in [Trail running shoes](/en/knowledge/running-shoes/) and [Shoe fit and cushioning](/en/knowledge/shoe-fit-and-cushioning/).

**Microspikes** provide metal-embedded traction on snow and ice. In the official Kahtoola MICROspikes sample: twelve stainless steel spikes per foot, 3/8 in (about 9.5 mm) spike length; pair weight S 11 oz / 312 g, M 11.9 oz / 338 g, L 13.1 oz / 372 g, XL 13.5 oz / 382 g; packed size 13 × 8 × 5 cm. The TPE elastic harness retains elasticity down to -22 F (-30 C); an integrated toe bail keeps the toe from pushing through the harness, and a raised heel tab aids donning with cold or gloved hands. The brand positions the product for hiking, backpacking and steep mountain terrain. Care is lukewarm-water wash and air-dry, inspect before each use, and remove the device from footwear between uses. The boundary to state clearly: chain microspikes rely on a harness wrapping the shoe, and narrow low-profile running shoes must be checked against the size range; on bare hard rock and smooth slabs the teeth mainly touch at points, without the rigid forefoot support of a mountaineering crampon.

**Gaiters** add no grip; they are a barrier. In the official Salomon Trail Gaiters High sample: single piece 5 oz; body fabric 78% polyamide / 22% elastane, back body 100% polyester, insert 88% styrene-butadiene rubber / 12% polyester. They keep gravel, snow, thorns and loose debris out of the foot and protect the ankle bone from knocks and scrapes, fixed with a Velcro closure and an under-foot strap. Care is hand wash cold at 30 C; no bleach, dry cleaning, ironing or tumble drying. Low gaiters cover less and weigh less; high gaiters protect the ankle bone and upper collar more fully; choose by your shoe collar height and the debris type on route.

**Price and risk boundary.** A pricier traction device mainly buys harness fit, tooth steel and cold elasticity; gaiters pay for durable outer fabric and fixing structure. On dry hard dirt on a short, non-icy route, basic trail shoes create no risk. Once you enter packed snow, hard ice or deep-snow scree, skipping a traction device matched to your shoe and a debris barrier is the real risk.

## Conditions for choosing deep-lug trail shoes

**Choose trail shoes with matching lug depth if:**

- The route is mainly hard-packed dirt, gravel, wet grass and thin mud, without continuous hard ice;
- Training values ground feedback and light weight over carrying a chain device;
- Weather and surface are controllable, and pre-run checks show no icy gullies, shaded pavements or stone steps.

## Conditions for choosing microspikes or gaiters

**Choose microspikes if:**

- The route includes packed snow, icy paths or shaded morning pavement in winter;
- You have checked that the spike size matches your running or hiking shoe, with a harness wrapping toe and heel;
- You will remove and stow them off snow and ice, so chains and teeth are not worn on hard rock or pavement.

**Choose gaiters if:**

- The route has deep snow, scree, fallen thorns or tall grass that pours into the collar;
- You want ankle-bone knock protection, or your socks often wet through from slush;
- You understand gaiters do not improve ice grip, and a traction device is still needed on hard ice.

## What not to do

1. **Use microspikes as mountaineering crampons.** Rigidity is insufficient to support steep snow ridges or ice climbing; turn back on surfaces outside the traction range.
2. **Buy spikes without checking shoe fit.** A harness that does not match toe, heel and sole curve can detach on descents or in snow; size charts and a try-on must come first.
3. **Wear spikes on bare hard rock and tarmac for long stretches.** Chains and teeth wear fast, and on hard rock the teeth cannot embed, which can add slip risk.
4. **Ignore loose gaiter straps.** An under-foot strap or ankle Velcro not secured lets the gaiter shift with stride, and debris still gets in.
5. **Store the harness under sustained bend in extreme cold.** Fatigued elastic cracks and snaps; spike failure often starts at harness cuts and eyelets.
6. **Look only at traction, not clothing and supplies.** NPS winter guidance also calls for layered clothing, sunglasses, sunscreen, water and food, a map/compass or GPS, and a willingness to turn around when visibility drops.

## Common failure modes and early signals

- **Spikes mismatch the shoe and detach on descents.** Early signals: harness off the toe, heel looseness, the whole device shifting when stepping in snow; continuing can leave a bare foot on ice on a hard slope. Stop rule: stop and refit immediately; if fit is unreliable, remove the spikes for safe ground and recheck the size after the event.
- **Rubber/elastic harness stiffens or cracks in the cold.** Early signals: whitened harness, creases, fine cracks around eyelets, lost rebound when donning; cold and bend accelerate aging. Stop rule: retire the device at the first crack; do not carry a suspect pair onto snow or ice.
- **Gaiter lets snow in or straps slip.** Early signals: snow over the collar, wet socks, under-foot strap rubbing the foot; usually a fixed point not tightened or a gaiter height that does not match the collar. Stop rule: stop and re-bind; switch to a high gaiter in deep snow sections.
- **Deep lugs slip and wear on hard rock.** Early signals: sole slide on turns, blunted teeth, weaker grip feedback; continuous hard stone and pavement consume deep lugs fast. Stop rule: remove spikes or switch to a hard-ground shoe on long rock sections, and inspect lug wear afterward.

## Worked examples: two winter route setups (assumed)

**Example one: an 18 km daytime winter ridge training run, with shaded packed snow and occasional icy stone steps.** Assume deep-lug trail shoes, with a pair of microspikes matched to the shoe model in the pack; put them on for icy steps and shaded hard snow, remove them on dry hard dirt and stone. Decide on gaiters by gravel and dew on route; keep winter layering, water and food on board.

**Example two: a 10 km early-morning suburban greenway with frost but no continuous ice.** Assume the surface is grass frost and thin dew, no stream crossings or shaded slopes; trail shoes with suitable grip suffice, carrying spikes and gaiters as backup rather than wearing chains early and wearing them down.

Both examples are instructional assumptions. Actual grip depends on temperature, snow quality, compaction and shoe condition; rehearse the combination on the same route in pre-race winter training, and see the broader winter setup in [Cold-weather running](/en/knowledge/cold-weather-running/).

## Boundaries

This page is outdoor gear and safety education, not mountaineering instruction. Microspike traction range, size match and load limits follow the brand's official guidance; steep snow ridges, ice climbing and winter rescue require dedicated skills and equipment. When ice, dropping visibility or signs of heat loss appear, turn around first; the broader risk judgment is in [Route safety](/en/knowledge/route-safety/).

## What would change this conclusion

- **Snow quality and surface:** deep soft snow, packed snow, hard ice, wet mud and bare rock demand different traction.
- **Temperature:** cold changes the hardness of rubber soles and elastic harnesses, and shifts icing times.
- **Shoe model:** runners, low hikers and high boots match spikes and gaiters in different ranges.
- **Race rules and route:** winter trail races may issue recommendations on collar height, traction devices or gaiters; follow the current handbook.
- **Evidence updates:** lug shapes, harness elasticity and wear data keep evolving; the page is revised when official specs change.
