## Definition and scope

**Periodization** divides a longer training period into preparation, competition and transition phases, and changes volume, intensity and specificity across those phases. It answers "what to train when", not "how fast to run each session". This page covers three common arrangements — linear (traditional), block and reverse linear — why taper and recovery weeks exist, the mistakes recreational runners most often make, and what the comparative research actually supports.

Scope: you already have a period of stable training, a target race within the next few months, and you want to arrange a season rather than decide every week from scratch. If you are returning from injury, pregnant, or managing cardiovascular, metabolic or respiratory disease, have a clinician or qualified coach set the season structure. **Periodization cannot replace individual coaching**; the same template means a different load for runners with different volume, recovery capacity and life stress.

## Three models and how they differ

| Model | Load progression | Time structure | Typical use | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Linear (traditional) | High volume and low intensity in preparation; volume falls and race-specific intensity rises as the race approaches | Season split into preparation, competition and transition; one or a few peaks | Common in 5K/10K and marathon training; also common among elite middle- and long-distance runners | Haugen et al. 2022; Casado et al. 2022; Galán-Rioja et al. 2023 |
| Block | Training concentrated on a small number of target abilities, with highly concentrated loads inside each block and blocks sequenced | Often 1–8 week concentrated blocks, sequenced as accumulation, transmutation and realization; can support multiple peaks | High-level athletes who need several peaks in one season | Issurin 2010; Mølmen et al. 2019; Galán-Rioja et al. 2023 |
| Reverse linear | The opposite emphasis to linear: peak volume arrives in the weeks before the taper, with race-specific intensity pushed later | Volume peaks late in the season, then a taper | Some marathon runners; definitions are not fully consistent across the literature | Haugen et al. 2022; González-Ravé et al. 2022 |

The difference is not "which model is more scientific" but **where the load peak sits**. Linear puts the biggest load early and narrows toward the race. Block cuts the load into concentrated blocks, each solving a small number of problems. Reverse linear pushes the volume peak into the weeks before the race, then tapers. Reverse linear is the least standardized: some papers describe it as high intensity first and volume later, while Haugen et al. 2022 describe some marathon runners registering their highest volumes in the weeks before the taper. The common feature is that the emphasis is reversed relative to linear, not a simple "fast first, long later" rule.

## Why taper and recovery weeks exist

Training stimuli need time to become adaptation, and fatigue can temporarily mask true fitness. A taper removes fatigue so that adaptations already earned can show; a recovery week gives the body a lower-load window inside a longer block so fatigue does not keep accumulating.

The evidence for tapering is more specific than the evidence for recovery weeks:

- **Bosquet et al. 2007** screened 182 studies and included 27 in a meta-analysis: the optimal taper was about **2 weeks**, with training volume decreased exponentially by **41%–60%** while **intensity and frequency were maintained**.
- **PLOS ONE 2023** reviewed and pooled **14 studies**: a 41%–60% volume reduction, maintained intensity and frequency, and a taper lasting up to about **21 days** was associated with improved time-trial performance. A pre-taper overload produced a larger effect in some studies, but the authors also note that the optimal taper duration varies between individuals.
- **Haugen et al. 2022** describe world-class 5K/10K and marathon runners starting their taper **7–10 days** before the main competition.

These three do not fully agree: the elite 7–10 day description and the meta-analytic "about two weeks" are not the same thing. A practical reading: shorter races can use the 7–10 day pattern; a longer race or one preceded by a heavy block can sit inside the two-week frame. **No direct comparison shows one is better**, so do not present either as the only correct choice.

Recovery weeks do not have an equally clear "optimal ratio". The site's [volume progression guide](/en/knowledge/training-volume-and-progression/) already states that a lighter week every 3–4 weeks is common practice, not a research finding. A more reliable gate is the 24-hour response: whether pain, stiffness and overall fatigue at the same site and time have returned to the pre-run level. If recovery is still incomplete for several days, reduce load rather than follow the calendar.

## How to arrange a taper week: cutting volume, keeping intensity

A taper is not stopping training; it removes fatigue while keeping the stimulus. The shared wording from Bosquet et al. 2007 and PLOS ONE 2023 is a 41%–60% volume reduction with intensity and frequency maintained; Haugen et al. 2022 describe world-class runners starting 7–10 days before the main event. For recreational runners, cut long-run volume first, keep short strides and race-pace sessions, and do not top up in the last week.

**Worked example (illustrative assumption, not an official prescription).** Baseline full week before the taper: 60 km. At the two ends of the Bosquet range:

- −41%: taper volume = 60 × (1 − 0.41) = 60 × 0.59 = 35.4 km;
- −60%: taper volume = 60 × (1 − 0.60) = 60 × 0.40 = 24.0 km.

Do not cut quality sessions proportionally with volume: a week with three sessions keeps 2–3 short quality sessions, shorter in duration but keeping the pace stimulus. Maintaining frequency means roughly the same number of sessions per week, not the same session length. The arithmetic only shows how to apply the range; a 60 km baseline does not suit everyone.

## The most common periodization mistakes

- **The same intensity all year.** If every run is "moderately fast", you get neither true low intensity nor true high intensity. Haugen et al. 2022 describe world-class runners doing **≥80% of running volume** at low intensity; Casado et al. 2022 describe elite middle- and long-distance runners using a hard-day/easy-day basis. Year-round moderate running erases both stimuli.
- **Tapering too early.** Starting a large volume cut long before the race removes the last training stimulus. The Bosquet "about two weeks" and the Haugen "7–10 days" describe competitive athletes; starting much earlier has thinner support.
- **Tapering too late.** Adding load or "topping up" in the last week leaves fatigue too little time to clear. The point of a taper is to maintain intensity while reducing volume, not to cram training at the end.
- **Racing too often to fit a cycle.** Haugen et al. 2022 describe world-class 5K/10K runners racing about **9 ± 3 times** per year, with the last race **4 ± 2 weeks** before the main championship; marathon runners race about **6 ± 2 times**, with the last race **10 ± 5 weeks** before the main event. If you race every 2–3 weeks, there is no room for preparation, specificity and taper. Choose priorities instead of treating every race as a main event.
- **Copying elite volume or a "block" label.** Haugen et al. 2022 describe world-class 5K/10K runners doing **130–190 km per week** across **11–14 sessions** in mid-preparation; that reflects an elite history and recovery context. Block periodization is also not automatically better: Mølmen et al. 2019 and Galán-Rioja et al. 2023 do not provide a stable conclusion that block is superior.
- **Treating a recovery week as a fixed formula.** "One lighter week every four" is a practice convention, not a proven optimal frequency. Sleep, pain and the 24-hour response are better guides than a rigid calendar.
- **Stopping entirely or topping up during the taper.** The evidence supports reducing load, not detraining; sudden extra sessions cancel the recovery the taper is meant to create.

## Failure modes: how to tell the arrangement is not working

- **Staying in medium intensity all year.** Every run sits in a "chattable but not easy" zone: easy days are not easy enough, hard days are not hard enough. In the log, no clear easy/hard split shows up in any week.
- **The recovery gate fails.** The same site still aches 24 hours later, morning resting heart rate stays high, or sleep worsens, yet load still rises by calendar. That means the arrangement is not being absorbed, not that "one more session will adapt you".
- **Races squeeze the training blocks.** A race every 2–3 weeks cuts preparation, specificity and taper into pieces. In Haugen et al. 2022's elite description, the last marathon race sits 10 ± 5 weeks before the main event; an amateur season must also leave a full taper window.
- **Copying the template.** Elite volume (130–190 km/week, 11–14 sessions for track runners in mid-preparation) or a block label pasted onto your own weekly plan; with different volume, history and recovery, the same block length does not produce the same adaptation.
- **Taper direction reversed.** Adding volume or topping up long runs in the last week, or stopping training entirely: the former leaves fatigue unresolved, the latter loses the last stimulus.

Whether the arrangement works is judged not by one session's pace but by recovery state, training performance and race-day feel over 2–3 weeks. If signals do not improve, seek assessment from a qualified coach or clinician rather than switching models again.

## Decision table: choosing an arrangement by condition

| Condition (if) | Action (then) | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One clear target race in the season, the rest are supporting races | Use a linear frame: base volume, specificity intensity, taper 1–2 weeks before | Linear is a common frame, not proven better for everyone |
| Several peaks needed in the year and recovery permits | Try a block frame: concentrated blocks on a small number of abilities | Block evidence is small and low quality; start with a single-season structure |
| Volume peak habitually sits in the last few weeks | Arrange reverse linear as described, with volume peak before the taper | Reverse linear definitions are inconsistent; it is not superior to other forms |
| Short training history, unstable recovery, or returning from injury | Prioritize frequency and recovery weeks; do not fit elite block lengths into the season | Periodization cannot replace individual coaching or medical assessment |
| The 24-hour response has not recovered for several weeks | Stop adding load, treat it as a recovery week, then decide whether to delay the target | The recovery gate outranks the plan calendar |

## An example amateur season

The table below is an **illustrative assumption** that shows phase order. It is not a validated plan and it gives no percentages.

| Phase | Weeks | Focus | Quality sessions | Long run | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base | 4 | Build aerobic base; add short strides or hills | 1 short quality session per week | The week's longest easy run | Do not chase pace; establish frequency |
| Specific | 4 | Race-specific rhythm and target distance | 1–2 per week, adjusted by recovery | Easy long run | At least two days between quality sessions |
| Taper | 1–2 | Remove fatigue, keep short intensity | Shortened; keep a few strides | Clearly shorter | Use Bosquet's 41%–60% frame or Haugen's 7–10 days |
| Race week | 1 | Race | No quality session | No long run | Short easy runs and strides only |
| Recovery | 1–2 | Recover | No quality session | Short easy runs | Do not rush back to pre-race load |

The table specifies order and spacing, not kilometres or exact ratios. Public research does not provide one season template for all recreational runners; leaving distance relative to your own baseline is safer than copying a number that looks precise.

## Racing calendar: main race, supporting races and gaps

Pick the main race first, then place other races before it, rather than preparing every race as a main event. Haugen et al. 2022 describe world-class runners: 5K/10K runners race about **9 ± 3 times** per year, the last race **4 ± 2 weeks** before the main championship; marathon specialists race about **6 ± 2 times**, the last race **10 ± 5 weeks** before the main event. These are descriptive statistics of elite practice, not amateur entry rules.

For amateur scheduling, separate the roles:

| Role | Purpose | Gap to the main race (reference) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main race | The season's single peak; specificity and taper are built around it | — |
| Supporting race | Test rhythm, fueling and start procedure | Several weeks before the main race; do not occupy the final taper window |
| Tune-up / time trial | Short distance or time trial, find pace feel | Early preparation or specificity phase |

The gaps list only descriptive ranges appearing in the elite research; an amateur's recovery capacity, work stress and injury history differ, so spacing should follow your own 24-hour response and post-race recovery. Frequent-race arrangement is in [The Training Week](/en/knowledge/training-week/).

## How far the evidence goes: comparisons are inconsistent

This is the most important part of the page. Comparisons between models do not agree, and it is not legitimate to pick the result that supports a preferred model and call it the conclusion.

> **Two positions side by side (no averaging).**
>
> - **"Periodization models do differ" position:** Mølmen et al. 2019's systematic review and meta-analysis (20 studies, 6 pooled) report small-to-moderate effects of block over traditional on VO₂max and maximal power; source type: systematic review and meta-analysis, but the authors note small samples and a mean PEDro 3.7/10.
> - **"No proven superior model" position:** Almquist et al. 2022's load-matched RCT and the systematic reviews by Galán-Rioja et al. 2023 and González-Ravé et al. 2022 find no stable performance advantage for any model; Kiely 2018 challenges periodization theory at the conceptual level. Source types: randomized controlled trial, systematic review, theoretical critique.
>
> The two sides cannot be averaged into "anything goes": the evidence concludes that models lack a consistent ranking, not that any arrangement works.

**Evidence favouring block periodization:**

- Mølmen et al. 2019 screened **2,905** records and included **20** studies, pooling **6**. Block compared with traditional showed a pooled standardized mean difference of **0.40 (95% CI 0.02–0.79)** for VO₂max and **0.28 (95% CI 0.01–0.54)** for maximal power; endurance performance at various thresholds showed moderate-to-large effects. But the authors explicitly warn that the studies were small and generally of low methodological quality, with a mean **PEDro score of 3.7/10**, so the results need caution.

**Evidence against a clear "one model is better" conclusion:**

- Almquist et al. 2022 ran a 12-week, load-matched comparison: block **n = 14**, traditional **n = 16**, in trained cyclists (VO₂peak 58 ± 8 ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹). There were no differences in time-trial performance or most adaptations. Red blood cell volume increased **10 ± 16%** more in the block group, while capillaries around type I fibres increased **20 ± 12%** more in the traditional group. The authors concluded that block was not superior to a "best-practice" traditional programme with progressively increasing load.
- Galán-Rioja et al. 2023 reviewed **7 studies with 161** trained road cyclists. Block lengths ranged from **1–8 weeks**, with block training volume **8.75–11.68 h·week⁻¹**; traditional volume was **7.5–10.76 h·week⁻¹**. The conclusion: no evidence currently favours a specific periodization model over **8–12 weeks**, and few studies have examined a full season systematically.
- González-Ravé et al. 2022 reviewed **11 studies**: reverse periodization was **no more effective** than other forms for sports performance, muscular endurance, maximal strength or maximal oxygen uptake; the authors called for more comparative studies.
- Kiely 2018 is not a comparative trial but a direct challenge to the conceptual foundations of periodization theory: the assumptions have remained largely unchanged for decades while the science of stress has evolved, and the author argues for re-evaluation rather than treating the traditional model as unquestioned background.

**Descriptive practice also does not support a single model:**

- Haugen et al. 2022 describe world-class middle- and long-distance runners using single or double periodization; some marathon runners use reverse linear and register their highest volumes in the weeks before the taper. Elite practice itself is not one template.

Putting both sides together, the reasonable conclusion is: **periodization models are planning languages, not a proven ranking.** Current evidence does not show that one model is better for every event and every level; comparisons are short, small and of mixed quality, and "block", "traditional" and "reverse linear" are defined differently across papers. You can use the models to make choices, but not to make guarantees.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Periodization is a scientifically proven best method."** It is a planning framework; Kiely 2018 directly questions its conceptual basis, and comparative studies do not agree.
- **"Block is always better than traditional."** Mølmen 2019's effects are small and the methods are weak; Almquist 2022 and Galán-Rioja 2023 find no performance advantage.
- **"Reverse linear is the advanced model."** González-Ravé 2022's 11 studies show it is no more effective than other forms, and its definition varies across the literature.
- **"Elites train this way, so I should too."** Haugen 2022 describes world-class volume, history and recovery conditions; copying elite mileage or block length carries more risk than benefit for recreational runners.
- **"A taper means stopping running."** Bosquet 2007 and PLOS ONE 2023 support reducing volume while maintaining intensity and frequency.
- **"A recovery week must come every four weeks."** That is a practice convention, not a proven optimal frequency; use the 24-hour response and sleep.
- **"More races mean shorter cycles are better."** Frequent racing compresses preparation, specificity and taper; choose a main race first, then schedule the others.

## Scope and safety

This page does not provide an individual season plan, exact percentages or a guarantee for race day, and it **does not give medical diagnosis** or replace a clinician's face-to-face assessment and prescription. Periodization should match your race calendar, baseline volume, recovery capacity and injury history; if you have an injury, a chronic disease, are pregnant or take medication, consult a clinician or qualified coach first.

If you develop worsening pain, a clearly elevated resting heart rate, several poor nights of sleep, persistently low mood, a sustained drop in performance, or feel more fatigued after the taper, stop adding load and seek assessment. Chest pain, fainting or severe breathlessness are emergencies; seek immediate care.

**Periodization cannot replace individual coaching.** A generic template can help you organise the questions, but it cannot decide whether a particular quality session should happen or whether a particular race should be skipped.

## What would change this advice

- New, higher-quality direct comparisons (especially same-event, same-level, load-matched randomized trials) that stably show one model superior for a specific distance or level would move this page's arrangement recommendation.
- Changes in your own training history, injury history, work stress and recovery capacity: base length, taper window and race spacing should all be rearranged accordingly.
- Updated institutional or research conclusions on taper dose (duration, volume cut, intensity retention); the current 41%–60% and about two weeks come from mixed-sport meta-analyses, and running-specific new evidence would be incorporated.
- This page does not give medical diagnosis; cardiovascular, metabolic or injury questions are decided by a clinician's assessment and a qualified coach.
