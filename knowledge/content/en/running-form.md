## Definition and who this page applies to

Running technique here means observable variables — cadence, stride, breathing, hill handling and surface adaptation — not one posture to copy. It applies to pain-free runners who can run 20 minutes continuously.

## Observe before changing

Technique changes with speed, gradient, fatigue and surface. Record your natural cadence, whether you can speak in full sentences, how steps change uphill and whether you speed up downhill. Observe over 2–3 sessions, then change one variable at a time.

## Cadence is not a 180 exam

A systematic review found that at fixed speed a higher stride rate was associated with less vertical displacement, lower ground reaction force and less joint energy absorption. The authors suggested it may reduce injury-linked biomechanical factors, but the studies were small and varied, so there is no universal target.

Count steps for 30 seconds in an easy run and double it. Treat 180 as a reference; if your cadence already feels easy, do not force it higher.

## Stride length mostly follows speed

Stride length is not a number to pull larger by itself. As speed rises, cadence and stride usually both increase; overstriding puts the landing farther ahead and adds braking and joint load. Decide speed first and let stride follow.

## Breathing is calibrated by the talk test

No single breathing rhythm applies to everyone. In the CDC talk test, moderate intensity lets you talk but not sing; vigorous intensity stops you saying more than a few words without pausing. An easy run should allow full sentences.

Nasal or mouth breathing both work; meeting ventilation demand matters. A fixed rhythm can add shoulder and neck tension.

## Uphill and downhill are different skills

Uphill running has higher step frequency, shorter swing and aerial phase and a greater duty factor. Downhill running has longer aerial time, lower step frequency, more rear-foot striking and more eccentric work.

Shorten stride and keep breathing easy uphill. Downhill, control speed instead of letting it go.

## Surface differences are limited but real

A small study compared 30 habitual rear-foot strikers on artificial grass, rubber and concrete. Most joint-motion and loading variables did not differ significantly, but concrete produced higher ankle moments and frontal-plane hip and knee moments. The authors suggested harder surfaces may raise overuse risk; this was a 30-person short-term mechanics comparison and cannot show that a surface causes injury. On hard ground with fatigue, reduce intensity.

## How to run a small technique experiment in three steps

The 5% adjustment below is hypothetical.

1. Run easy for 10 minutes on flat ground, count steps for 30 seconds and double it.
2. Change one variable: at the same speed raise cadence about 5% for 3–5 minutes, or shorten stride only uphill.
3. Observe over 2–3 weeks with two short practices weekly; keep what feels natural and seek assessment for new pain.

## Technique variables at a glance

| Variable | Flat | Uphill | Downhill |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cadence | Natural | Higher | Lower |
| Stride | Rises with speed | Shorter | Lengthens |
| Breathing | Full sentences | Faster | Faster |
| Landing | Near centre of mass | Shorter | Lighter |

## Worked example: time cost of a shorter uphill stride

Assume a 1-kilometre climb at 6% gradient, about 60 metres of ascent. Flat stride is 1.0 metre; uphill it is 0.8 metres. That kilometre takes about 1000 steps flat and 1250 uphill, 25% more. At 170 steps per minute, the flat section takes about 5.9 minutes and the climb 7.4 minutes. These figures are hypothetical.

## Strength, mobility and the limits of form cues

Form cues are most useful as short reminders, not as a full description of how to run. "Run tall", "quick feet", "land under yourself" and "relax the shoulders" each point at one part of a complex movement, and a cue that helps one runner can make another stiffen. Choose one cue per session, say it once or twice, and then let the body organise the rest. Filming a short section from the side, at normal speed, is a better feedback loop than trying to feel every joint while moving.

Strength work supports form indirectly: a runner who can control a single-leg squat and hold a hip hinge usually has more room to maintain posture late in a run. Mobility work helps only where a specific restriction exists, and general stretching before running is not a reliable way to prevent injury. If a cue causes pain or changes your gait, drop the cue and treat the pain as the more important signal.

## Common misconceptions

- “Everyone must reach 180.” Studies are small and varied; height, pace and gradient change natural cadence.
- “A longer stride is faster.” Overstriding can add braking and joint load.
- “Nose-only breathing is better.” Both work; a fixed rhythm can add tension.
- “Downhill is faster flat running.” Longer flight and eccentric load make speed costly.

## When to stop and seek help

Stop and seek assessment for persistent pain, swelling, limping, restricted movement, night pain, or numbness, weakness or radiating symptoms. If the same area worsens, do not keep adapting. Chest pain, fainting or severe breathing difficulty is an emergency.

## Frequency and cycle: one variable over 2–4 weeks

Use a 2–4 week cycle: 3–5 minutes in easy runs in week one, 8–10 minutes in week two, brief hill use in week three, review in week four. Change one variable only, without adding speed or new shoes. Keep what feels natural after 8–12 weeks.

## Checklist

- [ ] Measure natural cadence on flat ground.
- [ ] Change only one variable at a time.
- [ ] Keep practices to 3–10 minutes over several days.
- [ ] Shorten stride uphill; control speed downhill.
- [ ] Record breathing and pain location.
- [ ] Seek assessment for new pain.

## Scope and limits

This page does not provide individual gait retraining, injury treatment or a prescription for every runner. Technique work does not replace sleep, load management, recovery, shoe assessment or medical care.
