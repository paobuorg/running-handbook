## Definition and preconditions

Post-race recovery is defined as the transition from finish to regular training, restoring fluid, energy, sleep and repair before rebuilding volume. It applies mainly to the 2 to 4 weeks after a marathon or ultramarathon. It holds when no acute injury needs emergency care and return decisions follow the body rather than the calendar. After a fall, head impact, inability to bear weight or marked swelling, seek care first.

## The first 24 hours

Take fluid and carbohydrate, adding protein if appetite allows; a carbohydrate-electrolyte drink works if solid food is hard. Shower, change clothes, then sleep. Alcohol affects sleep and recovery, so avoid heavy drinking on the first night.

## The first week: walking, sleep, food

The first week needs little running. Walk, move lightly and sleep, watching soreness, swelling and unusual pain. Delayed-onset muscle soreness appears a day or two after the race, with tenderness, stiffness and reduced strength; it differs from an acute injury. Keep regular hours, and let normal meals with carbohydrate, protein and vegetables replace precise calculation.

## The protein and carbohydrate frame

The ISSN protein position stand states that 1.4 to 2.0 g per kilogram per day is sufficient for most exercising individuals; per serving about 0.25 g/kg, or 20 to 40 g, distributed every 3 to 4 hours. Whole foods can meet the need; supplements are an option. For endurance athletes carbohydrate remains the priority for performance, while protein may offset muscle damage and support recovery. The ISSN nutrient-timing paper adds that carbohydrate timing and amount affect performance and recovery, adapted to the individual, the duration and the demand.

## How to do it: three steps for three weeks

1. First 7 days: walk and mobility only, logging pain, swelling and sleep; no quality sessions or long runs.
2. Week 2: easy running every other day for 20 to 30 minutes; add volume only if the run day and next day are normal.
3. Week 3: extend easy runs to 40 to 50 minutes; after 2 normal weeks, consider one quality session, otherwise stay easy.

## Worked example: a 70 kg runner

The numbers are hypothetical. Assume 70 kg and no acute injury. Protein at 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg is 70×1.4 = 98 g to 70×2.0 = 140 g a day; 0.25 g/kg is about 70×0.25 = 17.5 g per serving, so 20 to 40 g at each of 3 to 4 meals fits.

| Week | Running | Intensity | Criterion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Week 1 | None; walk 20 to 30 minutes | Very low | Walking pain-free |
| Week 2 | Easy run every other day, 20 to 30 minutes | Low | No next-day worsening |
| Week 3 | Easy run 40 to 50 minutes | Low | Stable for 2 weeks |
| Week 4 | One short quality session possible | Medium | No anomaly 48 hours after |

## The recovery window: stage criteria, not fixed days

The order is: first restore walking and daily life, then short easy running, and only later quality work and the long run. These windows are a common arrangement, not a universal prescription. The number of weeks needed after the same marathon varies with base, age, weather and post-race symptoms. Moving a stage depends on whether walking is normal, pain has settled, sleep and energy are back, and an easy run feels easy — not on how many days have passed.

If normal walking is still impossible in the first week, or soreness changes from muscular stiffness to a local sharp pain or swelling, do not move into week-two easy running; seek assessment first. General small-dose rules after a break are in [returning to running](/en/knowledge/return-to-running/), the rest-versus-movement choice in [recovery day](/en/knowledge/recovery-day/), and sleep as the base variable in [sleep and recovery](/en/knowledge/sleep-recovery/).

## Criteria for returning to running

Walking is normal, pain has settled, sleep and energy are back, and an easy run feels easy. No quality session or long run immediately after the race.

## Sleep, appetite and the first two weeks

Sleep is usually the first thing to improve and the last thing runners protect. In the week after a marathon or ultra, aim for a regular bedtime rather than a fixed number of hours, and treat an unusually deep or long sleep as a normal response rather than a sign of illness. Appetite often lags for a day or two before returning strongly; if you cannot eat much immediately, prioritise fluids and small carbohydrate portions, then rebuild normal meals as hunger returns.

Between weeks one and two, the useful question is not "can I run?" but "is my normal walking, stair climbing and sleep back to baseline?" If stairs still feel awkward, the tissues are not ready for impact loading. A short easy jog that leaves you no more tired the next day is acceptable for some runners; a session that changes your gait is not.

## Common misconceptions

- A “flush-out” run speeds recovery. It does not clear soreness and may add muscle damage.
- The calendar is the standard. Returning 2 weeks after a marathon suits some and is far too soon for others.
- Protein alone is enough. When carbohydrate is short, protein is used for energy.
- Heavy drinking on the celebration night. Alcohol affects sleep and recovery.
- Adding volume the moment pain disappears. The next-day response and normal gait still need checking.
- Returning to intensity within a week. Quality work and the long run wait until walking, sleep and easy running are all back to baseline.

## When to stop and seek care

Seek care for sharp pain, marked swelling, inability to bear weight, a locked joint, numbness or weakness, fever, chest pain, fainting or unusual palpitations. After a head impact, worsening headache, vomiting, confusion or drowsiness is an emergency. The NHS advises not running while in pain and seeking advice when pain is severe, persistent or accompanied by swelling. Persistent insomnia, low mood or fatigue lasting weeks also warrants professional assessment.

## Frequency, review and progression

Assess weekly for 3 weeks: check pain, sleep, walking and how easy runs feel, deciding progression every 2 weeks. A stage must be normal for 2 consecutive weeks; any worsening steps back. If easy running is still impossible at 4 weeks, consult a doctor or physiotherapist.

## Post-race checklist

- Fluid, food and sleep within 24 hours; no long drive.
- Log pain, swelling and sleep daily.
- Calculate protein by body weight and spread it over 3 to 4 meals.
- Confirm normal walking and restored sleep before the first recovery run.

## Scope and limits

No individual rehabilitation plan, diagnosis or medication advice. Medical conditions, pregnancy, previous injury or persistent symptoms warrant a doctor or physiotherapist.

## Recovery by distance: 5K to 100 miles

Recovery is not “take a few days”. Distance, climb, descent, weather and age all change the timeline. The table is a conservative starting point, not a medical prescription.

| Race | Full running volume | Quality sessions | Next race | Key risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5K/10K | 3–7 days | 5–7 days | 2–3 weeks | Going hard because you “feel fine” |
| Half | 7–10 days | 10–14 days | 3–4 weeks | Iron and sleep debt |
| Marathon | 14–21 days | 14–21 days | 4–6 weeks | Muscle damage, immunity |
| 50K | 21–28 days | 21–28 days | 5–8 weeks | Quads from descent, feet |
| 100K | 28–42 days | 28–42 days | 8–12 weeks | Sleep, appetite, anaemia |
| 100 mi | 42–56 days | 6–8 weeks | 10–16 weeks | Persistent fatigue, infection, mood |

Add 1–2 weeks if you are over 50, under-trained, raced in extreme weather or finished injured. If you trained well and finished comfortably, use the lower end but still pass the gates below.

## 0–72 hours: handle the basics

| Time | Focus | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–2 h | Fluid, carbohydrate, some protein; dry clothes | Deep massage, hot bath |
| 2–24 h | Normal meals, sleep; walking for circulation | Alcohol, sun exposure, long standing |
| 24–72 h | Easy walking, sleep, watch urine and swelling | “Flush” runs, quality, long stretching |

Deep massage or a hot bath immediately after a race can worsen inflammation and hypotension; hydrate, warm up and eat first. Chest pain, breathing difficulty, altered consciousness, inability to urinate, severe swelling or fever is an emergency.

## Week 1 and weeks 2–4

**Week 1:** walking and very easy jogging only, no more than 30–40% of pre-race weekly time; no quality, climbing or long run. Record morning heart rate, sleep, pain and appetite.

**Weeks 2–4:** add 10–20% weekly, frequency first, then duration, then speed. Quality waits until three things are true: easy running does not flare within 24 hours, sleep is stable, and morning heart rate is back to baseline. Heavy legs in week 3 after a marathon are not a character flaw; they are insufficient recovery.

## Nutrition, sleep and strength

- **Carbohydrate:** prioritise glycogen replacement in the first 24 hours; small frequent meals beat one huge meal.
- **Protein:** 20–30 g per meal across 3–4 meals; powder is not required.
- **Iron and blood markers:** persistent fatigue, palpitations or cold intolerance after an ultra merits a blood count and iron studies, not self-prescribed high-dose iron.
- **Sleep:** add 30–60 minutes for 1–2 weeks; sleep loss hides recovery signals.
- **Strength:** start light isometrics and single-leg stability in week 2; no heavy squats until pain has settled.

## Two worked examples

**3:30 marathon, age 35, well trained.** Days 0–3 walking and sleep; days 4–7 easy 20–30 minutes; week 2 40–50 km; week 3 55–60 km; week 4 one 4×1 km threshold if 24-hour response is clean; week 5 half marathon; week 6 a 10K race.

**100K, age 48, 14-hour finish.** Week 1 walking and sleep; week 2 walk-run 30–40 minutes; weeks 3–4 easy 30–50 km; week 5 one 60-minute easy run; week 6 only consider short threshold. If appetite or blood markers are still abnormal in week 3, seek care before training.

## Three gates before quality

1. Easy 45–60 minutes with pain ≤1/10 at 24 hours.
2. Seven consecutive days of stable sleep and morning heart rate.
3. Single-leg calf raise, bridge and bodyweight squat symmetrical on both sides.

All three: add the first threshold. Only two: keep easy running.
