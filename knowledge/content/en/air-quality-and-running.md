## The short answer

Air quality is not a binary "run / do not run" switch. It behaves more like a dose: on the same route, a longer or harder run means more pollutants inhaled. The decision needs three inputs — **the AQI band, whether you belong to a sensitive group, and whether the session can move in time or indoors**.

This page uses the US AirNow six-band AQI scale and the WHO 2021 air quality guideline values. AQI is a public-communication index, not a pollutant concentration; countries set their own bands. For a race abroad, follow the local environment or health agency.

## The six AQI bands and what to do

The table below summarises AirNow's public advice; the last column is a running-specific translation, not an additional official rule.

| AQI | Official band | Official advice | What it means for a run |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 0–50 | Good | No action required; "a good day for outdoor exercise" | Run as planned |
| 51–100 | Moderate | People unusually sensitive to air pollution should reduce prolonged or heavy exertion outdoors | Most people run normally; known sensitive individuals reduce intensity or duration |
| 101–150 | Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups | It is still OK to exercise outdoors, but sensitive groups should reduce prolonged or heavy exertion, for example walk instead of run or exercise when air quality is better | Sensitive groups walk, shorten, reduce intensity or move indoors; others may run but avoid long high-intensity work |
| 151–200 | Unhealthy | Sensitive groups should avoid prolonged or heavy exertion outdoors; everyone should reduce outdoor exertion | Everyone reduces intensity and duration; sensitive groups move indoors or reschedule |
| 201–300 | Very Unhealthy | Sensitive groups should avoid all outdoor exertion; everyone else should limit it | No outdoor running; healthy people limit activity to light effort |
| 301–500 | Hazardous | Health warnings of emergency conditions | No outdoor exercise |

"Sensitive groups" is an official risk grouping, not a self-assessment. AirNow lists active children and adults and people with lung disease as ozone-sensitive groups, and people with heart or lung disease, older adults and children as particle-sensitive groups. If you have asthma, COPD, coronary disease or heart failure, or take relevant medication, treat yourself as one band more conservative.

## Why running inhales more than walking

Running is not "the same air in a different posture". Exercise raises ventilation, so the volume of air entering the respiratory tract per minute rises sharply. At higher ventilation, a larger proportion of air bypasses the nasal passages, which normally provide some filtration and conditioning. The review also notes that exercise-related changes in nasal resistance can let particles reach the lower respiratory tract.

That does not make running harmful by definition. It means that **at the same AQI, running produces a higher exposure dose than walking**. This is why AirNow suggests walking instead of running at 101–150, and a gym session at 151–200. "I feel fine" is not a safe criterion, because it underestimates the dose.

WHO's 2021 guidelines set the annual PM2.5 guideline level at 5 µg/m³ and the 24-hour level at 15 µg/m³; for PM10 the values are 15 µg/m³ annual and 45 µg/m³ 24-hour. PM2.5 is fine particulate matter, and the guideline states that air pollution increases morbidity and mortality from cardiovascular and respiratory disease. Keep the distinction clear: **WHO guideline values are health-based targets, while AQI bands are a local communication tool** — they are not the same ruler. In 2019, 99% of the world's population lived where WHO guideline levels were not met; outdoor air pollution was estimated to cause 4.2 million premature deaths that year, with 68% attributed to ischaemic heart disease and stroke.

## A decision order for real sessions

### Everyday training

1. **Check the AQI for the hour you will run**, not yesterday's number. Ozone and particles behave differently: ozone is usually lower in the morning and rises with sunlight and heat, while particles respond to traffic, heating, dust and inversions.
2. **Move the time if you can.** On high-ozone days, the AirNow school guidance explicitly recommends scheduling outdoor activity in the morning. Commute peaks, the downwind side of arterial roads and construction sites are usually poor choices.
3. **Change the intensity if you can.** Turning intervals into an easy run, or 90 minutes into 40, follows the dose logic better than forcing the session in a mask.
4. **Move indoors if you can.** At 151–200 the official advice for sensitive groups is indoors; at 201–300 everyone should avoid outdoor exertion.

### Wildfire smoke

Wildfire smoke is not ordinary smog. EPA's wildfire smoke guidance is direct: **if it looks or smells smoky outside, try to stay indoors or find a cleaner-air shelter; avoid strenuous activities such as going for a run**. Coughing, wheezing or shortness of breath is itself a signal to reduce exposure immediately.

There is no universal "wait for the wind" formula. Verifiable practice is to check local AirNow/NowCast reporting and follow health-agency activity restrictions; indoor air can be improved with a certified air cleaner, but equipment selection and indoor ventilation are beyond this page. If a health warning has been issued, the training plan is not an exception.

### Race day

When a race cannot move, the priority order is **shorten exposure > lower intensity > change route**. Check monitors near the course, not just the city average; loops along arterial roads, tunnel mouths and industrial zones are usually worse. If the organiser changes waves, cut-offs or cancels the race because of air quality, follow the organiser.

## When to stop and seek care

These are not "push through" situations:

- **Chest pain or pressure** during or after exercise, or pain radiating to the arm, jaw or back;
- **Severe breathlessness**, inability to speak a full sentence, or blue lips or nail beds;
- **Fainting or near-fainting**;
- For asthma, **no improvement after using the reliever medicine in your written action plan**, or needing it more often than usual;
- Cough, wheeze or chest pain that keeps worsening after leaving the polluted environment.

Stop exercising and seek emergency care. This page does not diagnose asthma, COPD, coronary disease or any other condition, and gives no medication plan. People with respiratory or cardiac disease should set an exercise and medication plan with a clinician, with air quality as one of the triggers for adjusting it.

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | More accurate statement |
|---|---|
| "Under AQI 100 is always safe" | 51–100 is runnable for most people, but unusually sensitive individuals should still reduce prolonged or heavy exertion |
| "A mask makes a normal run fine" | Exercise raises ventilation and breathing resistance; fit and moisture affect real-world protection. This page does not treat masks as an exercise solution |
| "Cloudy or cool means clean air" | Ozone peaks with heat and sunlight, and particles can accumulate under cloud; how the weather feels is not an air-quality measurement |
| "Indoors is always clean" | Indoor air depends on ventilation, filtration and indoor sources; a room facing a busy road or a underground car park is not a shelter |
| "Training adapts you to pollution" | Fitness does not change the dose relationship between pollutants and cardiovascular or respiratory risk |

## What this page cannot answer

- The concentration at your home or on your course: check the local monitoring station; this page has no live data;
- Your personal susceptibility: age, underlying disease, medication, pregnancy and previous reactions all change risk;
- Which mask or air cleaner suits exercise: that is equipment selection and indoor environment, outside this page;
- Your long-term health outcome: the evidence is population-level association, not an individual prediction.

## Review rhythm: before every session and weekly notes

Air quality changes by the hour, so **re-check the live AQI before every session** rather than reading it once in the morning; dust, wildfire smoke and rush hour can move the same location's value within hours.

Keep a weekly note of three things through a training block: cough, eye or nose irritation, and earlier-than-usual fatigue. If the same symptom worsens for two consecutive weeks, cut outdoor intensity first and get medical review, instead of attributing it to "bad form lately". Write the indoor alternative into the week's plan in advance, so it actually happens when AQI rises.
