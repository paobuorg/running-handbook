## Definitions and scope

This page covers **lactate threshold and VO₂max testing** in running: which methods exist, how large their errors are, and whether the resulting number can set training paces.

Assumptions: you already train regularly and want to decide whether to test, whether your watch's threshold estimate is trustworthy, or where a coach's threshold pace comes from. If you are just starting, have no race goal, and simply want to run consistently, the likely conclusion is that you do not need a test yet. If you have cardiovascular, metabolic or respiratory disease, or have had chest pain or fainting during exercise, a clinician should set the test protocol.

**Test results cannot replace individual coaching.** The same threshold number means a different load for runners with different training histories.

## The conclusion first: a threshold is not one number

In everyday language, "lactate threshold" sounds like a single point. In the research literature it refers to at least four different constructs:

| Concept | Definition | Typical use | Main problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lactate threshold / LT1 | The lowest intensity at which blood lactate rises persistently above baseline | Aerobic ceiling; boundary between easy and steady running | Rules for detecting the first rise are not standardised |
| OBLA (4 mmol/L) | The intensity at which blood lactate reaches a fixed 4 mmol/L | Traditional threshold pace; textbook and lab reports | 4 mmol/L is a group average, not an individual threshold |
| MLSS (maximal lactate steady state) | The highest intensity at which lactate rises by no more than 1 mmol/L during a 30-minute constant run | Research reference for the sustainable ceiling | Requires repeated testing and is costly |
| Ventilatory threshold (VT1/VT2) | The intensity at which gas exchange changes non-linearly | Cardiopulmonary exercise testing; no blood sampling | Related to, but not identical with, lactate threshold |

Faude et al. (2009) reviewed the different lactate threshold concepts and concluded that they do not describe the same physiological intensity and cannot be used interchangeably. In other words, **"what is my threshold?" first requires the question "which threshold do you mean?"**

## Why the definitions disagree: three hard facts

**First, 4 mmol/L is a historical average, not an individual truth.** MLSS was once assumed to occur at 2.2 mmol/L or, more commonly, 4 mmol/L. Later work found large individual variation: reported blood lactate at MLSS ranges from about **1.5 mmol/L to almost 7 mmol/L**, with a mean of about **3.7 mmol/L** (Goodwin 2007). Treating 4 mmol/L as everyone's threshold substitutes a group average for an individual value.

**Second, test duration changes the result.** MLSS depends on the duration of the constant-load bout: 30-minute tests produce lower MLSS power outputs than 20-minute tests (Jones 2019). The same runner can get a different threshold pace from a different protocol.

**Third, the gap between a fixed threshold and MLSS can be quantified.** Wahl et al. (2021) compared the reverse lactate threshold (RLT) with MLSS in 16 participants. RLT speed differed from MLSS speed by **0.06 ± 0.05 m/s** (ICC 0.98), while the fixed 4 mmol/L OBLA differed by **0.13 ± 0.23 m/s** (ICC 0.83). The OBLA error was roughly twice as large, with much wider individual variation.

## Laboratory methods: blood sampling and gas analysis

**Incremental blood lactate testing** is the most common laboratory approach: treadmill or cycle ergometer stages increase in intensity, a fingertip blood sample is taken at the end of each stage, and the lactate-speed curve is used to identify a threshold by a fixed value, a breakpoint, or an individual method.

**MLSS testing** is the research reference method: typically **four to five 30-minute constant-speed runs** on separate days, with MLSS defined as the highest speed at which blood lactate rises by no more than 1 mmol/L in the final 20 minutes (Jones 2019). It is closer to a "sustainable ceiling", but it is time-consuming and rarely done by recreational runners.

Laboratory error comes not only from the decision rule but also from sampling timing, the analyser, day-to-day condition and pace control. **No public source gives a single universal "laboratory error percentage"**; what is clear is that changing the protocol systematically changes the result under the same definition.

## Field methods: 30-minute time trial and race-time conversion

**The 30-minute time trial** has the most direct field evidence. McGehee et al. (2005) compared four field methods with laboratory lactate threshold testing in **27** runners and triathletes with at least three years of competitive experience. The 30-minute trial and the VDOT method were as accurate as the laboratory assessment for estimating **speed at lactate threshold**, and the 30-minute trial also provided a usable **threshold heart rate**.

Practical conditions:
- Use a flat route with minimal interruptions, with adequate warm-up and cool-down.
- The 30 minutes is a **maximal sustainable** effort, not an easy run.
- Use average pace as an upper reference "near threshold", not a second-by-second target.
- Weather, terrain and fatigue all change the result; a few tens of seconds of difference between two tests in the same runner is normal.

**Race-time conversion** (VDOT, Riegel) uses a recent race to estimate equivalent performance and then maps that to training intensities. Its error comes from differences in race distance and goal distance, course and weather, and the fact that a recent race may not reflect current fitness. These methods are starting points, not precise prescriptions.

## Ventilatory threshold: no blood, but not lactate threshold

Ventilatory threshold is identified from gas exchange: ventilation, carbon dioxide output and oxygen uptake change non-linearly at VT1 or VT2. It needs no blood sample and is useful in cardiopulmonary exercise testing, but it measures the **respiratory response**, not blood lactate.

Loat and Rhodes (1993) reviewed the relationship and concluded that it is not settled; the two thresholds measure different physiological signals and cannot be treated as equivalent. They often occur at similar intensities, but similar is not identical, and one cannot be converted into the other.

## VO₂max: laboratory standard and field estimate

**Laboratory VO₂max** uses incremental exercise with gas analysis and looks for the point at which oxygen uptake stops rising with workload. The problem is that a plateau does not always appear: Poole and Jones (2017) note that at least **40%** of healthy subjects show no plateau even when VO₂max is genuinely reached, and this is more common in children, older adults, very unfit people and patients with cardiovascular or pulmonary disease. Secondary criteria such as respiratory exchange ratio, age-predicted maximal heart rate and blood lactate are unreliable, so when no plateau appears the appropriate step is a verification phase, not automatic acceptance of the peak value.

**Field estimation** is best known through the Cooper 12-minute run: the original 1968 study included **115** US Air Force men and reported a correlation of **0.897** between 12-minute distance and laboratory VO₂max. That shows real value, but also that the test explains only part of the variance; the sample was young men, so the equation cannot be transferred unchanged to everyone.

## Wearables: useful as a reference, not as a laboratory result

Lu et al. (2025) had **100** healthy recreational runners complete an indoor incremental test and then an outdoor test guided by their watch:

| Device | Success rate for a single test | Threshold HR MAPE | Threshold pace MAPE |
|---|---|---|---|
| Huawei | 78% | 6.32% | 12.70% |
| Garmin | 65.22% | 7.15% | 25.78% |
| Coros | 47.06% | 5.95% | 22.63% |

All three devices **overestimated threshold pace**; the Huawei and Garmin differences were significant, while Coros had p = 0.08, so they should not be lumped together as "all significantly overestimated". Correlations for threshold heart rate were only **r = 0.13–0.67**, with R² of **0.02–0.45**. In other words, watch-derived threshold heart rate is at best weakly to moderately correlated with laboratory values, and threshold pace can be off by roughly a quarter.

A 2025 systematic review included **13** studies: **7** found wearable VO₂max estimates valid or acceptable, and **3** supported valid lactate threshold estimation. The authors explicitly question usefulness in elite endurance sport. **Models and algorithms not covered by these studies have no public validation data and are unknown.**

## Should recreational runners test?

| Your goal | Recommendation | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Just starting, goal is to finish | Do not test yet | Build consistent volume and routine first; the number has no practical use |
| Want to run more steadily and avoid one intensity for everything | Use a 30-minute trial or a recent race once as a starting point | Low cost, enough to separate easy, moderate and hard |
| Have a specific race goal and need threshold sessions | Testing is reasonable, but fixed 4 mmol/L is not an individual threshold | Calibrate with MLSS or a 30-minute trial and record conditions |
| Want watch-derived thresholds to prescribe training | Use only as a reference | Threshold pace error is 12.70%–25.78%, too large for second-by-second targets |
| Have a disease, take medication, or feel unwell during exercise | Seek medical assessment first | Maximal testing may provoke risk |

## Using the result: relative guidance only

1. **Identify which threshold you measured.** LT1, OBLA, MLSS and VT2 correspond to different intensities.
2. **Use 30-minute trial average pace as the upper reference "near threshold".** Threshold sessions are usually slightly slower than that pace, not equal to or faster than it.
3. **Use relative zones, not a stopwatch.** Easy running is clearly below threshold, steady running is close to but below it, and intervals are above it; see [training pace zones](https://learn.paobu.org/en/articles/training-pace-zones/).
4. **Compare like with like.** Numbers from a different device, protocol or route are not directly comparable.
5. **Check against race performance.** If the threshold-derived race time differs substantially from a recent real race, trust the race and adjust the threshold.
6. **Retest every 6–12 weeks.** More frequent testing turns normal day-to-day variation into apparent progress.

## Clear statement: numbers from different definitions cannot be swapped

- OBLA at 4 mmol/L is not individual MLSS; in the study cited here the speeds differed by 0.13 m/s on average, with larger individual differences.
- Lactate threshold is not ventilatory threshold; they are related but measure different signals.
- 30-minute trial average pace is not laboratory MLSS speed; it is an estimate, not a gold standard.
- A watch's "threshold pace" is none of the above; it is an algorithmic inference.
- Numbers from different devices, protocols or sampling intervals cannot be placed in the same pace table.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The threshold is one number."** There are at least four definitions; ask which one.
- **"4 mmol/L is my threshold."** It is a group average; individual MLSS blood lactate ranges from about 1.5 to almost 7 mmol/L.
- **"My watch says X, so I train at X."** Threshold pace error can be 12.70%–25.78%, and devices tend to overestimate pace.
- **"No plateau means I did not try hard enough."** At least 40% of healthy people show no plateau even at true VO₂max.
- **"The Cooper test gives an exact VO₂max."** The original correlation was 0.897, explaining only part of the variance, in young men.
- **"One test lasts a year."** Fitness changes; retest every 6–12 weeks under similar conditions.
- **"Thresholds from different devices are comparable."** Different algorithms and definitions make the numbers non-interchangeable.

## Checklist

- [ ] Do I know which definition my report uses?
- [ ] Did I record the protocol, device, route and weather?
- [ ] Am I using a recent result rather than a personal best from six months ago?
- [ ] Am I treating threshold pace as a range, not an exact target?
- [ ] Did I avoid hard training in the 48 hours before the test?
- [ ] Will I check the threshold estimate against race performance?

## Limits of this page

This is exercise-science education, not a diagnosis or individual prescription. Before maximal testing, anyone with cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, metabolic disease, recent infection or fever should seek **medical assessment**. **Stop** and seek emergency help if chest pain, chest tightness, fainting, unusual palpitations, severe breathlessness or confusion occurs during a test. Results are for training-intensity reference only and cannot diagnose disease; whether and how to test should be decided with a qualified coach or clinician who understands your **risk** factors.
