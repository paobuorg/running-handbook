## Definitions and scope

This page covers how the foot first contacts the ground: usually classified as **rearfoot, midfoot or forefoot**, or dichotomised as rearfoot versus non-rearfoot. It does not argue for a correct style. It separates three questions: what the actual distribution is, whether the injury evidence agrees, and what happens when a runner changes pattern.

Assumptions: you already run regularly, you are wondering whether to change your landing pattern, or you are confused by claims that "forefoot is professional" and "heel striking ruins your knees". If you have active Achilles, plantar, shin or knee pain, deal with it through the [return-to-running](https://learn.paobu.org/en/articles/return-to-running/) and medical pathway first; changing your gait is not a substitute for diagnosis.

**Foot strike is not a treatment.** This page cannot replace an individual movement assessment, footwear assessment or medical diagnosis.

## Three measurement approaches, often mixed up

Before comparing numbers, check which measurement a study used:

| Approach | How it is judged | Common problem |
|---|---|---|
| Three categories | High-speed video of the first contact: rearfoot, midfoot, forefoot | Rater disagreement; borderline landings are hard to classify |
| Two categories | Rearfoot versus non-rearfoot only | Midfoot and forefoot are merged, hiding differences |
| Continuous measures | Contact angle, ankle dorsiflexion angle, strike index | Different thresholds between studies make pooling difficult |

A systematic review included 13 studies: six used categorical classification and seven used continuous measures. The split in methods is one reason the findings cannot simply be added together.

## Prevalence: rearfoot striking is the majority, including among elites

The most commonly missed fact is that **rearfoot striking is not an amateur flaw; it is also the majority pattern in elite fields.**

| Population and setting | Rearfoot share | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Long-distance road running, early in races | 79% | Bovalino 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis |
| Same, as distance increases | 86% | Same |
| Elite half marathon at 15 km (283 analysable) | 74.9% (midfoot 23.7%, forefoot 1.4%) | Hasegawa 2007 |
| 2017 World Championships women's marathon, lap 4 (78 finishers) | 73% (midfoot 24%, forefoot 3%) | Hanley 2019 |
| 2011 Milwaukee Marathon at 8.1 km (1,991 classified) | 93.67% (n = 1,865) | Kasmer 2013 |

The difference between elite and recreational runners is one of **proportion**, not "elites forefoot, amateurs rearfoot". Within one race, Kasmer 2013 found that faster performers were less likely to heel strike (Kruskal-Wallis, p < .0001), but the design was cross-sectional and cannot show that switching to forefoot makes anyone faster.

## Does speed change the pattern?

The common assumption is that running faster naturally produces a forefoot strike. The data do not support that as a general rule.

- **Patterns do change during races, but mostly towards rearfoot.** In Bovalino 2021, **11%** of runners changed pattern as distance increased, and most changed from non-rearfoot to rearfoot — consistent with reverting to a more economical pattern when fatigued.
- **In shoes, speed barely matters.** Lai 2020 had 20 regular male runners run from slow to fast on a treadmill; shod, **95%** used a rearfoot strike, falling by only 5% at the fastest speed.
- **Barefoot, rearfoot share is much lower but unrelated to speed.** In the same study only **23.8%** of barefoot landings were rearfoot, and the distribution did not change significantly with speed (chi-square = 2.26, p = 0.89). Strike distribution was significantly associated with shoe condition (chi-square = 99.07, p < 0.01).

A more accurate statement: **foot strike is associated more strongly with whether shoes are worn and how they are built than with speed.** This is a contingency-table association, not evidence that changing shoes directly changes landing pattern; mechanisms and individual variation need separate study. See [running shoes](https://learn.paobu.org/en/articles/running-shoes/).

## Injury relationship: the findings disagree, so show both columns

This is where caution matters most. Only by placing "rearfoot is riskier" and "not supported" side by side does the shape of the evidence become visible.

| Pointing to more injury with rearfoot | Pointing to inconsistency or the opposite |
|---|---|
| In Burke 2021, **3 of 6** categorical studies found rearfoot strikers had a significantly higher retrospective injury rate | In the same review, **1** study found greater risk with midfoot/forefoot striking |
| Some retrospective studies link rearfoot striking with knee problems | Of 7 studies using continuous measures, only **1** reported a significant relationship with injury |
| — | The Burke 2021 GRADE table rated the overall evidence **very low** (the structured abstract says low): retrospective designs, small samples, crude strike classification |
| — | Alexander 2021 notes there is **no prospective evidence** that any pattern increases or decreases future injury |

The key distinction is **retrospective versus prospective**. Retrospective studies ask how injured runners used to land; they cannot answer whether changing pattern prevents injury. No public source gives a consistent figure for how much injury risk falls after switching to forefoot, so that number is **unknown**.

If you take one sentence: **current evidence is insufficient to label any foot strike pattern as an injury risk factor, let alone to use it as prevention.**

## What changes when you switch: load transfer, not load removal

Switching to forefoot does not remove impact; it relocates it. Alexander 2021's BJSM infographic summarises this directly:

| Region | Load change after switching to forefoot |
|---|---|
| Knee | Load decreases |
| Calf (ankle plantarflexors) | Load increases |
| Ankle and Achilles tendon | Load increases |
| Foot | Load increases |

The same material notes that habitual rearfoot and habitual non-rearfoot runners show **no difference in running economy**, and that the overwhelming majority of recreational shod runners already rearfoot strike.

The benefit of changing is therefore "load a different structure", and the cost is that tissues which were not previously loaded that way must adapt. For someone with existing Achilles or plantar problems, the direction may be exactly wrong — see [Achilles tendon pain](https://learn.paobu.org/en/articles/achilles-tendon-pain/).

## How long adaptation takes

Public sources offer only small, short studies, each with clear limits:

- **12 weeks of gait retraining plus minimalist shoes**: Yang 2020 randomised 30 men, of whom 17 completed. In the combined group, **78% (7/9)** of rearfoot strikers converted to forefoot; loading rate fell, but **ankle joint moment rose**.
- **12 weeks, three sessions per week, five-finger minimalist shoes plus forefoot strike**: Zhang 2024 randomised 30 men, of whom 28 completed; **57% (8/14)** converted. Achilles tendon length and cross-sectional area increased significantly, and Achilles loading rate and peak force changed over time. The authors call this preliminary evidence.

Both studies involved only **habitually shod male recreational runners**, with small samples and a fixed 12-week window. Neither answers how long full adaptation takes, nor how much running volume is safe during it. **No public source sets a universal adaptation period**, so claims that "two weeks is enough" have no basis.

One easily missed signal: 13 of 30 participants in Yang 2020 did not complete the 12 weeks. Dropout itself shows that the execution difficulty of gait change is widely underestimated.

## Injury risk from changing directly: unknown

"How much does injury risk rise if I copy a video and switch to forefoot?" There is **no citable unified number** for this.

Only directional evidence exists: switching to forefoot raises load on the calf, ankle, Achilles and foot (Alexander 2021), and a 12-week progressive programme did show increased ankle joint moment (Yang 2020). Those are **biomechanical measures**, not clinical injury rates. Converting a change in a kinetic variable into "X% more injuries" is over-interpretation.

This page therefore says unknown rather than offering a precise-looking percentage.

## Why gait change often fails

Putting the sections together, failure is usually structural rather than a matter of willpower:

1. **Treating a cross-sectional difference as cause.** Forefoot is more common among elites, but that does not mean forefoot makes elites. Runners fast enough to be in those fields are more likely to land forefoot anyway; Kasmer 2013 and Bovalino 2021 describe associations only.
2. **Changing the movement without changing the load progression.** The 12-week studies converted runners within controlled, progressive programmes. Switching the whole weekly volume to forefoot immediately asks the Achilles and calf to carry a new load before adapting.
3. **Ignoring the direction of load transfer.** Knee load falls; ankle and Achilles load rises. If those are the weak links, gait change amplifies the problem.
4. **Dropout.** The 13/30 dropout in Yang 2020 suggests that sustaining a new pattern is much harder than learning it once; fatigue, hills and the later stages of races bring back the old pattern.

These four points also explain why gait change can look effective in individual cases yet fail to show a stable benefit in population studies.

## Clear conclusion: no evidence supports changing everyone to forefoot

Ranking the evidence by strength:

- **Prevalence**: rearfoot striking is the majority in both recreational and elite fields (74.9%–93.67%), with hard data.
- **Advantage**: Bovalino 2021 finds the competitive advantage of non-rearfoot striking **inconclusive**; Alexander 2021 reports **no running-economy difference** between habitual patterns.
- **Injury**: categorical studies disagree, continuous measures are almost entirely non-significant, overall evidence is **low**, and there is **no prospective evidence**.
- **Changing**: there is load transfer and small-sample 12-week adaptation evidence, but the change in injury rate is unknown.

The conclusion is therefore: **no evidence supports changing every runner to a forefoot strike.** For a runner with no symptoms, stable performances and consistent training, changing landing pattern has no demonstrable benefit and a clear adaptation cost. Individual change only makes sense when a qualified professional proposes it for a specific problem, such as recurrent load issues at one site.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Forefoot is what professionals do"**: 73% of women's World Championships marathon finishers still rearfoot struck in lap 4, and 74.9% of an elite half-marathon field rearfoot struck at 15 km.
- **"Rearfoot striking always damages the knee"**: knee load is indeed lower, but the evidence is graded low, and no prospective study shows that changing prevents injury.
- **"Running faster makes you forefoot"**: 95% of shod runners rearfoot struck at every speed, falling only 5% at the fastest.
- **"Barefoot running fixes it"**: barefoot rearfoot share is lower (23.8%), but that comes from the shoe change, not from speed or intent.
- **"Changing has no cost"**: the cost is moving load from the knee to the ankle, Achilles and foot.
- **"Two weeks is enough"**: no public source sets a universal adaptation period; existing interventions use a 12-week window.
- **"It worked for one person, so it generalises"**: individual experience is not population evidence, and this page does not cite case reports.

## Checklist

- [ ] Do I know my current landing pattern, checked in easy runs, when fatigued and on hills?
- [ ] Do I have active Achilles, plantar, shin or knee pain?
- [ ] Am I considering a change because of a specific problem, or because forefoot "sounds more professional"?
- [ ] Do I understand that knee load falls while ankle and Achilles load rise?
- [ ] Am I prepared for at least 12 weeks of gradual work rather than switching everything at once?
- [ ] Am I recording pain location and the 24-hour response before and after?

## Limits of this page

This is exercise-science education, not a diagnosis or individual prescription. **Stop the relevant training and seek medical care** if pain persists beyond 1–2 weeks without improvement, worsens daily, or is accompanied by swelling, night pain, limping, restricted joint movement, numbness, weakness or radiating symptoms. Chest pain, fainting or severe breathlessness are emergencies: stop and seek urgent help. Runners with a history of Achilles, plantar fascia or stress fracture problems should obtain a qualified assessment before changing landing pattern.
