## Conclusion: what to do first

The most common mistake in preparing a trail ultra (100K or 100 miles) is extending a road marathon mileage plan straight upward. The limiting factors are not repeated flat impact: they are muscular work on climbs, eccentric load on descents, tolerance for hours on foot, judgment at night, fueling rhythm while carrying a pack, and recovery across consecutive long days. The main line of the plan is an aerobic base on roads and trails, one weekly climbing or downhill technique session, weekend long blocks that grow as single runs or back-to-back pairs, rehearsed night, load and fueling practice, and a taper that cuts volume while keeping stimulus.

Three actions first. First, get the race's segment elevation profile and compute total climbing, longest continuous climb and longest continuous descent, then map those numbers onto training sessions. Second, rotate weekend blocks between sustained running, climbing sessions and back-to-back pairs rather than adding flat mileage alone. Third, rehearse the mandatory kit, bladder, food and lighting on training runs; do not try a new combination on race day. With persistent pain, worsening sleep, marked fatigue, or weather and terrain risk beyond your preparation, return to the last stable block rather than pushing the schedule.

This page is training education, not a personal prescription. Altitude, extreme weather, prior injury or cardiovascular and metabolic conditions should be assessed by a clinician or qualified coach.

## Who this is for, and who it is not for

**For you if** you already hold a stable road or trail base with mileage and sustained-run tolerance at a reasonable level, and your target is a trail race of 50K or longer; you have finished a half or full road marathon and want to move into trail ultras; or you coach athletes and need an ultra periodization framework.

**Not for you if** you cannot yet run continuously for 60 minutes or have no trail experience — start with [Hill and mountain path basics](/en/knowledge/hill-and-mountain-path-basics/) and [Ultra-running basics](/en/knowledge/ultra-running-basics/) first. This page does not apply during acute injury, post-surgical rehab, pregnancy, or with cardiac, metabolic or joint conditions, which require clinical assessment first. Finishing a road marathon is often overestimated as sufficient ultra preparation: a flat finish shows your aerobic and flat-pace tolerance reach a certain level, but says nothing about downhill eccentric tolerance, terrain judgment or prolonged fueling adaptation.

## Decision branches: from road running to trail ultra

First identify which specific abilities you still lack relative to the target event, then choose cycle length and block format.

| Current situation | Main gap | Path | Likely cycle |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Road marathon finish, rarely on hills | Climbing work, downhill eccentric load, terrain judgment | Add trail frequency and climbing sessions; start blocks as single trail long runs | 16–24 weeks, starting point |
| Trail long runs but nothing beyond 8 hours | Time-on-foot tolerance, fueling rhythm, night sections | Lengthen weekend blocks; rehearse repeated feeding and night segments | 16–20 weeks |
| Finished 50K/50 miles, moving to 100K | Back-to-back recovery, climbing distribution, kit validation | Add back-to-back blocks and loaded sessions; arrange specificity by race segments | 18–24 weeks |
| Targeting 100 miles | Ultra-long tolerance, sleep-deprivation management, consecutive-day rhythm | Longer block cycle, more cautious taper, review every block | 24+ weeks (editorial example) |

Distance is not the only input. Cumulative climbing, technical difficulty, altitude, weather and cutoff all shape the plan. A 100K with 5,000 m of gain demands more muscular work and downhill load than a 100K with 2,000 m; the plan cannot be arranged by kilometers alone.

## The weekly training template (example)

Distance, time and elevation below are illustrative and adjustable to the race. The trail week also follows hard-day/easy-day: recovery between hard sessions, long blocks on the weekend.

| Day | Session | Intensity | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Rest or easy walk | Very low | Absorb the weekend block |
| Tuesday | Easy trail or road run 50–70 min | Full sentences | Aerobic base |
| Wednesday | One climbing session or downhill technique bouts | Moderately hard | Terrain-specific stimulus |
| Thursday | Rest, strength or [cross-training](/en/knowledge/cross-training-for-runners/) | Low to moderate | Stability and recovery |
| Friday | Easy run 40–60 min | Easy | Maintain mobility |
| Saturday | Weekend block: single long run or back-to-back day one | Easy, slightly slow | Time-on-foot and fueling practice |
| Sunday | Back-to-back day two (optional) or rest | Very low to low | Next-day tolerance or recovery |

Strength work 1–2 times weekly covers hips, glutes, ankles and single-leg stability (see [Strength training for runners](/en/knowledge/strength-training-for-runners/)); trail demands more glute-leg and downhill stability than flat running, and strength sessions should not be forced the day after a block.

## Climbing sessions and downhill eccentric load

**Uphill.** Minetti 2002 tested ten elite mountain runners on a treadmill and found running energy cost rises nonlinearly with gradient, on an asymmetric curve: the added cost uphill exceeds the savings from an equivalent downhill. Fitted level cost was about 3.6 J/kg/m, rising to 18.93 at +45% while dropping only to 3.92 at −45%. The 2017 uphill/downhill review adds that uphill running has higher cadence, shorter swing and aerial phases and a greater duty factor, with more muscular work around the hip. The training implication: climbing is not "jog slowly upward," but a practiced stable walk-run rhythm, hip drive and breathing allocation; using a walking posture on steep grades to save work is a legitimate strategy.

**Downhill.** The same review notes downhill running has longer aerial time, lower cadence and more rear-foot striking, with work dominated by energy dissipation and eccentric contraction. Downhill eccentric load on quadriceps, tendons and joints is the most underestimated ultra variable: road running rarely involves continuous long descents, while a trail race may descend for several kilometers in a row. Training implication: downhill bouts are arranged in segments and progressed gradually, with footstrike choice and single-leg stability worked on; do not hammer long descents while fatigued. Next-day muscle soreness and recovery after a downhill session are key signals of whether the load exceeded tolerance.

The energy-cost curve describes gradient and metabolic cost only; it does not convert flat pace into an undulating-course finish time. Pace on trail is judged segment by segment; see the [Pace tool](/en/tools/pace/) and [Grade-adjusted pace](/en/tools/grade-adjusted-pace/) for estimate ranges.

## Time-on-foot: arranging prolonged standing running

One core ultra constraint is standing and running for hours on end, which differs from a single marathon duration. Time-on-foot training adapts muscle, tendon, stomach, psychology and fueling rhythm to a sustained load rather than simply accumulating kilometers.

Workable rules:

1. **Extend time before piling distance:** schedule blocks by completable duration; pace on trails and undulating ground is naturally slower than on roads. Judge by watch time and subjective tolerance, not road pace.
2. **Rehearse fueling in blocks:** for steady exercise over 60 minutes, carbohydrate intake of about 30–60 g/hour (ISSN) should be practiced; ultras also train carrying and tolerating solid food, water and electrolytes (see [Long-run fueling](/en/knowledge/long-run-fueling/)).
3. **Stomach problems are often under-trained:** nausea and reflux during long runs usually come from an unrehearsed feeding rhythm. Find tolerable foods and frequency in blocks rather than changing the plan on race day.
4. **Read recovery after the block:** Monday and Tuesday response (soreness, easy-run quality, sleep) decides whether next week's block can grow.

## Night, load carrying and back-to-back long sessions

**Night sections.** Many ultras include an early start or after-dark leg. FHWA-cited NHTSA data show nearly half of pedestrian fatalities occur between 6:00 p.m. and midnight; among pedestrians struck at night, eight of ten drivers reported not seeing the person; a pedestrian in dark clothing may first be seen at about 55 feet (roughly 17 m). Trail races have no traffic, but lighting, visibility and fatigued judgment remain critical: training should verify headlamp brightness, spare batteries, reflective markers and route-flag recognition (see [Night running and traffic](/en/knowledge/night-running-and-traffic/)).

**Load carrying.** Pack weight changes posture, sweating and fueling arrangement. Mandatory gear, bladder, food and clothing should be carried at race weight in training; start light and progress toward the race pack rather than first running fully loaded on race day. The NPS Ten Essentials framing: navigation, sun protection, clothing, illumination, first aid, fire, repair kit, food, water and water treatment, shelter; electronic navigation should have a paper backup.

**Back-to-back long sessions.** These simulate consecutive-day recovery, usually two sessions over a weekend with day two shorter than day one. Their value is running and judging in a partially fatigued state, so day two is not about pace; when recovery is insufficient, replace the back-to-back with a single block plus a rest day.

## Race-specificity checklist

Late in the build-up, check item by item whether the target course and your training align. Each row should be answerable "yes" four weeks before the race.

| Check item | What to take from race materials | How training covers it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total climb and longest continuous climb | Segment elevation profile, climbing between aid points | Climbing session duration and grade approach common race segments |
| Longest continuous descent and technical surface | Descent length, surface type | Segmented downhill repeats, footstrike choice practice |
| Night/early-morning section | Start time, daylight on leg | At least one night or low-light block; verify lighting |
| Mandatory kit and pack weight | Event rule list | Train at race pack weight |
| Aid intervals | Station spacing, available food | Blocks fuel at similar intervals; validate stomach tolerance |
| Weather and altitude | Race-period temperature, precipitation, altitude | Adjust intensity in heat or altitude (see [Heat and humidity](/en/knowledge/heat-and-humidity/)) |
| Navigation and marking | Marking style, lost-route risk | Practice map reading and route checks per [Trail navigation](/en/knowledge/trail-navigation/) |
| Exit and comms | Cutoff points, rescue method, signal coverage | Make a personal Plan B; leave your itinerary with someone |
| Back-to-back/ultra-long tolerance | Estimated finish time | Back-to-back blocks or long single runs |
| Kit break-in | Shoes, socks, bladder, clothing | All worn in blocks; no race-day replacement |

NPS mountain safety guidance: leave a trip plan with a trusted person (route, contacts, arrival and return times, companions); mobile coverage may be absent, consider a locator beacon and conserve battery; check weather, prepare for lightning and flash flood, keep a Plan B; turn back before water is half gone; GPS can be unreliable, so carry alternative navigation.

## Evidence and conflicts

### What the research supports

- **Asymmetric slope energy cost:** Minetti 2002 measured metabolic cost differences across level, uphill and downhill grades; uphill work cost exceeds the downhill saving.
- **Different movement patterns uphill and downhill:** the 2017 review summarizes cadence, aerial phase, footstrike and muscle-work direction differences.
- **Prolonged exercise fueling:** the ISSN position stand gives about 30–60 g/hour carbohydrate beyond 60 minutes, adjusted to individual tolerance.
- **Taper principles:** the 2023 PLOS ONE meta-analysis supports cutting volume, preserving intensity and frequency, within up to 21 days; this comes from endurance exercise generally, not an ultra-specific trial.

### Limited evidence or coaching experience

- **Ultra cycle length, weekly climbing volume, block ratio:** public research gives no uniform prescription for 100K/100-mile runners; the templates here are editorial, adjusted to course, base and recovery.
- **Optimal back-to-back frequency and duration:** practice commonly uses two weekend sessions, but no controlled trial fixes the best combination.
- **Elite trail data:** Haugen 2022 describes road track and marathon elites; its figures cannot transfer directly to mountain ultras. Borrowable is the structure — mostly low intensity, hard-easy days — not absolute mileage.
- **Downhill training dose:** evidence is mostly biomechanical description; no RCT fixes the safest downhill repeat volume. Progress by muscle soreness and the 24-hour response.

### Which trail claims currently lack evidence

- **"Just run downhill and you will adapt":** downhill eccentric load must progress gradually; downhill repeats in a fatigued state are a high-injury scenario.
- **"The more back-to-backs the better":** back-to-backs probe partial-fatigue performance; stacking them when recovery is short only accumulates excess load.
- **"Race pack weight does not matter":** load changes posture and energy cost and must be adapted to in training.

## Worked example: 100K weekend-block progression (assumed)

Example runner: targeting a mountain race around 100K with an estimated 12–16 hours on foot; current longest single run about 3 hours, no current pain. Rule: the block grows one step every two weeks; every fourth week is a light week; back-to-back day two runs about 60% of day one. Figures below are an editorial assumption, not a prescription.

| Week | Weekend block | Day one | Day two | Block total | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Single long run | 2h30 | — | 2h30 | Record baseline |
| 2 | Single long run | 3h00 | — | 3h00 | Easy trail |
| 3 | Single long run | 3h30 | — | 3h30 | Practice fueling |
| 4 | Light week | 2h00 | — | 2h00 | Planned regression |
| 5 | Single long run | 3h30 | — | 3h30 | Stable |
| 6 | Back-to-back | 2h30 | 1h30 | 4h00 | First back-to-back |
| 7 | Single long run | 4h00 | — | 4h00 | Includes climbing |
| 8 | Light week | 2h30 | — | 2h30 | Regression |
| 9 | Back-to-back | 3h00 | 1h50 | 4h50 | Day two tests fatigued judgment |
| 10 | Single long run | 4h30 | — | 4h30 | Load near race pack |
| 11 | Back-to-back | 3h00 | 1h50 | 4h50 | One night section |
| 12 | Light week | 2h30 | — | 2h30 | Pre-taper regression |
| 13 | Single long run | 5h00 | — | 5h00 | Cycle's longest single |
| 14 | Back-to-back | 3h00 | 2h00 | 5h00 | Simulate consecutive effort |
| 15 | Taper | 2h30 | — | 2h30 | Cut volume, keep stimulus |
| 16 | Race week | Short easy activation | — | — | No new kit |

Reading the example: the cycle's longest single run is 5 hours and longest back-to-back block totals 5 hours. For a 100K expected to take 12–16 hours, training blocks cover only part of race duration; the rest depends on race-day rhythm, fueling and segment strategy. This is normal — you do not need to train through the full race distance. If day two of weeks 6, 9, 11 or 14 brings persistent pain or fails to recover fully, step back one level rather than continuing to progress.

## Common failure modes and early signals

1. **Adding flat mileage without climbing or downhill:** legs give out late on race climbs and descents. Early signals: leg soreness appears early on climbs; quadriceps stay sore after descents.
2. **Chasing distance in blocks without fueling practice:** stomach collapse or energy depletion on race day. Early signals: nausea, reflux or energy drop in blocks; find a tolerable plan in training.
3. **Back-to-back on an under-recovered weekend:** technique deforms on day two, eccentric load lands on fatigued tissue. Early signals: shorter stride, heavy footstrike, hesitant descents.
4. **First meeting navigation and night conditions on race day:** low-light misjudgment, difficulty reading flags. Early signals: slowed reaction to markers in training, relying on others to lead.
5. **All kit left for race day:** shoes, socks, bladder and pack cause blisters or pressure sores over long hours. Early signals: rubbed red spots or pressure points after training blocks.
6. **Ignoring accumulated fatigue:** after several long blocks, sleep worsens, resting heart rate rises, easy runs slow. Overtraining syndrome has no single diagnostic marker; first reduce load and exclude illness (see [Load and overtraining](/en/knowledge/load-and-overtraining/)).

## When not to follow this table

| Situation | Action |
| --- | --- |
| Swelling, heat or deformity at a joint or tendon, joint giving way, inability to bear weight | Stop running and seek clinical assessment; do not continue blocks |
| Same-site pain after three consecutive sessions, or pain not back to baseline within 24 hours | Stop progression, return to the previous level, follow the relevant injury page |
| Two consecutive weeks of worse sleep, elevated resting heart rate, plateau or declining performance | Reduce volume to recover, exclude illness, seek professional assessment if needed |
| Heat, thunderstorm, flash flood, very poor visibility | Cancel or move indoors/low impact; do not go uphill under the original plan |
| Terrain beyond current stable ability | First lower technical difficulty; build experience on controllable ground |
| Prior-injury site reacts after blocks | Shorten that session type; adjust with a physiotherapist |

## What would change this advice

- **Course differences:** high mountain rock, fast trail, desert or snow, tropical versus high-altitude races demand different climbing, load, cooling and technique; the plan must be rearranged for the target course.
- **Population and base:** road-to-trail converts, experienced trail runners, older runners and women differ in tolerance and risk background; block progression cannot be copied across groups.
- **Evidence update:** research on ultra periodization, back-to-back and downhill dosing is still developing; this page adjusts if later reviews reach different conclusions.
- **Health status:** joint, tendon, cardiovascular or metabolic conditions should be assessed clinically before arranging blocks and climbing sessions.
- **Goal change:** moving from 50K to 100K to 100 miles raises block time, back-to-back frequency and taper depth; for road marathon preparation see [Marathon training plan](/en/knowledge/marathon-training-plan/).
