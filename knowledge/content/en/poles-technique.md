## Bottom line

Poles suit trail runners and hikers on long outings, sustained climbs, loaded carry or with existing knee load; they are not carried to look professional. Their job is to add support points, shift part of the load to the upper body and improve balance, not to replace strength work. Start from a 90-degree elbow default length, shorten uphill, lengthen downhill, and adjust the two poles separately on traverses. Plant the poles beside and slightly ahead of the body; the wrist strap transfers force rather than a death grip on the handle. Race rules vary widely: some events allow poles but require carrying them the whole race, some signed zones ban them, and other events ban them outright; check the current regulations before entering. One point must be clear: poles do not replace core strength, ankle stability and balance training. They are a load-sharing tool, not a mobility aid, and cannot carry full body weight.

## Definition and preconditions

This page covers a pair of adjustable trail-running or hiking poles; a single walking staff is a different case. Preconditions: the route has sustained rolling terrain or long distance; you have practised planting rhythm on short hills so you will not tangle yourself or others on narrow sections; there is no acute pain in the upper limbs, shoulders, wrists, knees or ankles that day. If any fails, get to know the route without poles first, or try poles only on short gentle sections. Pole actions on variable ground pair with the [technical trail skills](/en/knowledge/technical-trail-skills/) page; uphill and downhill gait and eccentric load are covered in [Uphill and downhill](/en/knowledge/uphill-and-downhill/).

## When poles help: climbs, long distance and knee load

The supporting research was done mostly under hiking conditions, so read the results as lower-limb load sharing, not a race-performance conversion. In a study of eight males walking downhill on a 25-degree ramp, poles significantly reduced peak and average ground reaction force, knee joint moment, and tibiofemoral compression and shear forces by 12-25%; the reduction came primarily from forces applied to the poles and a more forward-leaning posture that shortened the knee moment arm. A second study of 15 experienced male hikers across three pack conditions found that poles significantly reduced sagittal-plane moment at every lower-extremity joint and reduced peak power absorption at ankle and knee, and held across pack loads. On trail: on long climbs, power-hiking poles give a pushing point; late in a long descent with tired quads, poles help share landing impact; runners with existing knee strain or older runners looking to reduce joint pressure have an optional load-sharing tool. On gentle jogging grades, poles interfere with arm swing, and dense technical sections where free hands are needed for balance can make them a liability.

## Sizing: elbow-angle baseline and height factor

Set the default length by elbow angle: grip the handle with the tip on the ground near your feet and the elbow at about 90 degrees; this is the baseline for flat ground and short up- and downhills. A manufacturer's beginner guide gives a factor basis: LEKI estimates beginner pole length at height x 0.7, while stressing that length depends heavily on personal preference and technique, with most professional athletes using longer poles; the 90-degree elbow and the manufacturer's size chart remain the final check. On three-section poles, set the upper adjustment mid-range and the lower section to target length, fine-tuning only the upper section on trail. Early signs of wrong length are shoulder, back, knee or wrist soreness: too long forces shrugging, too short bends the torso to carry weight. Fixed-length poles follow the manufacturer's height chart; do not shorten them yourself.

## Uphill technique

Shorten poles uphill: on long climbs shorten each pole about 5-10 cm; the steeper the slope, the shorter the poles, so the shoulders never feel lifted or pushed into pack straps, and there is no pull-up-like tug on the body. Rhythm is contralateral: left pole with right leg, right pole with left leg; plant beside and ahead of the body, and merge the push into the stepping rhythm. On steep power-hiking sections, plant the pole firmly before loading it; do not place the tip on loose scree or in mid-air. The uphill goal is to share hip and leg work and keep rhythm, not to pull with the arms; if the shoulders and arms tire before the thighs, the poles are too long or the push pattern is wrong.

## Downhill technique

Lengthen poles downhill: about 5-10 cm from the default. Raising the hands keeps you more upright and improves balance, and lets poles, hands and upper body absorb part of the initial landing impact, giving tired knees and legs a break. Plant beside and slightly ahead, probing stability before transferring weight. On technical descents where both hands are needed to grip or dodge obstacles, collapse the poles or carry one; do not let two poles catch at your feet. The knee-force reductions in the studies came from downhill walking, and running descents have a different impact pattern; poles are an auxiliary buffer and do not remove the quadriceps' eccentric braking job.

## Traverse and technical sections

On a long traverse, shorten the uphill-side pole and lengthen the downhill-side pole to stay even on the hillside, adjusting the length difference with the slope. On roots, scree, steps and wet rock, a pole can probe ground first as a third contact point; but where a landing needs both hands to climb over, cross or balance, stow the poles to one side to avoid tip snagging. On narrow trails, in groups, or where signs require stowing poles, follow the signs and regulations.

## Storage, wrist straps and race rules

Thread the wrist strap from below the wrist and rest it on the grip so load travels through the strap to the arm rather than a pinched fist; this saves the wrist over long outings and keeps the pole from flying off if the hand opens. When stowed, collapse and fasten the poles and cover the tips so they do not point at runners or spectators behind you. Race rules follow the current official regulations. UTMB World Series 2027 regulations state that if you choose to carry poles you must carry them for the entire race; it is forbidden to start without poles and retrieve them mid-race, and poles may not go in spare bags. Pole use may be prohibited in certain signed zones for safety or environmental reasons. Picking up poles after the start or abandoning them during the race draws a 1-hour penalty in the 100M races and a 30-minute penalty in the OCC/MCC/ETC races; dangerous behavior such as uncovered tips aimed at runners or spectators draws a further time penalty. Other events may ban poles outright or require them on the mandatory gear list; check the gear list, checkpoint rules and aid-station restrictions before signing up.

## Decision table: scenario, state and pole choice

| Scenario | Poles? | Action points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gentle jogging slope, arm rhythm normal | Usually no | Poles interfere with arm swing and rhythm |
| Sustained long climb, power-hike | Yes, shorten 5-10 cm | Contralateral plant; merge push into climbing rhythm |
| Late-run descent, tired quads | Yes, lengthen 5-10 cm | Poles share initial impact; probe before transferring weight |
| Long traverse | Adjust both poles separately | Short on uphill side, long on downhill side |
| Dense technical terrain, hands needed | Stow poles or use one | Avoid tip snagging; free-hand balance first |
| Signed ban zone or event ban | Stow / do not carry | Follow current regulations and on-course signs |

## What poles do not replace

This point stands on its own. Poles shift part of the load to the upper body and lower lower-limb joint moments, but they do not replace core strength, ankle strength and proprioception training. The trail-running review identifies inadequate neuromotor control, balance and coordination, running through fatigue, and abnormal kinematics on variable terrain as key mechanisms behind injury progression; those are addressed only by strength, balance and skill practice. REI's positioning is explicit: poles are not mobility aids and are not designed to support full body weight like braces, a walker or a medical cane. If you need poles to stand or walk on flat ground, what you need is medical assessment and rehabilitation, not a pair of poles. After repeated sprains, chronic instability or injury, rebuild strength and balance through the rehabilitation progression; poles are only a transitional aid.

## Common misconceptions

- **Longer poles save more effort.** Over-long poles force shrugging and upper-body compensation, tiring the shoulders first; size follows the 90-degree elbow and slope adjustments.
- **Pulling yourself uphill on poles.** Uphill power comes from the hips and legs; poles are a point merged into rhythm, not an arm pull. An arm-dominated pull burns the upper body first.
- **Two poles out on every technical descent.** Dense sections need free hands for balance; poles can snag. Stow them when that is the safer choice.
- **Poles remove the need for strength training.** They share load but do not replace core, ankle stability and proprioception; chronic instability does not heal by carrying poles.
- **Assuming every event allows poles.** Rules differ by event and category; ban zones, drop-bag limits and penalties are checked in the current regulations.

## Failure modes and stop rules

1. **Shoulder or wrist soreness, white-knuckled grip.** Early signs: shrugged shoulders, tight wrists. Stop to adjust length and strap threading.
2. **Tips snagging self or others.** On narrow ground, roots or in a group, if the tips catch, collapse the poles and walk the section.
3. **Relying on poles to bear weight.** If each step loads the poles and the arms carry the main support, core and leg support are insufficient; cut volume and add strength work rather than lengthening the poles.
4. **Acute pain signals.** Persistent knee, ankle, wrist or shoulder pain, or a joint giving way, during pole use: stop the section and assess. After a fall, marked swelling, inability to bear weight or an impact is an acute injury to be assessed medically.

## Scope and limits

The load-reduction data come mainly from downhill and loaded hiking studies, with small male samples; they are not a direct promise of joint protection in trail running. Pole model, length, terrain, weather and individual technique all change the effect; race gear rules follow the current official regulations. Anyone with upper-limb, shoulder, wrist, knee or ankle history or balance issues should consult a coach or clinician before use. This page does not diagnose or prescribe medication.

## What changes this advice

- **Route and terrain.** Long loaded climbs benefit most; gentle technical ground or dense rock may give poles little value or make them a liability.
- **Body state and phase.** Late in a run, at the tail of a long outing, or with existing knee load, the sharing role is clearer; fresh jogging on gentle slopes may not need them.
- **Gear rules.** Events, categories and course sections differ on carrying, stowage and bans; follow the current regulations.
- **Evidence updates.** Pole biomechanics, metabolic cost and gear research keep evolving; this page is updated when higher-quality evidence changes the guidance.
