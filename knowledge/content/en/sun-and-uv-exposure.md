## The UV Index is not the temperature

The UV Index (UVI) measures the intensity of ultraviolet radiation reaching the ground. It is not directly tied to air temperature, and a cool or overcast feel does not lower it. The EPA scale is **1–2 low; 3–7 moderate to high; 8 or higher very high to extreme**. WHO's advice is simpler: **sun protection is recommended when the UV Index reaches 3 or above**.

Running adds three complications: long exposure, sweat that undermines sunscreen, and routes near reflective surfaces. The sections below cover how to read the index, then how to apply it to running.

## Bands and actions

| UV Index | EPA level | Official advice | For a run |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Low | No special protection needed; minimal sun protection is sufficient | Most people can run as planned |
| 3–7 | Moderate to High | Protection needed; seek shade from late morning to mid-afternoon; broad-spectrum SPF-15 or higher, protective clothing, wide-brimmed hat, sunglasses | Long or midday runs should use shaded routes and sunscreen on exposed skin |
| 8+ | Very High to Extreme | Extra protection needed; be especially careful from late morning to mid-afternoon | Avoid midday where possible, shorten exposure, cover more skin |

EPA also offers a field check that needs no index lookup — the **shadow rule**: if your shadow is taller than you (early morning, late afternoon), UV is likely lower; if your shadow is shorter than you (near midday), UV is higher, so seek shade and protect skin and eyes.

One note on numbers: the EPA page states SPF-15 or higher, while dermatology and WHO guidance more commonly state SPF 30 or higher. This page makes no product recommendation; it explains what **broad-spectrum, SPF 30+, water-resistant** labels mean. Choose according to local labelling and your own tolerance.

## UVA, UVB and "you can burn on a cloudy day"

WHO's Q&A gives two key proportions: **UVA accounts for about 95% of UV reaching the Earth's surface**, penetrates deeper into the skin, causes immediate tanning, and is linked to skin ageing and skin cancer risk. **UVB mainly causes delayed tanning and burning** and significantly promotes skin cancer. Most UVB is filtered by the atmosphere, but it is still strongest around summer midday.

At mid-latitudes, UV is highest during the **four-hour period around solar noon in summer**. UV increases by about 10% for every 1,000 m of altitude. Reflective surfaces add exposure: grass, soil and water reflect less than 10%, sand about 15%, sea foam about 25%, and **fresh snow can reflect as much as 80%, almost doubling a person's UV exposure**. This is why snow running and skiing produce sunburn and even snow blindness.

Clouds are the easiest thing to misjudge. WHO warns that **thin or light clouds have little effect and may even enhance UV through scattering**; an overcast day or a cool breeze is not a reason to skip protection. Even long periods in open shade can burn sensitive skin on a high-UV day.

## Practical details for runners

### How much, and when

The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) application guidance is a usable baseline:

- Choose **SPF 30 or higher, broad-spectrum (UVA and UVB), water-resistant**;
- Apply **about 15 minutes before going outdoors** so the skin can absorb it;
- Most adults need about **1 ounce** (roughly a shot glass) for skin not covered by clothing, and **at least 1 teaspoon for the face**;
- **Reapply every 2 hours**, and **immediately after swimming or sweating**;
- Cover the neck, ears, tops of the feet, backs of the legs and hairline; use an SPF 30+ lip balm.

Running magnifies under-application: sweat, towels and clothing friction all remove product. Rather than a thin spray at the door, apply the stated amount and schedule reapplies at fixed points (for example every 40–60 minutes or at the turnaround).

### Clothing is more reliable than cream alone

WHO is explicit: **the best protection is shade and clothing, not sunscreen alone, and sunscreen should not be used to extend time in the sun**. For runners that means:

- A wide-brimmed hat protects eyes, face, ears and neck;
- Wraparound sunglasses providing 99–100% UVA/UVB protection;
- Tightly woven clothing covering shoulders and upper arms is more reliable than a singlet plus a lot of sunscreen;
- Put long runs in the early morning or late afternoon, or choose shaded routes.

### Reflective surfaces and special environments

| Environment | What to watch |
|---|---|
| Snow | Fresh snow can reflect up to 80% of UV and almost double exposure; the chin, inside of the nose and eyes are easily missed |
| Beach | Sand reflects about 15%, with water and sea foam adding more; coastal wind can disguise the heat |
| Altitude | UV rises about 10% per 1,000 m, so the same duration is harsher |
| Water-side routes | Water reflects less than 10%, but sea foam about 25%, and exposure stacks between shore and water |
| Thin cloud / overcast | Thin cloud may enhance scattered UV and does not justify less protection |

## The two sides of vitamin D

WHO also states that **small amounts of UV are beneficial and essential for vitamin D production**, while excessive exposure is carcinogenic. Both sentences belong together. Runners who are outdoors regularly usually get enough sun exposure for vitamin D synthesis. The group that needs attention is people with **very low sun exposure, deeply pigmented skin at high latitudes, or full-body covering for religious or cultural reasons**; WHO suggests they consider oral vitamin D supplementation.

Do not chase vitamin D by tanning or sunbathing, and do not use sunbeds. WHO explicitly recommends avoiding artificial tanning devices, and artificial tanning should never be considered a way to achieve sufficient vitamin D. Whether you are deficient and whether you need a supplement are clinical questions; this page gives no dose.

## Where sunburn care ends and medical review begins

The principle for ordinary sunburn is to **stop further sun exposure** and follow local health-authority or pharmacist advice. Seek medical care for:

- Extensive **blistering**, or obvious blisters on the face or hands;
- **Fever, chills, headache, nausea or dehydration**;
- Signs of **infection** (increasing redness, swelling or pus);
- Severe eye pain, marked light sensitivity or difficulty seeing (possible photokeratitis, which needs assessment);
- A **new or changing mole**, bleeding, irregular borders, uneven colour, or a wound that does not heal — see a dermatologist rather than self-assessing.

This page does not diagnose skin cancer, photosensitivity disorders or drug reactions. People taking **medication that may increase photosensitivity**, with a family history of skin cancer, albinism or immunosuppression should use a more conservative strategy and check the medicine leaflet or consult the prescriber or a dermatologist.

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | More accurate statement |
|---|---|
| "No need for sunscreen on a cloudy day" | Thin cloud can enhance scattered UV; protect when the UV Index reaches 3 |
| "One application lasts all day" | Reapply every 2 hours and immediately after sweating |
| "Higher SPF means I can stay out longer" | High SPF is not a licence to extend exposure; sunscreen should not extend time in the sun |
| "A tan is a sign of health" | Tanning is a skin response to UV damage, not a health marker |
| "Snow is only about cold" | Fresh snow can reflect up to 80% of UV and almost double exposure |
| "More sun is better for vitamin D" | Small amounts are beneficial, excess is carcinogenic; at-risk groups should consider oral supplementation rather than longer sun exposure |

## What this page cannot answer

- The live UV Index where you are: check the local meteorological or environment agency;
- How a specific sunscreen performs on you: it depends on amount, reapplication, water resistance and skin;
- Whether you are vitamin D deficient: this needs a blood test and clinical interpretation;
- Whether a skin lesion is benign: a clinician must examine it; this page does not judge.

## Scope and the reapplication rhythm

Scope: this page covers everyday and race-day UV exposure, not photosensitive conditions, photosensitising medication or diagnosed skin cancer, which need dermatology advice.

Practical rhythm: check the day's UV Index before going out and choose sunscreen and cover accordingly; **reapply every 2 hours**, and immediately after heavy sweating, towelling or swimming. Treat it as a periodic item — start the habit two weeks before summer and review exposure time and any burning weekly through the season, rather than waiting for peeling skin.
