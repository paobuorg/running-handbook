## Conclusion: what to do first

If you run at least twice a week, are adding mileage, or are training for an event, the injury-prevention levers rank by evidence strength as follows: **keep load progression within what your body can recover from and respond early to warning signals, do regular strength and stability work for the hips, knees, ankles and core, protect sleep and recovery, and only then turn to shoes, terrain and stretching as conditional adjustments.** Current evidence does not support "stretch every day and you will not get injured," nor does it support "buying or rotating several pairs of shoes will prevent injury."

Three actions to take first. First, record pain, fatigue and sleep after every run, and use the 24-hour post-run response to decide whether load can go up. Second, schedule two to three strength sessions per week covering hips, glutes, ankles and core. Third, when pain keeps worsening, swelling appears, the knee or ankle gives way, or pain shows up at rest at night, stop running and follow the care boundaries below rather than testing yourself with one more run.

This page is health education, not a diagnostic tool. The structural source of pain, imaging decisions and medication all have to be handled by a clinician.

## Who this is for, and who it is not for

**For you if** you are a beginner or intermediate runner trying to make everyday training or a race block steadier; you have recurring mild load-related niggles and want to review your training arrangement; or you coach athletes and plan cycles.

**Not for you if** you already have red-flag symptoms (see "When to stop and seek care" below). Those situations need a clinical pathway first; there is no self-care plan on this page that fits them. Runners with prior joint, ligament, tendon or metabolic conditions should set their prevention plan with their doctor or physiotherapist.

Prevention does not mean keeping mileage as low as possible. Runners training for a marathon or trail event still need progressive load; this page is about adding load within a recoverable range, not about resting indefinitely.

## Framework: split the problem into layers

Running-related injury is multifactorial. It helps to check it in four layers instead of blaming one muscle or one pair of shoes every time pain appears:

| Layer | What it contains | Related page or tool |

| Capacity | Hip/knee/ankle strength, tendon tolerance, balance and proprioception, running form and cadence | [Strength and taper](/en/knowledge/strength-and-taper/), [Cadence and stride](/en/knowledge/cadence-and-stride/) |

| Conditions | Shoe wear and fit, surface, up- and downhill, weather | [Running shoes](/en/knowledge/running-shoes/), [Running surfaces](/en/knowledge/running-surface/) |

A systematic review of 36 studies covering 23,047 runners found that 26.2% reported a running-related injury, while the evidence linking weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity or recent changes to injury was conflicting. The authors caution against recommending one optimal training progression for every runner. Load is an important factor, but it is not the only factor, and there is no universal safe line.

## Evidence and conflicts

### Load: the 10% rule is not a physiological law

"Do not increase weekly mileage by more than 10%" is the best-known heuristic, but research has not established it as a fixed threshold. The GRONORUN RCT randomised 532 novice runners to either a 13-week graded programme based on the 10% rule or a standard 8-week programme; injury incidence was 20.8% and 20.3% respectively (P=0.90), so the graded programme did not significantly reduce injuries. The Nielsen 2014 study of 873 novices split runners into progressions below 10%, 10%–30% and above 30%; no significant difference in overall injury rates appeared across the groups. Distance-related injuries showed a rising trend only in the above-30% group (HR 1.59, p=0.07, not statistically significant). A further questionnaire study of 556 half-marathon and 441 marathon runners found no association between weekly volume or longest endurance run and running-related injury.

Workable wording: **progression rate is a risk signal, not a safety licence.** Staying below a band does not guarantee no injury, and going above it does not mean injury is certain. Tendon tolerance, prior injury, sleep and life stress all change what you can absorb.

### Strength: the strongest preventive signal in the evidence we reviewed

The Lauersen 2014 meta-analysis stratified preventive measures by type. Strength training had the clearest effect, RR 0.315; proprioception (balance) training RR 0.550; multi-component programmes RR 0.655; stretching RR 0.963, a confidence interval crossing 1, with no protective effect. Acute injuries RR 0.647 (0.502–0.836) and overuse injuries RR 0.527 (0.373–0.746) were both reduced by preventive programmes. Note that the strength arm contained only four studies, and the authors say evidence across a wider range of injury types still needs to grow; this is not a guarantee that strength work means no injury.

### Sleep: an association exists, but it cannot predict alone

The Utrecht analysis of 425 Dutch recreational runners split sleep into duration, quality and sleep problems and identified four profiles. Only the Poor Sleepers profile was significantly associated with injury (OR 1.78, 95% CI 1.14–2.78). The study was a cross-sectional survey and cannot prove that sleeping enough prevents injury; the model explained only 5% of variance, which puts sleep in its place as one risk factor among many.

### Which "prevention" practices currently lack evidence

- **Stretching alone to prevent injury.** In the meta-analysis the stretching confidence interval crossed 1, and the authors do not support stretching for injury prevention, before or after exercise. Stretching can stay as a flexibility activity, but it does not replace strength and progressive load.
- **Prescribing shoes from static arch shape.** Three military randomised prospective trials covering 7,203 recruits found little difference in injury rates between arch-height-matched shoes and a uniform stability shoe; the authors conclude arch-height shoe prescribing had little influence on injury risk in basic training.
- **Over-promising shoe rotation.** The sources we reviewed provide no controlled evidence that rotating among several pairs reduces injury. The actionable prevention point is replacing worn shoes and confirming fit; whether rotation itself helps is not supported strongly enough to recommend.
- **One fixed progression number.** Both 10% and 30% come from specific study populations and cannot be turned into a hard threshold for everyone.

## Worked example

The numbers below are an assumed example showing how to calculate weekly progression, not a recommended plan.

**Baseline**: a novice runner currently does five runs per week totalling 40 km, has no current pain, and sleeps about 7.5 hours.

**First week, planned at 44 km:**

- Weekly progression = (44 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100% = **10.0%**.
- Longest run goes from 10 km to 12 km; long-run share of weekly volume = 12 ÷ 44 ≈ **27%**. No source sets a fixed ratio for the long-run share; this is a recording measure to compare against two weeks later.

**Second week, if volume rises to 52 km:**

- Progression versus the previous week = (52 − 44) ÷ 44 × 100% = **18.2%**;
- Two-week cumulative progression from baseline = (52 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100% = **30.0%**, at the boundary of the above-30% group in the Nielsen study.

**Counter-example**: jumping straight from 40 km to 56 km in one week means progression = (56 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100% = **40.0%**, above the 30% high-progression band in that study. Adding an interval session or losing sleep in the same week stacks the load further.

Whether the increase holds depends not only on the mileage percentage but on whether pain returns to baseline 24 hours after running, whether easy-run pace and RPE stay stable, and whether sleep and mood have not worsened over several days. You can organise these variables with the [injury risk check tool](/en/tools/injury-risk-check/) and the [training load tool](/en/tools/training-load/) before deciding whether to add or cut next week.

## Common failure modes and early signals

1. **Adding mileage and intensity in the same step.** Weekly volume, the long run, quality sessions and hills all move up in one week; the watch mileage changes little, but tissue stress rises clearly. Early signals are slower easy runs, higher RPE at the same pace, and soreness carrying into the next day.
2. **Using "it warms off" as proof it is fine.** Warm-up may temporarily dull pain, but load-related problems often reappear late in the run, then start earlier and persist afterwards. The right check is whether pain returns to baseline 24 hours later, not whether it tolerates during the run.
3. **Flexibility and recovery tools only, no strength.** Foam rolling, stretching and massage can supplement recovery, but the preventive evidence we reviewed sits with strength, proprioception and progressive load. Without a strength base, recovery tools do not raise tissue tolerance.
4. **Treating fatigue as a willpower issue.** Persistent fatigue, stalled or falling performance, and changed sleep or mood should first raise the possibility of insufficient recovery and prompt exclusion of illness, rather than pushing through. Overtraining syndrome has no single diagnostic marker; assessment is clinical and covers training, recovery and health factors.
5. **Returning to prior volume before the prior injury has rehabilitated fully.** Previous injury is a recognised risk background; incomplete rehabilitation makes the same region break down again. See [Return to running](/en/knowledge/return-to-running/) and [Pain and load](/en/knowledge/pain-and-load/).

## When to stop and seek care

Do not test with "one more run" when any of the following appear. Stop running and seek clinical assessment:

- Clear swelling, deformity, redness and heat over a joint or tendon, or fever and chills;
- Giving way, joint locking, or a pop or crack at the time of injury;
- Inability to bear weight or move the joint through its normal range;
- Pain that keeps worsening after several weeks of reduced load, or pain at rest at night;
- Persistent fatigue and performance decline that does not recover over weeks, with obvious changes in sleep, appetite or mood.

NHS running-injury guidance advises against running through pain; severe knee pain or swelling warrants prompt medical review. This page does not judge structural injury, arrange imaging, or recommend a painkiller type or dose. Medication choices and imaging decisions belong to a clinician.

## What would change this advice

- **Population**: novices, returning runners, older runners, female runners and runners with prior injuries have different tolerance and risk backgrounds; one load threshold cannot be copied across groups.
- **Race phase**: the taper late in a block, altitude or hot conditions, and disrupted sleep through travel all change recovery conditions; the priority order may need rearranging in those phases.
- **Evidence updates**: the links between strength, sleep and injury continue to be studied; if later high-quality cohorts or reviews set different thresholds, this page changes at review.
- **Health status**: runners with arthritis, tendon history, cardiovascular or metabolic conditions should have a prevention plan assessed clinically first.

## Scope and limits

This page is general running health education, not a diagnosis, treatment prescription or medication advice. The RR, OR and progression figures come from specific study populations and cannot be converted into a personal injury probability; cross-sectional studies do not prove causation. Children, pregnant runners, post-operative runners and people with underlying medical conditions should be assessed clinically first. This site does not interpret imaging and does not replace a sports medicine doctor, physiotherapist or pharmacist.
