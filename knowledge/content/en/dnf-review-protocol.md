## What this page solves

Deciding to withdraw and reviewing a withdrawal are two different jobs. The decision needs red flags and a safe exit; the review needs data, a timeline and a boundary of responsibility. Mixing them leads to debates on the trail and to an “I am not good enough” story afterwards.

This page handles only the review after a withdrawal or cut-off. If you are still on course with dizziness, hypothermia, vomiting or a changed gait, use [DNF decision-making](/en/knowledge/dnf-decision-making/) and call race medical; a review is not first aid.

## Medical first, causes later

In the first 0–2 hours after withdrawal, do only three things: warmth/fluids, medical check, transport and bed. Do not analyse “why” in this window and do not drive. Hypothermia, dehydration, hyponatraemia or foot injury may show up hours later.

Chest pain, confusion, persistent vomiting, inability to weight-bear or abnormal urine means medical care, not a hotel observation. Keep the medical record; it feeds the later review and return plan.

## Within 24 hours: record, do not judge

While memory is fresh, write one page of timeline: last normal meal, last urination, when nausea/cold/dizziness began, last checkpoint and cut-off, withdrawal location, what pacer/crew said, what medical gave. Facts only, not “I was weak”.

Save the bib, timing record, race report number, weather and kit photo. Do not decide “never again” within 24 hours; judgements at emotional peaks or troughs are unreliable.

## Within 7 days: sort causes into six buckets

Sort causes into: medical (heat illness, hypothermia, hyponatraemia, injury), fuelling (too little or too much fuel/water/salt), pacing (too fast early), navigation (wrong line/detour), kit (torch/shoes/warmth failure), external (weather/cut-off/transport). Write one primary cause per bucket; do not blame “willpower” for everything.

Add a seventh bucket: decision errors (not stopping, not carrying, not withdrawing). Review them, but do not punish yourself with shame; fix them with a checklist next time.

## Data table: turn the story into evidence

Make a table: checkpoint, planned arrival, actual arrival, planned split, actual split, stop time, fuel, symptoms, weather. Separate “I felt” from “the record shows”; if there is no record, write “unknown” rather than reconstructing from memory.

## Four common misreads

Misread one: treating a cut-off as “bad fitness”. A cut-off is an external constraint: late start, long checkpoint stop, course congestion. Two: treating one heat illness as “I cannot handle heat” without reading wet-bulb temperature and acclimation.

Three: treating “could not eat” as “weak gut” without checking gel concentration, caffeine and total carbohydrate. Four: treating withdrawal as a character flaw and ignoring red flags. Each misread repeats the same error next time.

## Change one variable at a time

Limit the review to three changes and execute only one next race. Examples: slow the first 5 km by 10 seconds; cut gels from three per hour to two plus real food; switch night lighting to primary plus backup.

Too many changes make the next race unreadable. Write them as if/then: if heart rate before CP2 exceeds X, switch to run-hike; if temperature exceeds Y, slow target pace by Z seconds.

## Recovery and return

Recover according to medical advice. Without a clear injury, use 3–7 days of easy walking and sleep, then assess a 30-minute pain-free jog; with a sprain, stress-fracture risk or persistent pain, see a clinician first.

Return through four gates: pain-free walking → pain-free jogging → 30 minutes continuous → volume. Spend at least 2–3 days at each gate. If pain or gait worsens, step back. Do not let “unfinished business” drive the load.

## Psychological review: turn DNF into information

Emotions after a DNF are usually shame, anger and grief mixed. Let them exist, but do not turn them into conclusions. Write three columns: what I did / what happened / what I change next time. Do not write “I am just not good enough”.

If the same pattern repeats (same heat, same gut, same fast start), consider a coach or sports-medicine doctor; for persistent insomnia, training avoidance or self-negation, seek psychological support.

## Chinese runner’s perspective

In Chinese races, a withdrawn runner often has to find a ride back; the checkpoint and finish may be far apart. Put “withdrawal transport” in the crew plan: who picks up, where, how long, and whether the bed is extended.

Overseas races add insurance, language and medical repatriation. Put rescue number, policy number, hotel address and emergency contact on an offline card; not only in the phone.

## Checklist

0–2 h: warmth/fluids/medical/transport. 24 h: timeline/documents/weather/kit photo. 7 days: six buckets/data table/three changes. 4 weeks: four return gates/medical review/next-race decision.

If any step is missing, do not say “I have reviewed it” before the next race.

If you review with crew or pacer, set rules first: facts and process only, no character judgement; each person says what they saw or did, not motives; end with three changes. Keep it to 45 minutes.

The host walks the timeline: last normal meal, first abnormal sign, last checkpoint, withdrawal decision, medical care. At each point ask “what information was available, what was missing”, not “why didn’t you push”.

## Thirty days after

Week one is records and recovery; week two is a pain-free jog assessment; week three decides whether regular training resumes; week four decides the next race. Do not enter a race in week one to prove you have moved on.

Thirty days later, reread the one-page table: if the same cause reappears, the change did not land; if not, the review actually changed the process.
