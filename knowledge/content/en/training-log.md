## What a training log is, and who it is for

A training log records each session's content, intensity, body response and life context in fixed fields, then uses weekly trends to explain why a run felt harder. It is for runners who train consistently and want to avoid blind load increases. Premise: record how a session actually felt, not only numbers that look good. First condition: fix a few decision-relevant fields.

## What to record: a minimum field set

Record the date, session type, duration or distance, how hard it felt, route or surface, and whether there was pain. For intensity, use the talk test or your own rating of perceived exertion (RPE); either says more about the day's load than pace alone. Add sleep, shoes, weather, work stress or menstrual-cycle information if useful.

| Field | How to record | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Date and type | Easy, quality, long run, race | Shows whether load is balanced |
| Duration or distance | Minutes or kilometres | Compares totals within a type |
| Intensity | Talk test or 0-10 RPE | Explains a harder day at the same pace |
| Pain | Site, 0-10 score, onset and settling | Supports reduce-or-seek-help decisions |
| Sleep | Duration and perceived quality | Places recovery beside load |

## How to record intensity

The talk test is fairly stable: at moderate intensity you can talk but not sing; at vigorous intensity you cannot say more than a few words without pausing for a breath. Recording it with RPE separates "same pace, harder day" from "pace and feel both worse".

## How to read trends

One session is easy to misread. More useful is the weekly total, the balance of easy and hard sessions, how long pain takes to settle, and whether sleep and fatigue worsen over several days. A systematic review found the evidence linking weekly distance, frequency, intensity or recent change to injury inconsistent; no single "safe progression" explains everyone. The log shows your pattern, not someone else's formula.

When pain, fatigue or sleep worsen for several days, review load and life stress before adjusting training.

## What wearable data can and cannot say

Pace, distance, heart rate and cadence from watches and phones are estimates. GPS drifts under trees, among tall buildings and in valleys; optical heart rate lags; pace is smoothed.

If the watch and your body disagree, trust persistent bodily signals and use the talk test, route and weather to explain the gap. Do not change a sensible session to fill a metric.

## How to build a usable log in three steps

1. Choose 5 fixed fields, such as date and type, duration or distance, RPE, pain and sleep, and fill them within 10 minutes of each run.
2. Review on a fixed day each week: total duration and distance, the easy-to-hard ratio, and any pain or sleep anomaly.
3. Summarise every 4 weeks: pick 1 clearest change for the next block, such as dropping 1 quality session or cutting the long run by 10%.

## A worked example: two four-week blocks (hypothetical)

Assume a runner runs 40 km a week in 5 sessions for 4 weeks at 6:30 per kilometre, RPE 4, sleeping 7.5 hours. Over the next 4 weeks volume rises to 48 km in 5 sessions, pace slows to 7:00, RPE rises to 6 and sleep falls to 6.5 hours. Pace is 30 seconds slower per kilometre, effort 2 points higher and sleep 1 hour shorter; three signals moving together suggest reducing load and reviewing sleep before adding volume. Numbers are hypothetical.

## Review frequency and cycles

Fill after each session, review on one day each week, summarise every 4 weeks, and check the fields every 12 weeks. Fields can change with goals, but not every week, or trends cannot be compared; change 1 variable per cycle.

## Common misconceptions

- "More detail is always better": too many fields lead to abandoned logs.
- "Pace is intensity": heat, wind and short sleep change load at the same pace.
- "Watch data beat feel": optical heart rate and GPS carry error.
- "A log replaces a doctor": it describes trends but cannot diagnose.

## Privacy and sharing

A route file can reveal home, work, regular routes and daily routines. Before sharing, remove or blur start and finish points, timestamps and device information, and check screenshots for sensitive places. Track conversion tools remove time, device and personal metadata, but inspect the file yourself before publishing.

## When to stop and when to seek help

When the log shows pain worsening across 3 consecutive sessions, night pain, joint swelling or a change in walking, stop quality sessions and reduce load before considering medical advice. Seek prompt assessment for chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness, persistent palpitations or marked weight change.

## Checklist

- 5 decision-relevant fields are fixed and consistently filled.
- Entries are completed within 10 minutes of each run.
- One day each week reviews volume, intensity and pain.
- Every 4 weeks produces one specific adjustment, not just an archive.
- Shared tracks have timestamps and start-finish points removed.

## Scope and limits

This page does not diagnose pain, fatigue or sleep problems. A log is not a medical record; persistent pain, chest symptoms, fainting, unusual breathlessness or marked weight change deserve medical assessment.
