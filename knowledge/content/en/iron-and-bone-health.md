## Why iron and bone appear on one page

Iron and bone look like separate systems, but they often fail together in runners. Iron is part of haemoglobin and myoglobin and supports oxygen transport; iron deficiency can reduce endurance performance. Bone stress injury (BSI) occurs when running load exceeds the bone's ability to repair itself. A 2025 review lists **iron deficiency** as one risk factor for BSI, alongside female sex, competitive participation, problematic low energy availability (LEA), vitamin D deficiency, irregular menstrual cycles and training prescription errors.

This does not mean iron deficiency causes a fracture, nor that iron supplements prevent one. It means that persistent fatigue, falling performance, repeated bone pain and menstrual changes may share an underlying problem: energy and nutrients not meeting the current training load.

## How to read ferritin

Serum ferritin is the commonly used marker of iron stores. The Alaunyte 2015 review provides these ranges and facts:

| Item | Value or finding |
|---|---|
| Physiological range in adult females | 15–200 μg/L |
| Lower limit for latent iron deficiency | 12–35 μg/L across studies, a wide range |
| Meaning of 12 μg/L | Usually indicates absent bone-marrow iron stores and complete storage depletion |
| Absorption versus ferritin | Absorption falls as ferritin rises, up to about 60 μg/L |
| At ferritin 10 μg/L | Absorption of both haem and non-haem iron is about 40% |
| At higher ferritin | Absorption falls, especially for non-haem iron |

So "low ferritin but not yet anaemic" is not the same as "no problem". Iron deficiency progresses in three stages: storage depletion (falling ferritin), reduced erythropoiesis (low serum iron and transferrin saturation), and finally anaemia (falling haemoglobin). The first two stages are called latent iron deficiency or iron-deficient non-anaemia. They may already affect performance while a standard blood count looks unremarkable.

**Do not rely on haemoglobin alone.** A normal haemoglobin does not exclude iron deficiency; ferritin, a blood count and clinical judgement need to be read together. Ferritin interpretation also changes with the laboratory and with inflammation, so this page does not provide a self-diagnosis threshold.

## Iron intake and absorption: why "eating lots of iron" may not be enough

| Fact | Implication |
|---|---|
| Haem iron absorption can reach 40%, while non-haem absorption is 2%–20% | Iron from animal sources is absorbed more efficiently |
| Haem iron is only about 10% of all dietary iron | Most dietary iron is non-haem |
| About 50% of dietary iron in Western diets comes from grain products | Staples and fortified foods matter, but absorption is affected by phytates and other inhibitors |
| UK reference nutrient intake for adult females is 14.8 mg/day; US recommended dietary allowance is 18 mg/day | These are population recommendations, not an individual athlete prescription |
| Some authors suggest endurance female runners need about 70% more iron | That would add roughly 10 mg/day to the UK value; it is a suggestion, not a universal official standard |
| Vegetarian runners had ferritin 7.4 μg/L versus 19.8 μg/L in meat eaters | Total iron intake was similar, but absorbable iron was 0.66 versus 0.91 mg/day |

Promoters of non-haem iron absorption include vitamin C, some organic acids, meat, fish and shellfish; inhibitors include phytates, phenolic compounds and calcium. Pairing iron-rich foods with vitamin C is a more practical step than simply eating more spinach. The timing of tea, coffee and calcium supplements relative to meals is also worth recording before changing.

**This site does not provide iron doses.** Whether to supplement, how much and for how long depends on ferritin, haemoglobin, inflammation, menstrual losses and gut tolerance, and should be decided by a doctor. Long-term self-supplementation carries a risk of iron overload and may mask gastrointestinal blood loss.

## The scale of bone stress injury

The Crunkhorn 2025 review reports:

| Item | Data |
|---|---|
| Share of musculoskeletal injuries in cross-country, track and field, triathlon, duathlon and recreational running | 15%–20% |
| Lower-limb share | 80%–95% |
| Tibia | 49% |
| Tarsal bones | 25% |
| Female versus male runners | 1.8–2.3 times higher in females |
| Female versus male triathletes | 2.9 times higher in females |
| Occurring in training versus competition | 66% versus 7% (Australian audit, 48 sports, 3 years) |
| Two or more BSIs within 3 years | 15% of athletes |
| Lumbar spine recurrence in cricket | 39% |
| High-school incidence | 1.5 per 100,000 athlete-exposures |
| NCAA incidence | 5.7 per 100,000 athlete-exposures |

These figures show that BSI is not rare, especially in runners with high volume, rapid load changes or low energy availability. They also show that **recurrence** is a central feature: a poorly managed first BSI raises the risk of the next one.

## Energy availability: where iron and bone meet

The 2023 IOC REDs consensus defines energy availability (EA) as:

```
EA = (energy intake − exercise energy expenditure) / fat-free mass / day
```

The consensus reports a prevalence range for low energy availability or REDs indicators of 23%–79.5% in female athletes and 15%–70% in male athletes. It distinguishes low energy availability that is adaptable from problematic low energy availability; the latter is associated with reduced musculoskeletal health, increased injury risk and impaired performance.

For runners, the useful part is not memorising the formula but recognising the pattern:

- Energy intake persistently below training expenditure;
- Unplanned weight loss, or long-term low body weight;
- Menstrual cycle changes or amenorrhoea;
- Repeated bone stress injuries or bone pain;
- Persistent fatigue, feeling cold, poor sleep and low mood;
- Falling performance while training load keeps rising.

These signals need clinical assessment. **Do not try to fix them by simply eating a little more or losing a little weight.** Problematic low energy availability involves endocrine, bone metabolic, psychological and nutritional systems.

## Who is more likely to develop bone stress injury

The review lists female sex, competitive participation, problematic low energy availability, vitamin D or iron deficiency, irregular menstrual cycles and training prescription errors. Some specific population findings:

- Female adolescent athletes training 12–16 hours or more per week have increased risk;
- Male adolescents running more than 30 miles (about 48 km) per week have increased risk;
- Most BSIs occur in training rather than competition;
- Lower-limb weight-bearing bones, especially the tibia and tarsal bones, are most common.

These are group-level risk factors, not a personal diagnosis. Their value is this: if you have several of them and develop local bone pain, seek assessment earlier instead of adding load.

## Recognition and referral boundaries

| Presentation | Action |
|---|---|
| Local, fixed, pinpoint bone pain, tender to touch, worse with running or hopping | Stop loading that area and seek sports medicine assessment; imaging may be needed |
| Pain at rest, night pain, limping, inability to bear weight | Seek medical care promptly; do not keep running |
| Pain with marked swelling, local heat or recent trauma | Seek medical care promptly to exclude fracture and other injuries |
| Repeated pain in the same bone, or multiple stress injuries | Assess energy availability, iron status, vitamin D, menstrual cycle and training load |
| Menstrual cycle change, amenorrhoea, unplanned weight loss | Seek medical care; do not treat these as training achievements |
| Chest pain, fainting, severe palpitations, unresolving breathing difficulty | Treat as an emergency |

This page does not interpret imaging, grade fractures or give painkiller or supplement advice. BSI severity, location and return-to-run timelines require clinical and imaging assessment; some sites, such as certain tarsal bones and the femoral neck, carry higher risk and cannot be managed with a blanket "rest for two weeks".

## What you can do (not a prescription)

- Match total energy intake to training expenditure rather than running a long-term deficit;
- Include carbohydrate, protein and vegetables in each meal, and avoid treating low body weight as a performance goal;
- Diversify iron sources, pair plant iron with vitamin C, and note the timing of tea, coffee and calcium relative to meals;
- Do not self-prescribe long-term iron or high-dose vitamin D;
- Change one training variable at a time, and reduce load rather than add it when bone pain appears;
- Track menstrual cycles, and for adolescents track height, weight and training hours;
- Seek medical care when red flags appear rather than self-diagnosing online.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A normal haemoglobin means no iron deficiency"** — iron deficiency can occur before anaemia; ferritin and a blood count need to be read together.
- **"Spinach will fix iron"** — plant iron has low absorption and is affected by phytates; total intake and absorption conditions both matter.
- **"Low ferritin means buy iron tablets"** — supplementation needs medical assessment, and long-term self-supplementation carries an iron overload risk.
- **"Bone pain is a normal response to high mileage"** — fixed pinpoint pain, night pain and limping are signals that need assessment.
- **"Two weeks of rest fixes every bone pain"** — severity and site differ greatly, and some sites need stricter management.
- **"Amenorrhoea means training is working"** — it is a warning signal of inadequate energy availability, not a badge.
- **"Vitamin D prevents all bone injuries"** — vitamin D is one factor and does not replace assessment of energy, iron, training load and menstrual status.

## Checklist

- [ ] Do I know my most recent ferritin and haemoglobin results?
- [ ] Have I treated a normal haemoglobin as proof that iron is fine?
- [ ] Is my total energy intake persistently below training expenditure?
- [ ] Do I have menstrual changes, unplanned weight loss or repeated bone pain?
- [ ] Am I taking long-term iron or high-dose vitamin D without medical supervision?
- [ ] When pinpoint bone pain, night pain or limping appears, do I stop adding load and seek care?
- [ ] Have I treated low body weight or amenorrhoea as a performance goal?

## Boundaries

This page is health education. It is not a diagnosis, treatment or nutrition prescription, and it gives no iron dose or medication advice. Ferritin thresholds, BSI grading and return-to-run timelines require clinical assessment. Pregnant and postpartum runners, adolescents, vegetarians, people with a history of eating disorders, gastrointestinal disease or chronic illness, and people taking bone-active medication should be managed with a doctor or registered dietitian. Emergency signals should be treated as emergencies.
