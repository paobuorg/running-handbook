## Bottom line

Adaptation comes from the “stimulus–recovery–supercompensation” cycle: insufficient load brings no progress, while load beyond recovery capacity causes declining performance and accumulated fatigue. This page keeps the concepts separate: **functional overreaching (FO)** is a temporary performance drop that improves after rest, with supercompensation; **non-functional overreaching (NFO)** and **overtraining syndrome (OTS)** involve a longer performance drop, and OTS recovery is measured in weeks to months. Three judgments come first. First, do not judge overtraining from a single device number: resting heart rate, HRV and blood markers are not validated independent diagnostic tools. Second, early recognition rests on a combination of signals — performance, perceived effort, sleep, mood and recovery state read together. Third, a performance decline that does not recover over weeks, with mood changes, needs other medical causes ruled out and professional assessment, not pushing through on reduced training.

The evidence boundary: load–injury evidence comes mostly from team-sport athletes and recruit cohorts, with limited direct evidence in recreational runners. The predictive value of ACWR (acute:chronic workload ratio) is openly divided; this page states both sides, does not average them, and does not settle on a single threshold. The site does not diagnose medical conditions; chest pain, syncope, unusual breathlessness and persistent palpitations follow first-aid and medical care.

## Definition and prerequisites

Training load here means the stimulus total and intensity a runner absorbs, including volume, intensity, frequency, race count and recovery arrangement. The overtraining syndrome guide describes OTS as “a maladapted response to excessive exercise without adequate rest”, involving neurological, endocrine, immune and mood changes. This page is for regularly training runners with persistent fatigue or declining performance; it assumes you record volume, recovery state and subjective feel together, not a single day's state.

If you have known cardiac, endocrine, anemia, infection or diet-related problems, seek medical assessment first. Low energy availability conditions (including menstrual dysfunction, bone, metabolic and psychological/behavioral changes) can look similar to fatigue and should be distinguished from overtraining; see the differential section. Everyday recovery arrangements are in [Rest day: doing nothing at all is not the point](/en/knowledge/recovery-day/) and [Sleep and recovery](/en/knowledge/sleep-recovery/).

## Load metrics: weekly volume, monotony and ACWR

Common load metrics each have limits and should be combined, not substituted for one another.

- **Weekly volume:** the most direct external load marker. There is no universally safe percentage for the increase; progression evidence is compared below.
- **Training monotony:** repetition in training content, intensity and route. The guide lists monotony as one potential trigger; even low total volume can under-recover when every session repeats the same intensity and rhythm.
- **ACWR (acute:chronic workload ratio):** acute load is usually the most recent week; chronic load is a medium-term rolling average (for example four weeks); the ratio observes how far recent load sits above the base level. Whether it predicts injury is debated (see conflict section).
- **Perceived effort and performance:** RPE at a given pace, time over a fixed distance and session quality; these are recorded as early signals in the guide. External device load cannot replace these observations.

Consumer wearables record heart rate, HRV and volume, but a 2025 review notes these devices are not medical-grade, validity evidence is limited, and monitoring value in high-level athletes is questionable. Device readings are auxiliary reference, not a self-diagnosis basis.

## Evidence conflict side by side: ACWR predictive value

Whether ACWR works as an injury predictor is not settled. The table below sets claims, source type and sample boundary side by side, without averaging.

| Position | Claim | Source type | Sample and boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Supporting: ACWR can predict load–injury | The review describes ACWR as a “best practice predictor” of training-related injuries; among elite cricket fast bowlers, ACWR ≤0.99 corresponded to about 4% injury likelihood in the following 7 days, and ≥1.5 to a 2–4 times greater risk; it proposes 0.8–1.3 as the training “sweet spot” and ≥1.5 as the “danger zone” | Narrative review and conceptual model (Gabbett 2016, BJSM) | Evidence mainly elite team-sport cohorts (cricket, rugby league, soccer); the authors note different sports may have different load–injury relationships, and extrapolation to individual-sport athletes needs caution |
| Critical/limiting: ACWR is not an independent predictive model | The multilevel meta-analysis found only a small-to-moderate association between elevated ACWR and injury risk, with very high heterogeneity; effects were not significant for time-loss injuries; current evidence does not support ACWR as a stand-alone causal or predictive model — only a contextual indicator within an individualized multi-marker load-management system | Systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis (Ding 2026, Frontiers in Public Health; PROSPERO CRD420245127618) | 41 studies screened; 16 studies with 797 athletes entered the meta-analysis; team-sport athletes; pooled Hedges' g = 0.35 (95% CI 0.16–0.54), I² = 95.8% |

This site's position: ACWR can be an auxiliary indicator of load change, but **no threshold has been validated for recreational runners**. Do not draw personal lines from team-sport figures such as 0.8–1.3 or 1.5. Load judgment should combine volume progression, monotony, sleep, mood, performance and perceived effort. Device and metric boundaries are in [Training log: volume, intensity and trends](/en/knowledge/training-log/).

## FO, NFO and OTS: the boundary

The FO/NFO/OTS distinction can often only be made retrospectively, based on time to recovery rather than symptom severity.

| Category | Presentation and recovery | Recovery-time standard (some definitions) | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Functional overreaching FO | Temporary performance drop, improves after rest, supercompensation possible | Days to weeks | Balance stimulus and recovery; usually no special treatment |
| Non-functional overreaching NFO | Longer performance drop, no supercompensation | Some definitions: fewer than 14–21 days of rest to previous level is NFO | Recover, correct triggers; professional assessment if needed |
| Overtraining syndrome OTS | Performance drop persisting weeks to months, with mood changes | Some definitions: more than 14–21 days of recovery is OTS | Rest (some favor relative rest), rebuild load, professional medical assessment |

The guide also states that resting heart rate, maximal heart rate and HRV give inconsistent results and do not differentiate FO, NFO and OTS; there is no specific validated blood marker. Clinical history should show: performance decline persisting despite weeks to months of recovery, mood disturbance, and absence of other identifiable causes. A separate differential is low energy availability (LEA) under RED-S: menstrual dysfunction, bone problems, metabolic, immune, cardiovascular and psychological/behavioral changes can mimic fatigue and should not be lumped into “trained too much”.

## Early signals: combined criteria, not a single device number

Potential triggers in the guide include increased load without recovery, monotony, excessive competitions, sleep disturbance and personal-life stressors. One early signal is increased perceived effort at a given workload. This site groups observable signals for continuous, combined recording.

| Signal group | What to observe | Caveats |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Performance | Slower at the same pace, lower quality at the same load, off-day sessions and races | A single fluctuation does not count; decline over consecutive days/weeks |
| Perceived effort | Higher RPE at the same pace, sessions feel harder | One of the guide's recorded early signals |
| Sleep | Difficulty falling asleep, lighter sleep, less time | Adults commonly average 7–9 h, but individual variation is large; persistent sleep problems see a GP |
| Mood and state | Irritability, low mood, loss of interest in training | Mood change is part of the NFO/OTS clinical history |
| Body signals | Recurrent upper-respiratory complaints, abnormal resting sensations, slower recovery | Resting HR/HRV do not differentiate categories; trend reference only |
| Other causes to rule out | Anemia, infection, endocrine problems, insufficient intake, life stress | These need professional assessment; do not self-attribute to overtraining |

A single number (one elevated resting HR, one low HRV night) cannot diagnose; trend and combination matter. Consumer-device precision differs from medical-grade monitoring; do not treat device readings as diagnostic conclusions.

## Volume progression evidence: the 10% rule and progression studies

The “weekly increase under 10%” rule is a common heuristic, but controlled evidence does not support it as a universal safety line.

- A randomized trial in 532 novices compared a graded ≤10% weekly progression program with conventional training: injury rates 20.8% and 20.3%, difference not significant (p = .90); the graded program did not reduce injury.
- A prospective study analyzing 873 runners grouped two-week weekly increases into <10%, 10–30% and >30%; no significant overall between-group injury difference; distance-related injury HR 1.59 (95% CI 0.96–2.66, p = .07), not significant; the authors suggest keeping the two-week increase under 30% may be prudent, but this is not a mandatory threshold.
- A systematic review of 36 studies and 23,047 runners estimated running-related injury incidence around 26.2%, while noting evidence conflicts and that the progression–injury relationship has no unified conclusion.

Position: 10% can serve as a conservative starting pace for novices, but it is not a validated injury safety line; progression should adjust for base, recovery and signals. Weekly structure and periodization are in [Training week and periodization](/en/knowledge/periodization/) and [Volume and progression: evidence and limits](/en/knowledge/training-volume-and-progression/).

## Recovery interventions: evidence strength and what works

The guide describes the treatment direction: FO needs only balancing overload with appropriate recovery; NFO/OTS treatment is rest (some propose relative rest), rebuilding volume before intensity under tolerance, starting from 5–10 minutes daily until 1 hour is tolerated; which strategy is best remains unclear.

Actionable recovery measures and their evidence position:

| Intervention | Position on this site | Evidence status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rest and deload | Deload or rest when combined signals appear after sustained decline | Guide treatment direction for NFO/OTS; best strategy not defined |
| Sleep | Treat sleep as a basic recovery condition; do not replace it with caffeine or supplements | NHS: adults average 7–9 h, large individual variation; persistent sleep problems see a GP |
| Diet and energy | Rule out insufficient intake, especially for women, long distance and preparation | IOC RED-S: LEA affects multiple systems; needs professional assessment |
| Active recovery | Low-intensity activity, mobility, easy walking fit a recovery day | Recovery-day concept in related pages; does not replace adequate rest |
| Supplements/device intervention | This site recommends no supplement; wearable readings are auxiliary | No supplement evidence in the guide; device validity evidence limited |

Recovery is not “lie down until you want to train again”; rebuild gradually by tolerance. Returning too early to the original intensity often recurs. Re-entry pacing is in [Return to running: restarting after injury or time off](/en/knowledge/return-to-running/).

## Red flags for medical care

Do not self-manage the following as “overtraining”: chest pain, syncope or near-syncope, unusual breathlessness, persistent palpitations; performance still declining after weeks of rest; marked low mood, anxiety or everyday impairment; recurrent infection, unexplained weight loss, menstrual dysfunction or bone pain; abnormal symptoms under known conditions. Persistent fatigue and decline should also first rule out anemia, infection, endocrine problems and other medical causes. This site does not diagnose or prescribe a rest duration before returning to training; qualified medical or sports professionals make that call with history, examination and follow-up.

## Decision-branch table: if → then

| Condition (if) | Action (then) | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One off day, one high device reading | Record the trend; do not conclude | Resting HR/HRV cannot differentiate categories (OTS guide) |
| Multi-week performance drop + higher perceived effort + sleep or mood change | Deload/rest; re-check the signal combination | Combined criteria; no single number |
| Just finished a heavy block or dense race period | Prioritize recovery, sleep and energy before the next block | Monotony and dense racing are listed triggers |
| Wanting rapid volume buildup | Start conservatively, adjust by base and recovery; do not apply a single percentage threshold | The 10% rule is not RCT-validated universally; <30% over two weeks is one prudent benchmark |
| Calculating ACWR as reference | Treat it as a contextual indicator within multi-marker judgment; do not draw team-sport thresholds | Two evidence sides; recreational-runner thresholds unvalidated |
| Red-flag symptoms appear | Stop self-adding load; seek medical assessment | Medical boundary; this site does not diagnose |
| Menstrual dysfunction, bone pain, insufficient intake | Seek assessment along the LEA/RED-S direction; do not attribute to training alone | IOC 2023 consensus |

## Worked example: four-week progression and ACWR illustration (teaching assumption)

Assume a runner's five weekly volumes are 50, 60, 65, 70, 75 km; sleep dropped from about 8 h to about 6 h; RPE at the same pace rose from 5 to 7; pace slowed about 30 s/km over the same distance.

| Week | Weekly volume (km) | Week-over-week increase |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 50 | — |
| 2 | 60 | (60 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100% = +20.0% |
| 3 | 65 | (65 − 60) ÷ 60 × 100% ≈ +8.3% |
| 4 | 70 | (70 − 65) ÷ 65 × 100% ≈ +7.7% |
| 5 | 75 | (75 − 70) ÷ 70 × 100% ≈ +7.1% |

- Chronic load (four-week average): (50 + 60 + 65 + 70) ÷ 4 = 245 ÷ 4 = 61.25 km.
- Week-5 ACWR illustration: 75 ÷ 61.25 ≈ 1.22.

Note: this is teaching arithmetic showing how the metrics are computed, not a safety line or diagnosis. 1.22 sits near the upper edge of the “sweet spot” quoted in a team-sport review, but that threshold comes from elite team-sport cohorts and has not been validated in recreational runners. In this example sleep, RPE and pace all worsened together; the combined signals matter more than the ratio itself. Tool-level load logging is in the [training load recording tool](/en/tools/training-load/).

## Failure modes and early signals

- **Volume only, ignoring recovery signals:** early signal is rising weekly volume while sleep, mood and same-pace RPE worsen together. Rule: record load and recovery side by side; deload when the combination appears.
- **Self-diagnosing from device readings:** early signal is treating one low HRV or high HR as a confirmed diagnosis, then changing training or deciding anxiously on that basis. Rule: devices are trend reference; diagnosis goes to professional assessment.
- **Monotone stacking of the same intensity:** early signal is near-identical sessions, routes and intensity, with blunted body response. Rule: vary intensity, recovery days and rest; avoid long monotone stimulus.
- **Pushing through the fatigue phase:** early signal is holding the original plan as performance drops, overriding discomfort with willpower. Rule: FO balances recovery; persistent non-recovering NFO/OTS cannot be pushed through.
- **Mistaking low energy for overtraining:** early signal is menstrual dysfunction, bone pain, weight loss or food restriction at the same time. Rule: investigate along the LEA/RED-S direction; see related pages.

## Common misconceptions

- “Fatigue means not training hard enough.” Persistent fatigue and decline are signals to handle, not a character test.
- “Low HRV means overtraining.” No single device indicator differentiates FO/NFO/OTS.
- “Under a 10% weekly increase you will not get injured.” The 10% rule is a heuristic; RCT evidence does not show universal injury reduction.
- “ACWR past a certain number guarantees injury.” Predictive value is divided, and thresholds come from team sports; recreational runners are unvalidated.
- “A few days' rest should fully fix it.” NFO/OTS recovery takes longer, and other causes must be excluded.

## Scope and limits

This page does not diagnose medical conditions, judge whether you have overtraining syndrome, or prescribe how long to rest before returning to training; qualified medical or sports professionals decide that with history, examination and follow-up. Load–injury evidence comes mostly from novices, team sports or specific cohorts; extrapolation to individual runners needs care; a single ACWR threshold is not validated for recreational runners; this site found no personal resting-HR or HRV diagnostic threshold (evidence insufficient). Pain and load in training are reviewed in [Pain and load: distinction, expression and when to seek care](/en/knowledge/pain-and-load/).

## What would change this advice

- **Training phase:** preparation, dense racing and taper periods differ in load and recovery; periodization is in related pages.
- **Body and life state:** sleep, work stress, diet and injury recovery all change tolerance.
- **Evidence updates:** research on ACWR, progression rates and wearable validity is still moving; this page is revised when high-quality reviews or consensus shift the position.
- **Personal history:** cardiac, endocrine, anemia and diet-related problems change the investigation order and should be assessed first.
- **Recovery outcome:** returning after deload versus persistent non-recovery points to different directions; judgment follows follow-up.
