## What load and overtraining mean, and who this is for

Training load is the total stimulus absorbed: distance, duration, intensity, frequency, gradient and surface. Overtraining syndrome (OTS) is a maladapted response to excessive exercise without adequate rest, involving neurological, endocrine and immune systems and mood changes. Premise: fatigue is a signal, not a weakness. First condition: exclude illness and life factors before discussing load.

## Fatigue and adaptation

Fatigue after training is normal. Heavy legs for days after a long run, deeper sleep or a slower easy day do not mean the plan is wrong. Adaptation happens between stimulus and recovery: enough stress with enough recovery makes you stronger; persistent stress and poor recovery accumulate fatigue. The question is not "am I tired?" but whether fatigue settles in reasonable time and whether performance, mood, sleep and health change together.

## From functional overload to overtraining syndrome

Sports medicine distinguishes ordinary fatigue, functional overreaching, non-functional overreaching and OTS. Functional overreaching recovers with rest and may aid adaptation; when load persistently exceeds recovery, performance may decline, fatigue can last weeks, and sleep and mood may change.

Persistent fatigue with declining or plateaued performance should prompt consideration of OTS and exclusion of illness. No single blood test confirms it; assessment is clinical and looks for contributing training, recovery and health factors. Thyroid disease, anaemia, infection, low energy intake, sleep disorders and depression can look similar.

## There is no universal load formula

Weekly distance, intensity, frequency and recent change are associated with injury, but a systematic review found the evidence inconsistent. No single "safe percentage increase" applies to everyone. "Add 10% a week" is a discussion point, not a law of physiology; a jump in intensity, reduced sleep or extra life stress can make a tolerable load intolerable.

## Load is more than mileage

Load is not one number; distance, duration, intensity, gradient, surface, shoes, sleep and life stress belong together.

| Dimension | How to observe | Common misread |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distance and duration | Weekly total, longest session | Mileage only, ignoring time |
| Intensity mix | Easy-to-quality ratio | Treating a jog as recovery |
| Gradient and surface | Ascent metres, trail share | Underrating descents and road |
| Life load | Sleep, work, caregiving stress | Blaming training for all fatigue |
| Recovery | Sleep duration, rest days | Replacing sleep with device data |

## How to judge and adjust load in three steps

1. Record: for 2 weeks, log session type, duration or distance, RPE, pain, sleep and how the day felt.
2. Compare: review weekly whether RPE at the same pace rises and whether sleep or mood worsen across days.
3. Adjust: if the change persists, cut volume by roughly 30%, drop quality sessions and observe for 2 weeks; if it still does not settle, seek medical assessment.

## A worked example: four weeks of rising load and falling sleep (hypothetical)

Assume a runner starts at 50 km a week in 5 sessions, sleeping 8 hours, with easy-run RPE 5. Weekly volume rises to 60, 65, 70 and then 75 km over 4 weeks. Over the same period sleep falls to 6 hours, easy-run RPE rises to 7 and pace slows by 30 seconds per kilometre. Volume is up 50%, sleep down 2 hours and effort up 2 points. If these move together, return volume to about 50 km, restore sleep and observe for 2 weeks. Numbers are hypothetical.

## Record trends, not single readings

A log should record session type, duration, RPE, pain, sleep and how the day felt. Review weekly: are easy runs slower? Does the same quality session cost more effort? Are resting heart rate, sleep and mood worsening together? These patterns carry more information than one day's reading.

## Review frequency and cycles

Log after each session, review one day each week, schedule a lighter week every 3 to 4 weeks, and revisit the load curve every 12 weeks. A lighter week cuts volume but keeps some running; change 1 variable per cycle.

## Common misconceptions

- "Fatigue proves the training worked": persistent fatigue and falling performance more often signal under-recovery.
- "Willpower overcomes it": OTS involves endocrine and immune changes, not attitude.
- "One rest week fixes everything": fatigue lasting weeks needs clinical assessment.

## When to reduce load or seek help

Reduce volume and intensity, and review sleep, energy intake, illness and stress, when you notice:

- performance falling or plateauing for weeks, not one bad session;
- fatigue that does not settle with rest, or unusual effort during light activity;
- marked changes in sleep, mood, appetite or ability to work;
- repeated infections, slow wound healing or persistently abnormal resting heart rate;
- chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness or persistent palpitations.

The first four deserve a prompt discussion with a clinician or sports-medicine professional; the last group needs urgent assessment. Do not use caffeine, painkillers or "one more week" to mask worsening signals.

## Checklist

- Training type, duration, RPE, sleep and pain are logged for 2 weeks.
- One day weekly is set aside to review trends.
- Recent short sleep, infection or a jump in life stress has been checked.
- A lighter week keeps some intensity instead of stopping.
- Fatigue lasting weeks has led to medical assessment.

## Scope and limits

This page does not diagnose OTS, anaemia, thyroid disease, depression or infection. Persistent fatigue and performance decline need professional assessment.
