## Definition and conditions of applicability

Returning to running means rebuilding a repeatable load after a layoff or a minor injury, not replacing missed distance at once. This page applies to runners who can walk without pain, have no new swelling and are not woken by pain at night. Two conditions must hold: pain has settled enough that walking and stairs are normal, and progress is judged in weeks. If pain is still present, no progression table applies.

## Triage first

Four signals rule out running: pain at rest, pain on flat walking, joint swelling, and night pain. NHS advice is not to run while in pain; when pain is severe, persistent or accompanied by swelling, seek medical or physiotherapy advice. Fever, chest pain, fainting or unusual breathlessness also need care, not adaptation.

| Signal | Check | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rest pain | Pain sitting or lying | Do not run; assess cause |
| Walking pain | Pain after 10 minutes walking | Do not run; use easy activity |
| Swelling | Compare with the other side | Do not run; seek assessment |
| Night pain | Woken by pain | Do not run; seek assessment |

## Week one and frequency

Week one reminds the body of impact; it is not about distance. One-minute runs with 1.5-minute walks are where NHS Couch to 5K starts, using three runs a week with rest days and building to 30 minutes continuous over nine weeks or longer. Keep three runs a week, and repeat the same dose three or four times before increasing: total time first, then running share, pace last.

## Next-day response

A good run does not prove the dose was right. Log pain during running (0 to 10), morning stiffness, whether walking is normal, and any sleep change. If the next day is worse than last week, step back one dose.

## How to do it: three steps

1. Confirm no rest, walking or night pain and no new swelling before week one.
2. Book three run-walk sessions, with rest days between and an easy total time.
3. After three or four repeats, add one variable only and log the next-day response.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

Assume six weeks off, pain-free walking, and week one at 20 minutes per session: one minute running, 1.5 minutes walking, three times a week. Weeks one and two hold that dose for six sessions; week three moves to 25 minutes; week four to two-minute runs with one-minute walks; week five to 30 minutes. Weekly time rises from 60 to 90 minutes, a 50% rise over four weeks, one variable at a time. These numbers are hypothetical, not findings.

## What the evidence supports

A review of 36 studies and 23,047 runners found 26.2% reported a running-related injury. Evidence on weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity and recent changes was conflicting, and the authors cautioned against one optimal progression for every runner. The schedule is a stepping-back rule, not an injury forecast.

## Common misconceptions

- "It felt fine, so add more": the next-day response is the signal.
- "Make up missed mileage": it steepens the dose curve.
- "Run through a little pain": NHS advice is not to run while in pain.

## When to stop and seek care

Stop for pain that rises and changes gait, new swelling, pain lasting into the next day, or night pain; return to pain-free activity and step back a dose. Seek care for severe or persistent pain, pain with swelling, fever, chest pain, fainting or unusual breathlessness.

## Checklist

- No rest, walking or night pain, and no new swelling.
- Three run-walk sessions booked with rest days between.
- Pain score and next-day response logged.
- Three or four repeats done before adding a variable.
- Four-week review date set.

## Scope and limits

This page does not diagnose injuries or cover return after surgery, bone-stress injury, cardiovascular conditions or postpartum recovery. A progression cannot replace examination or rehabilitation assessment.
