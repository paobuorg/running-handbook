## Conclusion first

The core message of returning to running after injury is: **triage first, start with repeatable small doses, and use "can this dose be repeated pain-free" as the gate to progress; do not make up missed mileage in one go.** The NHS position is clear: do not run through pain; severe knee pain or swelling means seeing a GP straight away, and severe shin pain, swelling or no improvement after a few weeks means seeing a GP or physiotherapist. Coming back is not a return to the pre-injury weekly mileage; it is a staged rebuild of tissue tolerance, movement control and psychological readiness.

Three actions come first. First, red-flag symptoms go to a clinician first; do not self-escalate. Second, start with run-walk or short easy bouts; move to the next stage only after the same dose repeats 2–3 times with a normal next-day response. Third, progression is judged by in-exercise pain, the 24-hour response, functional tests and subjective confidence — not by the calendar.

This page is training and rehabilitation education, not a diagnosis, treatment plan or rehabilitation prescription. Acute injury, persistent pain, swelling, numbness or weakness, or a prior injury history should be assessed by a doctor or physiotherapist first.

## Definition and premises

- **Return to running:** the process of getting back to running training after injury or layoff. This page distinguishes "back to easy running" from "back to unrestricted training and competition".
- **Stage gate:** conditions that must be met before the next stage; no volume or intensity increase before the gate is passed.
- **Reinjury signal:** pain, swelling, limp or functional decline during exercise or within 24 h after, indicating excessive load.
- **Run-walk:** alternating running and walking by time or segment as a starting structure; the NHS Couch to 5K first week pairs 1 min running with 1.5 min walking. See the [run-walk tool](/en/tools/run-walk/).
- **RTS (return to sport):** the international ankle-sprain consensus defines this as being sanctioned for unrestricted training and cleared or available for competition selection; it is not the same as being able to jog two laps.

This page covers beginner rebuilding, return after common overuse injuries, and recovery after layoff of several weeks. Acute trauma, post-surgery, joint swelling and neurological symptoms need professional assessment, covered in the next section. Pain-versus-load judgment is in [Pain and load](/en/knowledge/pain-and-load/); volume arrangement is in [Training volume and progression](/en/knowledge/training-volume-and-progression/).

## When to see a clinician first, rather than self-returning

The following are not "ease back gradually" situations; get assessed first:

- Severe pain, marked swelling, joint instability or repeated giving way;
- Numbness, tingling, weakness, abnormal skin colour or temperature;
- Pain that does not settle with rest, or persistent night pain;
- Pain after a fall or impact, or suspected fracture or ligament injury;
- Symptoms that worsen on return, or a second recurrence at the same site.

NHS gives injury-specific thresholds: severe knee pain or swelling warrants an immediate GP visit; severe shin pain, swelling or no improvement after a few weeks warrants a GP or physiotherapist. Clinical conclusions, imaging results and rehabilitation restrictions take priority over the general stage table here.

## Acute handling: PEACE and LOVE

The BJSM 2020 PEACE and LOVE framework carries soft-tissue care from immediate management (PEACE) through subsequent management (LOVE), replacing the earlier ICE/RICE/PRICE/POLICE acronyms:

- **Protect:** unload or restrict movement for 1–3 days to minimize bleeding and fibre distension; rest should be minimized because prolonged rest compromises tissue strength and quality, and pain signals guide when to stop protection.
- **Elevate:** raise the limb above heart level to promote fluid flow; evidence for elevation is weak but its risk-to-benefit ratio is low.
- **Avoid anti-inflammatory modalities:** the editorial notes that inhibiting inflammation may negatively affect long-term tissue healing, especially at higher doses, and questions cryotherapy — there is no high-quality evidence that ice treats soft-tissue injuries. This is an editorial position that differs from some traditional advice; it is recorded side by side here, not averaged.
- **Compress:** taping or bandages limit oedema and haemorrhage; after an ankle sprain compression seems to reduce swelling and improve quality of life.
- **Educate:** early passive modalities such as electrotherapy, manual therapy or acupuncture have insignificant effects on pain and function compared with an active approach and may be counterproductive long term; realistic recovery expectations should be set.
- **Load:** add mechanical stress early and resume normal activities as soon as symptoms allow; optimal loading without exacerbating pain promotes repair, remodelling and tissue tolerance.
- **Vascularisation:** pain-free aerobic exercise starts a few days after injury to boost motivation and blood flow.
- **Exercise:** strong evidence supports exercise for treating ankle sprains and reducing recurrence, restoring mobility, strength and proprioception; pain guides subacute progression.

Note: PEACE and LOVE is a soft-tissue management framework and does not replace clinical judgment for fracture, intra-articular injury or neurological injury.

## Return stages and gate table

| Stage | Content (then) | Gate to the next stage | Roll-back rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0. Triage and acute phase | Pain-free daily activities; PEACE/LOVE care; clinician clearance when needed | Pain-free walking, no marked swelling, professional permission if required | Red-flag symptoms mean clinical care; do not enter run-walk |
| 1. Run-walk start | 1 min run / 1.5–2 min walk, 20–30 min, 2–3 times per week, rest between sessions | Mild or no pain during; no worsening or swelling next morning and within 24 h; normal gait | Worsening pain or poor next-day response: roll back a stage or rest |
| 2. Longer run bouts | Gradually raise run-bout ratio (e.g. 3–4 min run / 1–2 min walk), about 30 min total | Same dose completed 2–3 times in a row with normal response; no limp | Repeated discomfort at the same dose: do not lengthen run bouts |
| 3. Easy continuous run | 20–30 min easy continuous running; pace allowing full sentences | Next-day recovery after easy runs; no same-site recurrence pain | Pace drop, gait change or pain returns: roll back to run-walk |
| 4. Extend and structure | 30–45 min easy run, gradually restore weekly mileage; add strength and proprioception work | A full session completed pain-free; functional tests (hop, agility, sport-specific) near symmetric side to side | Mid-late session symptoms: cut back and reassess |
| 5. Unrestricted training | Resume quality sessions, long runs and sport-specific work; matches RTS assessment | Pain during sport and over the last 24 h controlled; adequate subjective confidence and stability; passes sport-specific functional tests | Any RTS criterion unmet: hold at the current stage |

Stage timing varies by individual and is not driven by the calendar. The Couch to 5K run-walk structure (3 runs per week with rest between, building over 9 weeks or longer to 30 min continuous) is a beginner reference, but post-injury return is usually slower.

## Worked example: returning after a two-week layoff (teaching assumption)

Assumption: an experienced runner with a mild overuse complaint rested two weeks, now walks pain-free with no swelling, and has been cleared to resume; pre-injury easy weekly mileage was about 30 km.

- **Week 1 (stages 1–2):** 3 run-walk sessions of 20–30 min each; total running time about 30–40 min, equivalent to 40%–50% of pre-injury mileage (about 12–15 km equivalent), not back to 30 km.
- **Week 2 (stage 3):** 2–3 easy continuous runs of 20–30 min; if the next-day response is normal twice, raise weekly mileage modestly, keeping the increment conservative.
- **Week 3 (stage 4):** one 30–45 min easy run, other sessions kept easy; add strength and proprioception work.
- **Progression check:** after each session record pain site, severity, next-day response and gait; only extend duration or weekly mileage after the same dose repeats normally 2–3 times.

The numbers are a teaching assumption, not a prescription. After a longer layoff, at older age, with recurrent prior injuries, or without clinical assessment, the starting dose should be lower. The relation between mileage and longest run is in [Training volume and progression](/en/knowledge/training-volume-and-progression/); plan structure is in [Marathon training plan](/en/knowledge/marathon-training-plan/).

## Reinjury criteria: signals that mean roll back a stage

| Signal | Meaning | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moderate or worse pain during exercise, at the same injured site | Current dose exceeds tissue tolerance | Stop the session, roll back a stage; observe 24 h |
| Pain or stiffness worse next morning or within 24 h after exercise | Insufficient recovery or excessive load | Do not increase; rest or cross-train; recurrent cases see a clinician |
| Limp, altered gait, one-sided avoidance | Compensated movement control | Roll back to walk-run or walking assessment; check strength and range |
| Local swelling, heat, worsening bruising | Uncontrolled acute inflammatory response | Stop running, seek clinical assessment |
| Marked side-to-side asymmetry in hopping, landing or cutting, or fear of loading | Sport-specific function not restored | No quality sessions; add strength and proprioception, retest symmetry |
| Low subjective confidence, fear of reinjury | Psychological readiness not met | Hold the current stage; do not push intensity through fear |

The PAASS international ankle-sprain consensus dimensions are a reference for return decisions: pain during sport and over the last 24 h, ankle impairments, athlete perception (confidence, stability, psychological readiness), sensorimotor control, and sport/functional performance (hopping, agility, sport-specific activities, completing a full training session). The consensus excludes ligament structural integrity on imaging and ligamentous laxity from the RTS decision: an imaging look that "hasn't healed" alone cannot decide return; function and symptoms are the gates.

## Training increments and the "10% rule": conflicting evidence side by side

The running community commonly uses "do not raise weekly mileage by more than 10%", but the evidence for this rule is inconsistent. Two study positions are presented side by side here, not averaged:

- **RCT showing no preventive effect:** Buist 2008 (GRONORUN trial) randomized 532 novice runners to a 13-week graded programme based on the 10% rule or a standard 8-week programme. Running-related injury incidence was 20.8% in the graded group and 20.3% in the standard group (χ² = 0.016, p = .90). The trial concluded the graded 10%-rule programme had no effect on injury incidence compared with the shorter standard programme.
- **Cohort trend for high increments:** Nielsen 2014 included 873 novice runners grouped by weekly distance progression: under 10% (or regression), 10%–30%, and over 30%. No statistically significant injury-rate difference appeared across the three groups for all injuries; distance-related injuries were more frequent in the over-30% group than the under-10% group at HR 1.59 (95% CI 0.96–2.66; p = .07), not significant overall. The authors' cautious advice was that novices may be well advised to progress weekly distance by less than 30% over a two-week period.
- **Review position:** a 2022 systematic review of 36 studies and 23,047 runners (abstract only accessible) found the evidence linking weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity or recent changes to injury conflicting, and cautioned against recommending one optimal progression for every runner.

Synthesis: 10% is a conservative, easy-to-use heuristic, not an RCT-fixed safety threshold. Large single jumps (especially over 30%) trend toward risk in some injury types, but the safe increment varies by population, injury history and baseline fitness. In the return period, err slow and make every dose repeatable; increment method is in [Training volume and progression](/en/knowledge/training-volume-and-progression/).

## Injury-prevention training: strength, proprioception and stretching evidence

Lauersen 2014's systematic review and meta-analysis of 25 RCTs, 26,610 participants and 3,464 injuries gives injury risk ratios (RR) by intervention:

| Intervention | RR (95% CI) | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strength training | 0.315 (0.207–0.480) | Injury risk reduced to about one third; no heterogeneity across the four studies |
| Proprioception training | 0.550 (0.347–0.869) | Protective effect |
| Multi-exposure preventive programmes | 0.655 (0.520–0.826) | Combined programmes reduce risk |
| Stretching | 0.963 (0.846–1.095) | Three studies showed no injury-prevention benefit |
| All preventive programmes: acute / overuse injury | 0.647 (0.502–0.836) / 0.527 (0.373–0.746) | Exercise programmes lower both injury types |

Treat strength and proprioception as stage content during the return, not something added only after running resumes. Strength work arrangement is in [Strength training](/en/knowledge/strength-training-for-runners/). Note: the review is a prevention-population analysis and does not set a mandatory number of sets for returning runners; the specific programme follows the injury type, arranged by a physiotherapist or coach.

## Psychological readiness and sport-specific functional tests

RTS is not only whether the body can run. In the PAASS consensus, perceived reassurance/confidence (81% agreement), perceived stability (74%) and psychological readiness (74%) are decision items; imaging structure and laxity are excluded. In practice, before quality sessions or racing, complete a full easy training session with symptoms controlled; compare bilateral hopping, landing and cutting; confirm stability on uncertain ground and pace. Both fear avoidance and overconfidence need adjustment — the former limits recovery, the latter pushes people through injury.

Training volume and performance are a reference: Fokkema 2020's questionnaire study of 556 half-marathon and 441 marathon participants found half-marathon runners with weekly volume over 32 km and a longest endurance run over 21 km finished faster; the study found no association between training volume or longest run and running injuries. This is observational and cannot be reversed into "chase volume during the return".

## Failure modes and early signals

- **Making up mileage after layoff in one session:** early signals are worse pain or heavy legs that evening or next day. Rule: start week one at a low dose; do not compare to pre-injury mileage.
- **Adding intensity before pain fully settles:** early signals are easy running OK but pain appears as soon as pace rises. Rule: quality sessions follow stable easy runs and functional tests.
- **Running only, no strength or proprioception:** early signals are recurring same-site discomfort and unstable landing. Rule: schedule strength and balance work alongside the return.
- **Replacing strength training with stretching:** early signals are persistent fatigue and poor joint control. Rule: stretching showed no injury-prevention benefit in the review; prevention rests on strength and proprioception.
- **Ignoring subjective fear and asymmetry:** early signals are hesitation on jumps and unwillingness to bear weight on one leg. Rule: psychological readiness and side-to-side symmetry enter the gates; do not push intensity through fear.
- **Self-escalating after repeated recurrence:** early signals are discomfort at the same site twice or more. Rule: return to professional assessment; adjust training structure and shoes/gear; see [Running injury prevention](/en/knowledge/running-injury-prevention/).

## Applicable boundaries

This page is training and rehabilitation education, not a diagnosis, treatment plan, imaging interpretation or medication advice. The stage table and worked example are a general framework and cannot replace clinical judgment specific to the injury type, imaging and physical examination. RCTs, cohort studies, systematic reviews and consensus frameworks are flagged separately by evidence type. This site does not perform examination, issue rehabilitation clearance, or replace emergency judgment.

## What would change this advice

- **Injury type and severity:** bone injury, tendinopathy, ligament injury and neurological symptoms follow different return rhythms; professional assessment takes priority over the general stage table.
- **Layoff length and baseline level:** longer layoff and lower baseline mean a lower starting dose; novices and experienced runners tolerate different loads.
- **Age, recovery and sleep:** recovery capacity, sleep, energy intake and prior injury history all change progression speed.
- **Evidence update:** return-to-sport criteria, increment thresholds and prevention interventions remain under study; the page adjusts if later high-quality RCTs, consensus or guidelines differ.
- **Disease and medication:** underlying conditions or drugs affect healing and load response; clinical assessment takes priority over the general figures here.
