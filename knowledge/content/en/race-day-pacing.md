## What this page covers

This page is about **how pace is distributed on race day**, from the gun to the finish. It is for marathon and half-marathon runners who already have a target time, and for first-timers who simply want an even race. It does **not** apply to trail races, ultramarathons or events where pace must follow terrain and aid stations — those need a different logic.

Pacing patterns usually fall into four types: even (very little difference between halves), mild positive (a controlled late slowdown), strong positive (a pronounced late slowdown) and variable (large fluctuations). Both studies below analyse finishers at large city marathons. They are observational: they show which patterns appear alongside which outcomes, not that switching to even pacing will necessarily make you faster.

## The four patterns

| Pattern | Boston Marathon share (2015–2017, 78,912 runners) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Even | 47.9% | Pace broadly consistent throughout |
| Mild positive | 31.9% | Controlled slowing in the second half |
| Strong positive | 12.2% | Marked slowing in the second half |
| Variable | 8.0% | Large pace fluctuations |

The study classified runners by the ratio of first-half to second-half time and by segment-to-segment pace variation, then clustered them into four groups. These percentages describe that field, not every runner everywhere.

## Pacing and outcome — correlation is not causation

| Data source | Even | Mild or negative | Strong positive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boston 2015–2017 | 217 minutes average | — | 276 minutes average |
| Chicago 2005–2016 | 242 minutes average | 256 minutes for negative splitters | 280 minutes for positive splitters |

The absolute times differ because the courses, weather and fields differ, but the direction is consistent: even splitters finished faster on average. The fastest runners varied by less than 5% between halves; the Chicago analysis treated a half-to-half difference under 2% as even.

The authors are explicit that correlation is not causation. You cannot conclude that forcing an even split guarantees a better time. A better reading is that even pacing tends to reflect good energy distribution and a conservative start, and both have value in their own right.

## Is a negative split optimal? The literature does not agree

"Negative split is best" is close to common sense in running circles, but the evidence is more specific: **record races are close to even, championship races often show negative splits, and the main amateur problem is not a missing negative split but starting too fast.** Three layers:

> **Two positions side by side (no averaging).**
>
> - **"Even/negative split first" position:** the two large observational analyses — Boston 78,912 finishers and Chicago 2005–2016 — show even or mildly negative splitters finishing faster on average; source type: post-race observational split analysis, not a randomized controlled trial.
> - **"Positive splitting may suit elites with pacers" position:** Fernandes and Maldonado 2023 use an energetic model plus CFD simulation; with drafting behind pacers, positive splitting can be fastest; source type: non-peer-reviewed preprint model, applicable only to elite races with pacers.
>
> The two sides differ in population and evidence type and cannot be averaged into "everyone should negative split".

| Context | Observation | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Men's marathon world records (1998–2023, 12 races) | Fastest first 5 km, then close to even; pace CV 1.52% ± 0.54% | Pacers and a time trial |
| Women's marathon world records (8 races) | Close to even; CV 1.61% ± 0.72% | Same |
| Men's world/Olympic champions (14 races) | More negative; CV 2.91% ± 0.75% | Position matters, tactics vary |
| Women's world/Olympic champions (14 races) | More negative with a fast finish; CV 3.18% ± 0.88% | Same |

The difference is the goal, not professionalism: record attempts reward even pace behind pacers, while championships reward a fast last 2 km. Casado et al. (2024) describe men's records as a fast first 5 km followed by even pacing, and men's championships as more negative; because the distributions differ, training should be specific to the target event.

Counter-evidence belongs here too. Fernandes and Maldonado (2023, arXiv preprint, not peer reviewed) modelled elite racing with drafting and found that **positive splitting** can be fastest when a pacer is present: the protected phase 2.4%–2.6% faster than the unprotected phase. That is a simulation, not a causal trial, and applies only to elite races with pacers — but it shows there is no consensus that negative splitting is optimal.

For amateurs, the measurable cost comes from the two observational analyses above: Chicago 2005–2016 averaged 280 minutes for positive splitters and 242 for even; Boston 2015–2017 averaged 276 and 217. Ability, weather and course are mixed in, so this is not purely a pacing effect, but the direction and size are consistent.

### How to arrange it: keep the half-to-half difference within 2%

Chicago treated a difference under 2% as even. That is a **classification threshold**, not an optimal target; for most amateurs it is the realistic execution band.

- With target time `T`, aim for a first half of about `T/2 × 1.00–1.02` and a second half of `T/2 × 0.98–1.00`.
- For 3:30, that is 1:45–1:47 and 1:43–1:45.
- Rather than chasing a faster second half, first stop the first half from being too fast; clear negative splits are rare among amateurs.

### Hilly courses: adjust by feel, not by pace

Minetti et al. (2002) measured the metabolic cost of running from −45% to +45% grade and found an **asymmetric** curve: the extra cost of a given uphill is larger than the saving from the same downhill. In practice:

- Let uphill splits be slower, controlled by feel or heart rate; do not accelerate to defend a flat-course pace.
- Do not sprint downhill, especially on net-downhill starts such as Boston; the first 5 km can feel free and be paid for after 30 km.
- Re-estimate the target from equivalent climb rather than copying a flat PB. There is no official seconds-per-100 m conversion (**unknown**); the site's grade-adjusted tool is an estimate, not a standard.

### Heat: move the target from PB pace to 93%–95% of PB pace

Beal et al. (2022) analysed the 2019 Doha women's World Championships: 32.0°C, 74% relative humidity, with 40 of 68 starters finishing (28 non-finishers, 41%), against 15% at London 2017. The top 10 started at **93.7%** of personal-best pace; places 21–40 started at **96.6%**. Groups did not differ before 15 km; the slower group then faded first.

- Set the opening target at 93%–95% of PB pace and take another 2%–3% off the first half, instead of "run PB pace and see".
- Pace is no longer the main metric: heart rate drifts with heat and dehydration (see [heart-rate drift](/en/knowledge/heart-rate-drift/)), so use feel, breathing and cooling points.
- Non-finishers matched finishers to 10 km, so "the start feels fine" does not predict completion; fluid, cooling and heat acclimation matter as much.

**Unknown:** there is no randomised trial linking negative splits to better times, no universal half-to-half target, and no official grade-to-pace conversion. All figures above are observational or modelled, not individual prescriptions.

## Two definitions of "the wall"

| Source | Definition | Observation |
|---|---|---|
| Boston study (PLOS ONE, 2026) | A pace decline of more than 20% in the final segment | 85.4% of the variable-pacing group hit the wall |
| Chicago analysis (World Athletics, 2016) | At least 33% slower in the second half than the first | About half of wall-hitters ran their fastest 5 km at the start |

Because the thresholds differ, wall rates cannot be compared across the two studies. What they share is that the wall is not sudden: it is usually built on going out too fast, glycogen depletion and dehydration. The Boston paper's introduction attributes the wall mainly to depletion of muscle and liver glycogen, forcing greater reliance on fat oxidation, which cannot sustain the same speed.

## The first 5 km — the most expensive minutes

Chicago produced a concrete comparison: about 50% of runners who hit the wall ran their first 5 km as their fastest segment. Conversely, runners whose opening 5 km was their slowest **never** hit the wall and finished on average about 50 minutes faster than the fast starters. That does not mean slower is always better; it means the opening minutes are amplified later.

In the Boston data, 82.0% of runners finishing under 3:00 used even pacing, compared with 19.6% of casual participants. That does not prove that even pacing gets you under 3:00. It more likely shows that better-trained runners are better able to hold a pace.

## Pacing groups and packs

The Chicago analysis found that runners running with or near a pacing group were about **five times** more likely to produce even splits. That is a strong association, but selection bias remains: people who choose a pacing group may already be more organised and more disciplined. For a first marathon, following a group slightly slower than your estimated ability is usually steadier than chasing a time alone.

The same analysis found that men were 3–4 times more likely than women to hit the wall; that women produced better even splits for a given finish time beyond 150 minutes; and that repeat entrants were older, paced more evenly and finished faster — about 19% of the 2005–2016 Chicago field had run the race more than once.

## Corrals, bag drop and toilets: rules from the race, principles from you

None of these is standardised; **everything depends on the current race instructions**. The New York City Marathon rules below are an example of what to check, not a universal standard.

| Item | NYC Marathon practice (example) | General principle |
|---|---|---|
| Corral assignment | Based on the best pace in the NYRR account or the estimated finish submitted at registration | Corrals usually follow a qualifying time; do not inflate a time to enter a faster wave |
| Corral closing | 2026 wave closings at 8:45, 9:25, 10:00, 10:40 and 11:15 | Closing is before the gun; check your own wave |
| Arrival | Arrive at least 60 minutes early | Big-city races need time for security, finding the corral and queues |
| Entering the corral | Enter at least 10 minutes before the start | Do not leave it to the last minute; a closed corral may send you to the back |
| Bag drop | Must be selected in advance; pre-check closes at 17:00 the day before; collection until 19:00 | Bag-drop only post-race dry clothes; keep ID, phone and essentials with you |
| Toilets | Portable toilets at the start and on course, but queues vary | Go once on arrival and once 15–20 minutes before the start; exact capacity and queues are **unknown** |

Three practical rules:

1. **Read the instructions 2–3 days out and write a countdown card**: arrival, security, bag drop, toilet, corral and gun, each with its own margin, working backwards from the published closing times.
2. **Do not jump corrals.** A faster corral does not make you faster; it forces you to chase in the first 5 km, spending the most expensive minutes of the race.
3. **Plan two toilet visits**: one on arrival, one before the corral. Drink less in the final hour but do not dehydrate; queue length cannot be predicted, so arriving early is the only reliable control.

## What to do — five race-day steps

1. **Set the target from a recent result.** Use a recent half-marathon or 10 km plus your long-run performances, not the time you hope for. The target pace should be one you have repeated in training.
2. **Break it into 5 km segments.** Convert the target into a range for each 5 km and write down a "fastest opening limit" — for example 5–10 seconds per kilometre slower than target. That is a practice convention, not a research finding.
3. **Run the first 5 km by feel.** Do not chase people who pass you. The aim of the opening is to be slightly slower than target, not to win back time.
4. **Check in at 10–15 km.** Can you still hold a conversation, are the legs easy, is heart rate controllable? If it already feels hard, take 5–10 seconds per kilometre off the target.
5. **Only accelerate after 30 km if you have reserves.** If the legs feel heavy and pace is slipping, take carbohydrate and fluid, shorten your stride, and do not fight it. Once wall symptoms appear, forcing the pace usually makes the second half slower.

## Decision table: choosing race-day pacing by condition

| Condition (if) | Action (then) | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flat, cool, and target pace repeatable in training | First 5 km 5–10 s/km slower, then even to target | Observational analyses show association, not causation |
| Hot and humid (around 32°C-type race) | Drop target to 93%–95% of PB pace, another 2%–3% off the first half | Based on one women's World Championships analysis; not extrapolated to every hot race |
| Net-downhill start or hilly course | Do not defend flat pace uphill; do not sprint downhill; hold the start by feel | Grade metabolic cost is asymmetric; no official seconds-per-climb standard |
| A pacing group matches the goal and your plan is settled | Follow a group slightly slower than ability; selection bias remains | Group association with even splits is strong, not causal |
| Already struggling at 10–15 km | Take 5–10 s/km off the target, prioritize finishing | This page does not make medical judgments for you |

## A worked example (hypothetical)

Target 3:30, average pace about 4:59 per kilometre (299 seconds per kilometre). One conservative split example:

| Segment | Pace | Time |
|---|---|---|
| First 5 km | 5:07 per km | 25:35 |
| 5–40 km | 4:59 per km | 2:54:25 |
| Final 2.195 km | 4:52 per km | 10:41 |
| Total | — | 3:30:01 |

This example only shows the shape — slower start, slightly quicker finish — and is not a prediction. Real races also depend on gradient, weather, fuelling and how you feel on the day. If the first 5 km gets pulled faster by the crowd, the later segments have to make up more.

## Two weeks out: rehearsal and 5-km re-checks

- In the long run 2-3 weeks before the race, include one 8-12 km block at goal pace in the middle of the run to rehearse the race-day start. In race week, do only a short 3-5 km confirmation and do not add volume. This is a site recommendation, not a research finding.
- Review once a week: record each 5-km split against goal pace, change only one variable (start, fuelling or route), and see whether the deviation shrinks or grows.
- On race day, re-check three things every 5 km: split pace versus goal, whether RPE matches the pace, and whether you can still drink and fuel normally. If two of the three are off, slow down or change the goal rather than chasing time.

| Checkpoint | What to look at | Allowed deviation | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 km | Whether the start has pulled you faster | Goal ±2% | Return to goal pace without braking hard; do not chase if slower |
| 10 km | Whether RPE matches pace | Goal ±2% | If breathing is ragged, stabilise pace first, not fuelling volume |
| 15 km | Heart-rate drift, weather, hills | Goal ±2% | In heat, reset to 93%-95% of PB pace instead of forcing the original goal |
| Halfway | Whether the first half is in the planned range | Half-to-half difference ≤2% | If more than 2% slow, check fuelling and course before surging |
| 30-35 km | Whether form is breaking and pace is dropping | More than 5% slow | Switch to finish mode and drop the time goal |
| 40 km | Whether you can still hold form | — | Only lift pace if form is stable; otherwise hold |

Worked arithmetic: a 3:30 goal is 5:00/km. If the first 5 km is 2% fast, you save about 30 seconds; spread over the remaining 37.195 km, that is only about 0.8 seconds per kilometre to recover. The problem is not that one split but treating "2% fast" as normal and continuing to add it at 10 km and 15 km — the observed cost of amateur positive splitting is 38-59 minutes and comes from a sustained positive split, not one quick start. This conversion is our own arithmetic from the goal time, not a research finding.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A bigger negative split is more professional."** Genuine negative splits are rare and require accurate fitness assessment; for most runners, even or mild positive pacing is more realistic.
- **"Following the person ahead is free speed."** Chicago data show fast starters are more likely to hit the wall; being overtaken is not the same as losing.
- **"The wall is purely a willpower problem."** Research links it to glycogen depletion, dehydration and going out too fast; it is not something to grit through.
- **"A pacing group guarantees a personal best."** Following a group is strongly associated with even splits but involves selection bias, and the group's splits may not match your target.
- **"Boston data apply to every course."** Boston opens with a net downhill and includes famous climbs, while Chicago is known for being flat; pacing distributions do not transfer directly.
- **"Race day is a good time to try new shoes or new fuel."** That adds untested variables, which is a separate problem from pacing.

## Stop signals and medical help

- Chest pain or tightness, fainting or near-fainting;
- Confusion, unsteady gait, cold clammy skin or a very high body temperature (treat as heat illness and call emergency services first);
- Severe vomiting or diarrhoea, or inability to keep taking fluids;
- Pain that causes a limp or prevents weight-bearing;
- When any of these appear, the finish time no longer matters. Stop and seek on-course medical help.

## Checklist

- [ ] Is the target pace based on a recent result rather than a wish?
- [ ] Have I written down each 5 km range and a fastest opening limit?
- [ ] Am I ready to let people pass in the first 5 km?
- [ ] Have I planned a check-in at 10–15 km?
- [ ] Have I rehearsed the fuelling and cooling plan for after 30 km?
- [ ] Will I accept a slower target if the day goes badly?

## Limits

This page rests on two observational analyses: 78,912 Boston Marathon finishers from 2015–2017 and Chicago Marathon data from 2005–2016. They cannot establish causation between pacing pattern and outcome, and they do not transfer directly to half marathons or trail races. The elite negative-split data come from post-race split analyses of world records and championships; the heat section comes from one women's World Championship analysis and does not transfer directly to amateur races. Every split example here is hypothetical, not a personal prescription. This page **does not give medical diagnosis** and does not replace a clinician's face-to-face assessment; runners with cardiovascular disease, a history of exercise-related fainting or heat illness should race under medical guidance.

## What would change this advice

- New randomized trials or larger, stricter observational studies that change the direction or size of the association between even/mildly negative splits and finish outcome would move this page's guidance.
- Changes in your own training state, weather and course: heat, a net-downhill start or hilly terrain call for the target to be reset per the decision table, not copied from training-day pace.
- Updated institutional conclusions on hot-race pace reduction and grade-to-pace conversion; the current 93%–95% figure comes from one women's World Championships analysis, and no official grade-seconds standard exists.
- When stop signals such as chest pain, fainting or serious heat illness appear, this pacing advice yields to on-course medical care.

## Put handoffs into race-day pacing

The race-day pace plan must include handoff and gear-change time. After a pacer takes over, the goal is not to chase lost time but to hold the agreed rhythm from [ultra crew and pacer handoff](/en/knowledge/ultra-crew-pacer-handoff/). Ten minutes lost at a handoff must be paid back against the cut-off.
