## Definitions and scope

Menopause usually affects women between 45 and 55, but it can happen earlier. Perimenopause means you have menopausal symptoms but your periods have not stopped. Menopause is reached after 12 consecutive months without a period. Symptoms often begin years before the final period and can continue afterwards.

It affects anyone who has periods and can be natural or follow surgery to remove the ovaries or uterus, chemotherapy, or a genetic cause. Hormonal contraception can mask both periods and symptoms, so the stage is sometimes uncertain.

This page is for runners in perimenopause or after menopause, and for coaches who plan their training. It is not a diagnostic tool. Unexplained chest pain, fainting, new palpitations or severe breathlessness should be assessed before training is discussed.

## Three timelines: perimenopause, menopause, symptoms

| Stage | Definition | What it means for training |
|---|---|---|
| Perimenopause | Menopausal symptoms are present but periods have not stopped; symptoms may start years earlier | How you feel can differ from day to day, so training has to follow symptoms |
| Menopause | 12 consecutive months without a period | The period of accelerated bone loss has begun; strength and impact work matter more |
| Postmenopause | Symptoms can persist; joint pain and vaginal dryness may continue | Long-term bone, muscle and cardiovascular health become the main thread |

Perimenopause is not simply "the few months before the last period". The NHS states that symptoms can last months or years and change over time: hot flushes and night sweats may improve, then low mood and anxiety may appear. A fixed 12-week plan that ignores symptoms is usually a poor fit.

## How symptoms change a session

At least six common symptoms listed by the NHS directly affect running.

| Symptom | NHS description | Practical effect on training | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hot flushes and night sweats | Sudden hot or cold feelings in the face, neck and chest, sometimes with dizziness | Layer clothing, avoid the hottest part of the day, extend the warm-up | Stop if dizziness, cold sweats or near-fainting occurs |
| Difficulty sleeping | Night sweats can leave you tired and irritable during the day | Replace a hard session with an easy run or shorten the distance | Persistent insomnia affecting daytime function should be assessed |
| Palpitations | Heartbeat suddenly becomes more noticeable | First check caffeine, sleep debt, medicines and dehydration | Chest pain, breathlessness, fainting or near-fainting is an emergency |
| Joint and muscle pain | Common, and may continue after periods stop | Reduce consecutive impact days; keep strength work | One-sided redness, heat, swelling, inability to bear weight or night pain should be assessed |
| Low mood, anxiety, brain fog | Mood swings, low mood, memory and concentration problems | Lower expectations for pace and PBs; protect sleep routines | If it affects work or daily life, or involves thoughts of self-harm, seek help promptly |
| Recurrent UTIs, vaginal dryness | May recur during this stage | Clean up and change into dry clothing after training | Recurrent infection or pain should follow NHS care pathways |

This table is not a symptom-to-workout prescription. It makes one point: the variables in menopause training are not only pace and mileage, but also sleep, temperature regulation and recovery.

## What exercise can and cannot do

This is the section most often misread, so the evidence comes first.

The 2014 Cochrane review included five randomised trials with 762 women who had hot flushes or night sweats. Three trials with 454 women compared exercise with no active treatment; the pooled effect was SMD −0.10 (95% CI −0.33 to 0.13), low-quality evidence. Two trials with 279 women compared exercise with yoga; the pooled effect was SMD −0.03 (95% CI −0.45 to 0.38), also low-quality. The conclusion was that **there is no evidence that exercise reduces hot flushes or night sweats**.

One small trial of 14 women compared exercise with hormone therapy. The hormone therapy group reported significantly fewer flushes in 24 hours, with a mean difference of 5.8 (95% CI 3.17 to 8.43). The sample is far too small for personal decisions, but it does not support the idea that exercise replaces hormone therapy for hot flushes.

Why does the NHS still list exercise? Because the goal is broader than hot flushes. The NHS recommends regular exercise to help mood, sleep and general health, and explicitly lists weight-bearing and resistance exercise for bone protection. Cochrane found no evidence for exercise as a treatment for vasomotor symptoms; it did not find that exercise is worthless. Those are different statements.

The practical conclusion: **do not use running as a treatment for hot flushes. If flushes are disrupting sleep and daytime function, discuss options, including hormone therapy, with a clinician.** Exercise is still worth doing, but its main returns are bone, muscle, cardiovascular fitness and mood, not "running the flushes away".

## Bone: why these five to seven years matter

A 2025 systematic review reports that bone loss can begin before menopause and continues at 3% to 5% per year in the first five to seven years after menopause. The NHS puts it more simply: women lose bone rapidly in the first few years after menopause. Risk is higher when menopause begins before age 45 or after removal of the ovaries.

Two implications follow.

1. **Bone health is not a post-menopause project.** If you are in perimenopause, this is the window to build strength and impact habits.
2. **Running does not automatically equal bone health.** Running is weight-bearing, but bone density also depends on strength training, calcium and vitamin D, smoking, alcohol, body weight and medicines. The NHS lists not exercising regularly as a risk factor, alongside low BMI, long-term steroid tablets, some conditions and family history.

Bone density is reported as a T score: above −1 SD is normal, −1 to −2.5 SD is osteopenia, and below −2.5 SD is osteoporosis. A DEXA scan usually takes 10 to 20 minutes. The T score is one part of risk assessment, not a self-diagnosis or a reason to start or stop medication on your own.

## Exercise dose: count impact and strength separately

The Royal Osteoporosis Society (ROS) gives specific doses. They target bone health, not running performance.

| Goal | ROS recommendation | How a runner can apply it |
|---|---|---|
| Impact | Without a history of spinal fractures, build up to 50 moderate impacts on most days; one jump counts as one impact, and impacts can be spread through the day | Jogging is listed by ROS as a moderate-impact example. If you already run, count the run toward the day's impact total rather than adding 50 jumps on top |
| Low-impact alternative | With spinal fractures or many broken bones, build up to 20 minutes of low-impact exercise on most days | Confirm which movements are safe with a doctor or physiotherapist |
| Strength | 2 to 3 days per week, 20 to 30 minutes, 3 sets of 8 to 12 repetitions; each session covers hinge, push, pull and squat patterns | Separate strength days from long runs; start with body weight or bands and add load when 12 good repetitions are easy |
| Aerobic | The NHS recommends regular weight-bearing activity such as walking, running or dancing | Easy running and walking still count; not every session needs to be fast |

ROS also advises talking to a doctor or physiotherapist before starting if you have had spinal fractures or many broken bones, are recovering from a fracture, or have questions about your own situation. This is not a formality: impact loading may not be appropriate for an already fragile vertebra.

A sample week (illustration, not a prescription):

| Day | Session | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Mon | Easy run 30–40 minutes | Aerobic, low load |
| Tue | Strength A (hinge, push, pull, squat) | Bone and muscle |
| Wed | Rest or 20-minute walk | Recovery |
| Thu | Easy run plus 4–6 short hills | Small dose of intensity, adjusted to symptoms |
| Fri | Strength B (different movements from A) | Bone and muscle |
| Sat | Long run or brisk walk | Endurance |
| Sun | Complete rest | Sleep first |

On a day with heavy flushes, night sweats or joint pain, replace the intensity session with an easy run or walk. Do not treat the plan as a punishment for having symptoms.

## Race day and symptoms

Symptoms do not pause for a race. Practical preparation includes checking the forecast and applying hot-weather hydration and cooling principles; layering clothes so you can remove a layer after the warm-up; packing a spare bra, sweatband and dry clothes; and rehearsing in training how you adjust pace on a bad-symptom day rather than trying it for the first time in a race.

Do not stake a personal best on a "good symptom day". A more realistic target is completion and perceived effort, especially during a period of poor sleep or frequent flushes.

Stop and seek medical help during a race if you develop chest pain, marked breathlessness, near-fainting or an unusual palpitation. That is not a test of willpower.

## When to seek medical help

**Emergency**: palpitations with chest pain, breathlessness, feeling faint or fainting require emergency help under NHS guidance. Do not drive yourself.

**Prompt GP review**: any vaginal bleeding after menopause, even a single small episode or spotting, should be reviewed by a GP. The NHS says such cases should be referred and should not wait more than two weeks for a specialist. A breast that is red, hot and swollen with a high temperature, a hard lump that does not move, nipple discharge that may be bloody, a change in breast shape, orange-peel skin dimpling, or a rash or inverted nipple should also follow urgent NHS pathways.

**Routine review**: hot flushes or night sweats that disrupt sleep and work; low mood, anxiety or brain fog that affects daily life; joint pain that limits weight-bearing; recurrent UTIs; palpitations that recur, worsen, or last more than a few minutes.

**General safety note**: this page does not diagnose, does not assess whether you need hormone therapy, and gives no medicine, dose or supplement advice. Hormone therapy is a medical decision, and risks vary by formulation and route. The review's points about starting within 10 years of menopause and about different progestins are context for a discussion with a clinician, not a self-prescribed plan.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Menopause always means getting slower and avoiding intensity"**: symptoms vary widely. Without unevaluated cardiovascular symptoms, intensity can stay, but it should follow sleep and symptoms on the day.
- **"High mileage guarantees strong bones"**: running is weight-bearing, but bone density also depends on strength training, nutrition, body weight, smoking, alcohol and medicines. High mileage is not high bone density by default.
- **"Exercise can replace hormone therapy for hot flushes"**: Cochrane found no evidence that exercise reduces hot flushes, and a 14-woman trial favoured hormone therapy. Medication decisions belong with a clinician.
- **"Sweating a lot proves the session worked"**: hot-flush sweating is a vasomotor symptom, not a reliable measure of training intensity.
- **"Calcium is enough"**: the NHS also emphasises weight-bearing and resistance exercise, vitamin D, stopping smoking and limiting alcohol.
- **"Strength training can wait until after menopause"**: bone loss can begin before menopause; perimenopause is the window.
