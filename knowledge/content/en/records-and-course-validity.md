## What this page covers, and when it applies

This page covers the conditions for a running record, course certification and timing conventions, and the difference between a personal PB and an official record. It assumes you want to judge whether a result can be compared or called a record; this page does not rule on whether a particular race or result has been approved by a governing body. Event distances and categories are in [Marathon distance and events](/en/knowledge/marathon-distance/); a route's altitude, surface and environment are covered on its route page.

## A record is a recognised result, not the fastest number

The World Athletics record system classifies results by event, sex and category, with open records marked Mx and women's records Wo. To become a record, a result must be completed on a compliant course with compliant timing and reviewed by the governing body; there can be a waiting period between running the time and formal approval, and a pending result cannot be called an established record.

The fastest number on a watch, sports app or social screenshot is only a lead for personal records and does not enter any official record series. A personal PB is your own best result with a standard you define; an official record has explicit event, category, course and approval conventions, and the two should not be mixed.

## Records split by event, sex and category

The same time means different things under different categories. World Athletics records are split by event (track, road, trail and so on), sex and category, with open records marked Mx and women's Wo; age-group, para and different distances each keep their own series. When comparing results across categories, first confirm event distance, group and course type match; close numbers cannot be ranked directly otherwise.

Official result lists also mark status: approved, pending approval or not recognised. A pending result may ultimately be excluded from the record series for measurement, timing or paperwork reasons; read the status before the time. A personal PB does not enter this classification system, and its convention is self-defined.

## What course certification solves

A certified course is independently measured for distance and route so results from different races can be compared. Certification does not guarantee a course is "easy" or "hard", and it does not replace judgement of altitude, gradient, wind and start density. A fast time on an uncertified route usually cannot support an official record or ranking; whether distance and start-finish layout comply follows the race's official certification documents.

## Timing: gun time and chip time

Gun time starts at the starting pistol; chip time starts when you cross the start mat. In crowded large races the two can differ by tens of seconds or more. Race rankings compare gun time; for personal performance the chip time is closer to the watch record, but cross-race comparison still depends on course and conditions. The standard marathon distance is 42.195 km; when the watch track disagrees with the official distance, official measurement prevails.

## How to do it: checks before claiming a PB

1. Confirm the course is certified and the distance matches the event rules.
2. Separate gun time from chip time, and record route, weather and pace for the day.
3. Check bib, eligibility and rule requirements; for example, Boston Marathon qualification follows the official standard and bibs may not be transferred.
4. For an official record, first check whether governing-body approval has completed; before that, call it a result close to the target.

A personal PB does not need official certification, but cross-course comparison should note route and conditions; do not treat a fast downhill, tailwind result as flatland ability.

## Quick reference: result conventions and what can be concluded

| Situation | What you can say | What you cannot say |
|---|---|---|
| Certified course, compliant timing, approved | Official result or record for that event/category | Extrapolate to other courses or categories |
| Certified course, result pending approval | A result close to record level, under review | It is already an official record |
| Fast watch time on an uncertified route | A lead for personal records | An official record or comparable result |
| Chip and gun time mixed | Record both conventions | Compare directly against ranking results |

## Worked example: watch, chip and official time differences

Suppose a certified marathon records 2:59:40 on the watch, 3:02:40 chip time and 3:04:55 official gun time. Watch and chip differ by about 3 minutes, from GPS drift, start position and track straightening; chip and gun differ by about 2:15, from the delay crossing the start mat. All three numbers honestly record different conventions, but only the official timing convention supports rankings and records. Treating the watch time as the official result over- or understates the outcome by several minutes.

## Worked example: why a pending result is not yet a record (hypothetical)

Suppose a runner clocks 2:59:40 on a compliant course. Before the governing body completes review and confirms course measurement, timing, anti-doping and rule paperwork are complete, the result status is "pending approval". At that point you can say "ran 2:59:40, close to the target", but not "set a new record". If review fails or paperwork is incomplete, the result does not enter the official record series; approval status follows the governing body announcement.

## Common misconceptions

- Assuming a certification label makes a result a valid record. Certification is one necessary condition; compliant timing, rule compliance and body approval are all required, and a pending result is not an established record.
- Reading a low-altitude, downhill course result as flatland ability. Course conditions affect the time, and record criteria also include route and rule requirements; this page does not restate specific altitude, gradient or wind thresholds, which follow the governing body rule text.
- Checking official distance against the watch track. GPS drift and sampling straightening make the track longer or shorter; official measurement prevails.
- Mixing PB and record. A PB is a personal best with a self-defined convention; a record is a formally recognised official result.

## When to check the rules

For qualification standards, record applications, category recognition or result disputes, follow the race rules and governing-body announcements. Bib transfer, pace running or failing to complete the prescribed route can invalidate a result; this page does not judge whether a specific case violated rules. Result disputes, injury or physical symptoms are outside what this site can rule on.

## Frequency and progression

Record each race's certification status, timing convention, weather and pace; judge improvement trends after several events. Personal best comparisons should stay on similar routes and conditions; the thinking behind research methods and evidence grading is in [How to read running research](/en/knowledge/reading-running-research/).

## Race-day checklist

- Confirm the race timing method (gun/chip) before the start.
- Record course certification, weather and altitude.
- Separate watch, chip and official times after the race.
- Note course conditions when claiming a PB; do not compare directly across routes.
- Wait for the official approval announcement when records or qualification are involved.

## Scope and limits

Record standards, measurement requirements and application procedures for each event follow the latest World Athletics and relevant race governing-body rules; this page is a general conceptual explanation.
