## Definition: separate insertional from noninsertional first

The Achilles tendon connects the calf muscles to the heel bone and is the largest tendon in the body. AAOS divides Achilles tendinitis into two types, with different locations and different treatment limits:

| Type | Location | Usual group and context | Key limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Noninsertional | Middle of the tendon, above the heel attachment | More common in younger, active people, especially runners | Eccentric heel drops may be appropriate under professional guidance |
| Insertional | Junction of tendon and heel bone | Often with tight calves and heel bone spurs; high running and jumping load | **Do not** do heel drops; they add compression at the insertion |

AAOS also separates tendinopathy (long-term degeneration) from rupture (a sudden tear). This is not wordplay: if you hear a **pop** at the back of the calf or heel during running and then cannot do a single-leg heel raise or push off, treat it as a possible rupture and **seek immediate medical assessment** rather than stretching or massaging it.

## Recognition: morning stiffness, pain after activity, and a changing pattern

Guy's and St Thomas' NHS describes:

- Pain, stiffness, and swelling at the back of the heel.
- The tendon may be warm and tender, and look red, swollen, or thickened.
- Pain varies: some people find it eases during activity and returns afterwards or with weight-bearing.
- Stiffness is worst on getting up in the morning or after sitting.

AAOS adds that pain can be in the middle of the tendon or at the heel attachment; there may be thickening, a bone spur, or swelling; and **pain is often notably worse the day after exercise**. The NHS running page sets a review threshold: if Achilles pain has not improved after **2–3 weeks**, see a GP or physiotherapist.

| What you observe | More consistent with an Achilles problem | More suggestive of another problem |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Back of the heel, along the tendon | Sole, arch, mid-calf muscle |
| Timing | Morning stiffness, lighter during activity then heavier after | Sudden tearing pain, cannot push off |
| Appearance | Thickening, local swelling, tenderness | Obvious red heat, wound |
| Function | Can walk, but single-leg heel raise hurts | Cannot do a heel raise, or heard a pop |
| Course | Weeks to months, fluctuates with load | Rapid worsening within hours |

## When to seek immediate care

- A **sudden pop** at the back of the calf or heel during running or jumping, followed by weakness or inability to do a single-leg heel raise.
- Obvious swelling, changed shape, or inability to bear weight.
- Local redness and heat with fever.
- Pain that keeps worsening after 2–3 weeks of relative rest.
- Night pain or pain at rest.

AAOS lists the sudden pop as a possible sign of Achilles rupture requiring immediate medical assessment. **This page does not diagnose a rupture and gives no advice on painkillers, steroid injections, or surgery.**

## Contributing factors: load change matters more than "inflammation"

GSTT lists higher body weight, tight or weak calf muscles, stiff ankle joints, sudden increases in activity (running, walking, ball sports), training errors such as lack of variety or too much hill running, and ongoing physical or mental health problems. AAOS adds that tight calves are closely associated with the insertional type, and that heel bone spurs are common in insertional tendinitis.

The practical implication is that the target is not simply "reduce inflammation" but **bring load down to what the tendon can currently tolerate, then build it back gradually**. Resting until pain-free and immediately returning to the old mileage commonly leads to another flare; GSTT also notes that pain fluctuations during rehab are normal.

## Three rehab steps: relative rest, pain management, heel-raise doses

### Step 1: Relative rest

GSTT defines relative rest as reducing weight-bearing activity on the injured area, such as long walks or runs, while continuing other exercise such as cycling or swimming. In practice:

- Suspend speed sessions, hill sessions, and long runs.
- Use cycling, swimming, or an elliptical for aerobic work.
- Wear supportive footwear rather than completely flat shoes.
- Record morning stiffness duration and pain 24 hours after running as your load gate.

### Step 2: Pain management (principles only)

GSTT allows short-term painkillers, advises ice wrapped in a towel (never directly on the skin), and supportive footwear. AAOS cautions that **NSAIDs should not be used for more than 1 month without talking to a primary care doctor**. This page gives no specific drug, dose, or regimen.

### Step 3: Heel-raise work, dosed by type and stage

GSTT's early and later doses can be followed within a pain-free range:

- Early stage: stand on the edge of a step with heels over the edge, hold for **20–30 seconds**, step down and rest, repeat **3 times**; progress to one leg.
- Later stage: stand on the edge of a step, rise onto the toes, **lower slowly**, do not let the heel drop below the step, perform **10–12 repetitions**, rest, and repeat for **3 sets**; progress to one leg.
- Frequency: as arranged by a physiotherapist, often every other day or several times weekly; pain during the exercise should stay acceptable and not be clearly worse the next day.

**Boundary:** AAOS states explicitly that heel-drop eccentric work is for **noninsertional** tendinitis only and should not be done with insertional tendinitis. If your pain is at the heel attachment, have the type confirmed before copying any online eccentric programme.

## Recovery time: 6–9 months is the common scale

GSTT states that most people need **6–9 months** of rehabilitation before returning to full activity without pain, and that increased pain or flare-ups during recovery are normal and can be managed with short-term load reduction. If symptoms do not improve after 6–9 months of appropriate exercise, shock-wave therapy and other options can be considered. AAOS notes that even with early treatment pain may last **longer than 3 months**, and surgery is usually discussed only after **6 months** of failed nonsurgical treatment.

For runners, these numbers mean: do not call it a failure because two weeks have passed, and do not call it cured because you can still run. The better markers are shorter morning stiffness, a stable 24-hour response, and a pain-free single-leg heel raise.

## A worked example you can adapt

Example runner: four runs per week totalling 40 km; 10 minutes of morning Achilles stiffness; pain 3/10 from 5 km, 5/10 later that day, and 15 minutes of stiffness the next morning.

Adjustment example (not a prescription):

1. Suspend hill and speed sessions; cut runs to 5 km on flat ground every other day.
2. Cycle 40 minutes on the other two days.
3. Record morning stiffness in minutes each day; if the next morning exceeds 15 minutes, remove another kilometre from the next run.
4. After a professional confirms it is the **noninsertional** type, do early-stage heel holds of 20–30 seconds × 3 every other day.
5. After two weeks without increasing morning stiffness, add one run or 1 km rather than returning to 10 km.

This example only shows how to use morning stiffness and the 24-hour response to set load; it does not promise recovery within 6–9 months.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Achilles pain is inflammation, so take an anti-inflammatory and keep running."** AAOS prefers tendinopathy for long-term degeneration; painkillers give short-term relief but do not change load capacity. Using NSAIDs for more than a month requires medical advice.
- **"Everyone with Achilles pain should do heel drops."** Not with the insertional type — an explicit AAOS boundary.
- **"A steroid injection works faster."** AAOS states that cortisone injections into the Achilles tendon are **not recommended** because they can cause rupture and research shows decreased tendon strength.
- **"No pain means it is healed."** GSTT notes fluctuations during rehab; judge by morning stiffness, the single-leg heel raise, and the 24-hour response, not by one good day.

## Frequency, progression, and review

- Heel raises: by type and stage, every other day or several times weekly, for months.
- Running: flat, easy, short first; add hills and speed last.
- Review: seek care if there is no improvement after 2–3 weeks; if 6–9 months of appropriate rehab brings no change, discuss other options with a clinician.
- Prevention: avoid sudden load jumps, limit consecutive hill work, maintain calf and ankle range, and wear supportive shoes.

## Self-check

- [ ] Is the pain in the middle of the tendon, or at the heel attachment (insertional)?
- [ ] Is there no pop, no inability to do a heel raise, no obvious swelling, and no inability to bear weight?
- [ ] Have you increased mileage, hills, or speed in the last two weeks?
- [ ] Are you using morning stiffness and the 24-hour response to set today's run?
- [ ] Has the heel-drop exercise been confirmed as appropriate for your type?
- [ ] Have you avoided unassessed steroid injections into the tendon?

## Scope and limits

This is health education, not a diagnosis or rehab prescription. Achilles pain can overlap with partial tears, tendon-sheath problems, bone conditions, or other diseases. AAOS and GSTT differ on imaging (GSTT says scans are usually unnecessary for diagnosis; AAOS says MRI or ultrasound may be used to rule out other problems); this page reports both. Children, pregnant runners, post-operative runners, people on anticoagulants, and people with metabolic disease should be assessed clinically first.
