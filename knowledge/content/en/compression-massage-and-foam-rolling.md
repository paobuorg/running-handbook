## Separate the three interventions

Runners often lump recovery tools together, but their mechanisms, evidence and risks differ:

- **Compression garments and socks** apply external pressure to a limb, with hoped-for effects on swelling, blood flow and delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS);
- **Sports massage** is manual manipulation of muscles and soft tissue by another person, often expected to relax tissue, improve flexibility and reduce soreness;
- **Foam rolling** is self-myofascial release, often expected to increase joint range of motion and reduce soreness.

None of them "repairs" tissue. What the evidence supports is usually much narrower than the marketing.

## Evidence at a glance

| Intervention | Evidence base | Performance | Soreness / range of motion | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Compression garments | 19 randomised trials, 350 participants | **No effect** on strength recovery (SMDs near zero and non-significant at every time point) | Not demonstrated | Systematic review and meta-analysis; only a weak effect after plyometric exercise |
| Sports massage | 29 randomised trials, 1,012 participants | **No evidence** of improvement in strength, jump, sprint, endurance or fatigue | DOMS improved by about 13%; small flexibility improvement | Systematic review and meta-analysis; very high heterogeneity for DOMS (I² = 86%) |
| Foam rolling | 14 studies | Short-term use does not harm performance; brief pre-exercise use does not affect performance | Short-term increase in joint range of motion; may attenuate performance decrements and DOMS after intense exercise | Systematic review, level of evidence 2c; methods varied, no consensus on an optimal programme |

## Compression garments — no evidence for strength recovery

A 2022 Sports Medicine systematic review and meta-analysis asked specifically whether compression garments reduce the deleterious effects of exercise on muscle strength. Across 19 randomised trials and 350 healthy participants, the pooled results at every post-exercise time point sat around "no effect":

| Time point | SMD (95% CI) | p |
|---|---|---|
| Under 24 h | −0.02 (−0.22 to 0.19) | 0.87 |
| 24–48 h | −0.00 (−0.22 to 0.21) | 0.98 |
| 48–72 h | −0.03 (−0.43 to 0.37) | 0.87 |
| 72–96 h | 0.14 (−0.21 to 0.49) | 0.43 |
| 96 h or more | 0.26 (−0.33 to 0.85) | 0.38 |

The authors also reported that **the body area where compression was applied made no difference**; a weak strength-sparing effect appeared only after plyometric exercise; and **no likely recovery benefit was shown after running**, which they explained by running causing a lower level of muscle damage than resistance exercise. If the purchase rationale is "recover strength faster after a run", the evidence does not support that expectation.

The review did record a nuance: compression has been associated with improved **running economy** and some biomechanical variables, but garment wear was not associated with improved sports performance in high-intensity exercise. Those are not interchangeable — running economy is oxygen cost, not race result.

One number that describes the market but not the effect: the review notes worldwide sales of compression garments reached 5 million pieces with an overall value of USD 45 million in 2019. Sales are not efficacy.

**Reasonable use**: if wearing compression makes a long run more comfortable, reduces chafing or eases subjective fatigue, wear it. Do not treat it as a recovery accelerator, and do not let it displace sleep, fluid and load management.

## Sports massage — a little for soreness, nothing for performance

The 2020 BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine review included 29 randomised studies and 1,012 participants — described by the authors as the largest examination of massage effects to that date. The conclusion has two halves:

- **No evidence** that massage improves strength, jump, sprint, endurance or fatigue;
- Massage was associated with **small but statistically significant** improvements in flexibility and DOMS.

For DOMS, the pooled result was about a **13% improvement**, SMD **1.13** (95% CI 0.44 to 1.82), across **311 participants and 10 studies**, with high heterogeneity (**I² = 86%**). High heterogeneity means the studies differed substantially in technique, duration, timing and population; 13% is not a fixed value that applies to everyone.

The practical meaning: massage is better framed as an adjunct for **subjective soreness and flexibility**, not a pre-race activation or post-race "lactic acid" tool. If it helps you feel better and sleep better, that has value; it should not crowd out sleep, nutrition and training planning.

The evidence **does not define an optimal technique, duration or timing**. Any claim that massage must be exactly 60 minutes within 24 hours after a race goes beyond what these reviews can support.

## Foam rolling — short-term range of motion, long-term programme unknown

The 2015 International Journal of Sports Physical Therapy systematic review included 14 articles and concluded:

- Foam rolling or roller massage has **short-term effects on increasing joint range of motion** without negatively affecting muscle performance;
- It **may** help attenuate decrements in muscle performance and DOMS after intense exercise;
- **Short bouts before exercise do not appear to affect** muscle performance;
- Because methods varied widely, there is **no consensus on an optimal programme**;
- The level of evidence is **2c**.

This makes foam rolling a low-risk, possibly useful tool, but not a mandatory recovery step. Its short-term range-of-motion gain does not replace targeted strength work or technique changes.

**Reasonable use**: place it at the end of a warm-up or on an easy day, roll each area briefly, and avoid bony prominences, wounds and acutely painful spots. Do not chase "the more it hurts, the better it works". If rolling worsens pain or produces numbness or bruising, stop and assess.

## Safety boundaries — when not to self-treat

The following is general safety guidance, not a conclusion of the reviews above:

- **Unexplained pain, swelling, redness or warmth in one calf**: do not try to "massage it out" with a roller or hands; seek prompt medical assessment for deep vein thrombosis (DVT). NHS lists throbbing pain in one leg (usually calf or thigh), swelling in one leg, red/blue/darkened skin around the painful area and swollen veins; with **shortness of breath or chest pain**, treat it as a possible pulmonary embolism and call emergency services.
- **Known or suspected fracture, muscle tear, infection or open wound**: do not apply pressure or massage over the area.
- **Taking anticoagulant medication**: deep pressure, strong massage and rolling may increase bleeding risk; ask the prescriber first.
- **Pain that keeps worsening, or new numbness or weakness** after massage or rolling: stop and seek care rather than adding more pressure.
- **Compression that causes numbness, tingling, skin colour change or deep creases**: remove it and reassess sizing.

This page does not diagnose DVT, muscle injury or any other condition, and gives no anticoagulant or pain-medication advice.

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | More accurate statement |
|---|---|
| "Compression speeds strength recovery" | The pooled 19-trial analysis found no significant effect at any time point |
| "Massage clears lactate and improves performance" | No evidence for strength, jump, sprint, endurance or fatigue; small effects on DOMS and flexibility |
| "The more a foam roller hurts, the better" | Evidence supports short-term range of motion and possible soreness relief, not pain intensity as a success criterion |
| "Copy whatever works for someone else" | Technique, duration, pressure and individual response vary; the review found no optimal programme |
| "Recovery tools replace sleep and fuelling" | Sleep and energy/fluid intake are the base; these tools are at most adjuncts |

## What this page cannot answer

- Which compression pressure class suits you: it depends on sizing, limb circumference and comfort; this page gives no product specification;
- How long or with what technique massage should be done: the reviews define no optimal protocol;
- Which muscles or how long to foam roll: evidence level 2c with heterogeneous protocols;
- Whether your calf pain is DVT: that needs clinical assessment; this page lists red flags but does not judge.

## Scope, a three-step decision and one example

Scope: intact skin, no known deep vein thrombosis or severe varicose veins, no sensory loss and no anticoagulant use. Do not self-apply compression in the first 48 hours of an acute strain or marked swelling.

Three steps: first set the goal (recovery, range of motion or performance), then choose the tool (compression, massage or foam rolling), then re-check the same measure 2–3 times. The evidence supports short-term effects only, so reviewing matters more than persisting.

Example (hypothetical): to test whether compression socks help your post-race soreness, run the same route twice, once with and once without, and rate soreness 0–10 at 24 and 48 hours. A difference under 1 point means no perceptible benefit for you, and there is no reason to keep buying them.
