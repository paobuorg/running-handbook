## Conclusion first

The core message of running sodium advice is not "take a fixed milligram amount of salt per hour". It is: estimate your fluid deficit and individual sweat sodium first, then decide whether and how much sodium to add; in most settings the risk from overdrinking is higher than the risk from a sodium deficit. The ACSM 2007 position stand notes considerable variability in sweating rates and sweat electrolyte content between individuals, and recommends customized fluid replacement programs. The 3rd International EAH Consensus (2015) identifies overconsumption of hypotonic fluids relative to exchangeable sodium, likely combined with non-osmotic AVP secretion, as the primary etiology and pathophysiological mechanism behind EAH and all known fatalities (Grade 1A).

Three actions come first. First, estimate your sweat rate by pre- and post-exercise weighing rather than copying a per-hour sodium figure from a drink label. Second, let thirst guide drinking; do not chase perfectly clear urine. Third, headache, nausea or vomiting, confusion, seizure, swelling or collapse mean stop fluids and exercise and seek emergency care — do not keep drinking water or sports drink.

This page is exercise nutrition and safety education, not diagnosis. People with cardiovascular or kidney disease, or those taking diuretics and other medication, should have fluid and sodium plans assessed by a clinician first.

## Definition and premises

- **Sodium (Na⁺):** the main extracellular cation, involved in osmotic pressure, neuromuscular excitability and fluid balance. Sweat also loses chloride and a small amount of potassium.
- **Sweat rate:** fluid lost per unit time, in L/h, derived from pre/post body weight, fluid intake and urine volume.
- **Sweat sodium concentration:** sodium per litre of sweat, in mmol/L; convert to milligrams by multiplying by 23 (1 mmol sodium ≈ 23 mg sodium).
- **Exercise-associated hyponatremia (EAH):** serum sodium below about 135 mmol/L during or after exercise, mostly dilutional from relative body-water excess.
- **Exercise-associated muscle cramps (EAMC):** muscle spasm during or after exercise; current evidence does not equate it simply with dehydration or sodium deficit.

This page covers road running, long runs and ultras. Short, low-intensity activity usually needs no dedicated sodium replacement. For everyday fluid intake, see [Hydration](/en/knowledge/hydration/); combined heat risk is in [Heat and humidity](/en/knowledge/heat-and-humidity/).

## Key mechanisms: sweat composition and individual variation

Sweat is not a simple filtrate of plasma; its sodium concentration depends on sweat-gland reabsorption, heat acclimation, energy expenditure and exercise mode. Baker 2022's retrospective analysis of 1,944 sweat tests from 1,304 subjects gives a reference population distribution:

| Measure | Mean ± SD | Median | Range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Whole-body sweat sodium (mmol/L) | 42.0 ± 12.6 | 40.3 | 17–106 |
| Whole-body sweating rate (L/h) | 1.09 ± 0.50 | 1.03 | 0.19–5.64 |

Two points must be read out. First, sweat sodium spans 17 to 106 mmol/L — about a sixfold difference — and sweating rate spans nearly thirtyfold; one label dose cannot fit everyone. Second, the regression models explained only 17%–23% of sweat-sodium variation; age group, race/ethnicity, fluid balance, sodium intake and relative VO2max showed no association, so a population average cannot replace individual testing. Higher energy expenditure went with higher sweat sodium, and warmer months (a proxy for heat acclimation) with lower sweat sodium; exercise mode, air temperature and sex may have small effects.

The ACSM 2007 target is to prevent excessive dehydration, defined as more than 2% body weight loss from water deficit, and excessive electrolyte balance changes; beverages containing carbohydrate and electrolytes can benefit over water alone under certain circumstances, depending on the exercise task and weather.

## Decision table: do not mix cramps, dehydration and hyponatremia

| Presentation | More likely situation | Handling | Evidence boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Local muscle tightness or spasm that settles with rest; no headache, nausea or altered awareness | Exercise-associated muscle cramp, possibly related to fatigue, neuromuscular control or local load | Stretch, rest, cool down, fluids; do not treat the cramp as an automatic "must add salt" diagnosis | Consensus-cited experimental and observational work suggests some cramps reflect fatigue-related neurological changes rather than simple water/sodium loss |
| Thirst, low urine output, over 2% weight loss, heavy legs | Dehydration tendency | Replace fluid in divided doses; add electrolytes in long or heavy sweating | ACSM: target prevention of excessive dehydration, individualized program |
| Weight stable or up, puffy hands/face, headache, nausea/vomiting, confusion, seizure | Suspect EAH (dilutional hyponatremia) | Stop further large fluid intake; stop exercise; seek medical care; do not force hypotonic fluids | EAH consensus: primary etiology is overconsumption of hypotonic fluid; symptom severity, not a single sodium value, guides treatment |
| White salt streaks on clothes, salty-tasting sweat | Weak clue toward high sweat sodium | Observe tolerance of electrolyte drink or small salt addition in long sessions | A clue is not a lab test; it cannot compute an exact replacement dose |

## How to estimate your own sweat sodium: operational steps

Without sweat patches or whole-body washdown testing, start by computing sweat rate, then use the population range as a conservative estimate. A personal sweat sodium concentration needs patch testing or laboratory sweat analysis.

1. **Baseline weigh-in:** empty the bladder before the run; weigh in dry, close-fitting clothing (or nude), record the time.
2. **Record intake and output:** weigh bottles before and after to get fluid intake; estimate any urine passed.
3. **Post-run weighing:** dry off, same clothing, weigh and record duration.
4. **Compute sweat loss:** sweat loss (L) = pre weight − post weight + fluid intake (L) − urine (L); sweat rate = sweat loss ÷ hours. Treat 1 kg weight difference as about 1 L of fluid.
5. **Estimate sodium loss:** sodium loss (mg/h) = sweat rate (L/h) × sweat sodium (mmol/L) × 23. Without a measured value, use the population mean 42 mmol/L as a neutral starting point, not your personal fixed value.
6. **Decide replacement:** under 60–90 minutes with modest sweating, water plus daily food usually suffices; only in long, heavy-sweating sessions, or confirmed high sweat sodium, trial electrolyte drinks or salty fuel in training. Fluid replacement need not match every millilitre; mild weight loss in long runs is common and acceptable.

## Worked example: a 70 kg runner, 2-hour long run (assumed)

Assumptions: pre-run 70.0 kg, post-run 69.2 kg (0.8 kg loss, about 1.1% body weight), 0.6 L drunk on route, no urine, 2.0 h run.

- Sweat loss = 0.8 L + 0.6 L = 1.4 L; sweat rate = 0.70 L/h.
- At 20 mmol/L sweat sodium (low end): sodium loss = 0.70 × 20 × 23 ≈ 322 mg/h, about 0.64 g over 2 h.
- At 42 mmol/L (population mean): sodium loss = 0.70 × 42 × 23 ≈ 676 mg/h, about 1.35 g over 2 h.
- At 80 mmol/L (high end): sodium loss = 0.70 × 80 × 23 ≈ 1.29 g/h, about 2.58 g over 2 h.

At the same sweat rate, high versus low sweat sodium changes hourly sodium loss by about fourfold. This is why a label's "300–600 mg sodium per hour" is an average figure: it may be insufficient for a high-sodium sweater and unnecessary for a low one. Sodium intake can attenuate the fall in blood sodium, but the consensus is explicit: it cannot prevent EAH when persistent excessive fluid intake produces fluid overload.

## Common misconceptions

1. **"Urine must be perfectly clear to be hydrated."** NHS guidance calls a clear pale yellow sufficient; drinking to dilute urine continuously is itself an EAH risk behavior.
2. **"A cramp means salt deficiency, chug a sports drink."** Cramp mechanisms are uncertain, and some cramps relate to fatigue-related neurological changes. Sports drinks remain hypotonic at 10–38 mmol/L sodium, far below serum at about 140 mmol/L; dilution from excess volume overwhelms the drink's sodium.
3. **"More salt is always safer."** The consensus considers modest salt replacement likely harmless or serum-sodium maintaining, but of modest to no benefit when overdrinking; evidence on harms of excessive sodium supplementation is unclear, so large-dose salt tablets should not be self-prescribed.
4. **"Everyone hydrates at the same sweat rate."** Sweating rate spans 0.19 to 5.64 L/h; weighing is the individualized starting point.
5. **"Only ultras need electrolytes."** Carbohydrate and electrolyte needs rise beyond 2.5–3 h, but EAH can occur in shorter events too; risk factors include inexperience, slow pace, extreme BMI and readily available fluids.

## Failure modes and stop rules

- **Scheduled forced fluid drinking:** early signals are weight unchanged or up, puffy hands, pale urine with persistent nausea. Rule: return to thirst-guided intake and check by weighing.
- **Plain water only in a long, heavy sweat session:** early signals are accelerating fatigue and muscle weakness. Rule: trial an electrolyte drink for long sessions, validated in training first; see [Long-run fueling](/en/knowledge/long-run-fueling/).
- **Salt tablets as a universal cramp fix:** recurrent cramps call for review of load, sleep and energy intake, not more salt tablets.
- **Continuing to run with EAH red flags:** headache, vomiting, confusion, seizure, breathing difficulty, marked face or hand swelling, collapse — stop exercise, do not keep drinking large volumes, seek immediate medical care.

## Applicable boundaries

This page is general exercise nutrition and safety education, not a diagnosis, treatment plan or medication advice. Sweat sodium and sweat-rate figures come from specific population datasets and cannot be converted into a precise personal loss; observational and consensus evidence is flagged separately per claim. Kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, pregnancy, lactation, or diuretic/antihypertensive medication call for clinician-assessed fluid and sodium plans. This site does not measure serum sodium or replace emergency judgment.

## What would change this advice

- **Duration and intensity:** under 60–90 minutes water usually suffices; ultra-endurance beyond 2.5–3 h raises carbohydrate and electrolyte needs.
- **Environment:** heat and humidity accelerate sweat and sodium loss; sweat sodium usually falls with heat acclimation; seasonal change or first hot-weather race needs re-testing.
- **Individual state:** age, sex, exercise mode, energy expenditure, prior EAH or cramp history all change the plan; dietary sodium intake showed no association with sweat sodium in this dataset.
- **Evidence update:** EAH management, sweat-sodium predictors and salt dosing are active research; the page adjusts if later high-quality consensus or reviews differ.
- **Disease and medication:** underlying conditions or drugs change the safe range for fluid and sodium; clinical assessment takes priority over the general figures here.
