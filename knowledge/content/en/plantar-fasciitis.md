## Definition and who this page applies to

The plantar fascia is a strong ligament running from the heel bone toward the toes that supports the arch of the foot. Plantar fascia pain is usually located at the **bottom of the heel and the arch**, and is common in runners, people who stand for long periods, and people with higher body weight. AAOS gives the scale: about **2 million patients** are treated for this condition each year in the United States, and it is one of the most common causes of pain at the bottom of the heel.

This page is for people whose heel pain is worst on the first step after sleep or sitting, eases after a few minutes of walking, and worsens again after prolonged standing, walking, or running. It is **not** for obvious swelling, inability to bear weight, sudden severe pain after an injury, or persistent numbness or tingling in the foot; those need clinical assessment.

## Recognition: three time points matter more than the location

NHS lists specific clues:

1. Pain is **worst when you first start walking after sleeping or resting**.
2. It gradually eases as you start moving.
3. It worsens again after long periods of standing, walking, or running.
4. Stretching the sole of the foot — raising the toes, walking upstairs — is painful.

AAOS adds the risk background: new or increased activity, repetitive high-impact exercise, long periods standing on hard surfaces, flat feet or a high arch, tight calf muscles, obesity, and age 40–60. The NHS list overlaps and specifically mentions recently starting exercise on hard surfaces and a sudden large increase in standing, walking, or running.

| What you observe | More consistent with plantar fascia pain | More suggestive of another problem |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Worst on the first morning step, eases with movement | Continuous pain, waking at night |
| Location | Bottom of the heel, arch | Top of the foot, toe joints, above the heel |
| Swelling | Usually minimal | Obvious redness, swelling, heat |
| Weight-bearing | Possible, though the first step hurts | Cannot bear weight or gait clearly changes |
| Sensation | Local tightness and soreness | Numbness, stinging, radiating pain |

## What to do first: a two-week self-care order

### Step 1: Reduce load instead of pushing through

NHS's "do not" list includes not walking or standing for long periods, not wearing high heels or tight pointy shoes, not wearing flip-flops or backless slippers, and trying not to walk barefoot on hard surfaces. For runners, the NHS running-injury page advises **stopping running when heel pain is significant and accompanied by swelling** rather than testing whether it "runs off".

Practical steps:

- Suspend speed work, downhill work, and long runs; keep short easy runs that are pain-free, or swim.
- Break up standing time: change position or sit every 30–40 minutes.
- Wear shoes with heel support indoors rather than walking in flat slippers.

### Step 2: Ice and stretch at the doses the sources give

- Ice: NHS gives **up to 20 minutes every 2–3 hours**; AAOS gives **rolling the foot over a cold bottle for 20 minutes, 3–4 times a day**; the NHS running page gives **15–20 minutes rolling a frozen bottle**. The shared principle is short, spaced, with a cloth barrier, never until numb.
- Stretching: AAOS calls stretching the calves and plantar fascia one of the **most effective** ways to relieve pain, with a dose of **a 10-second hold repeated 20 times per foot**. NHS advises regular gentle stretching without pushing into sharp pain.
- Night splint: AAOS notes a night splint keeps the plantar fascia stretched during sleep and is very effective at reducing morning heel pain. Whether it suits you is a clinical decision; this page does not substitute for a prescription.

### Step 3: Shoes, insoles, and load capacity

- NHS advises shoes with **cushioned heels and good arch support**, plus insoles or heel pads if needed.
- AAOS lists new or increased activity, repetitive high impact, long standing on hard surfaces, flat feet or a high arch, tight calf muscles, obesity, and age 40–60 as risk factors. That means addressing load and footwear together, not buying one pair of shoes.
- NHS advises losing weight if overweight, where feasible. This does not mean weight is the only cause.

## Recovery time and return-to-running signals

AAOS gives the prognosis: **more than 90% of patients improve within 10 months** of starting simple treatment. That carries two messages: most people do not need surgery, and the condition is often slow, so "must be better in two weeks" is not a fair standard.

Use three return signals:

1. First-step pain drops from sharp to a mild tightness and does not rise within 24 hours.
2. After three consecutive easy runs, the next morning's pain is no worse.
3. Everyday walking no longer changes your gait because of heel pain.

Once these hold, add distance before speed. If obvious swelling returns after running, the NHS running page advises stopping and seeking care.

## A worked example you can adapt

Example runner: five runs per week totalling 60 km; first-step pain 7/10, dropping to 2/10 after five minutes of walking, rising to 5/10 after 8 km.

Adjustment example (not a prescription):

1. Reduce five runs to three runs of 5 km, all flat and easy; swim or cycle 40 minutes on the other two days.
2. Do the calf and plantar stretch "10 seconds × 20 repetitions" twice daily.
3. Roll the foot over a cold bottle for 15–20 minutes after running.
4. Replace worn everyday shoes; do not walk indoors in flat slippers.
5. Every third day, record first-step pain and the 24-hour response; after two weeks without worsening, add 1 km.

This example shows how to use morning pain and the 24-hour response to set dose. It does not promise recovery within 10 months and does not replace clinical treatment.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Roll a golf ball or tennis ball harder, even if it hurts."** The sources support cold-bottle rolling and gentle stretching, not rolling into sharp pain. Rolling until bruising has no supporting evidence.
- **"The heel spur is the cause."** AAOS states that a heel spur can result from longstanding tension but **most people with heel spurs do not have heel pain**, and the spur is not the cause of plantar fascia pain. Do not treat a spur on an imaging report as a verdict.
- **"The softest shoe will fix it."** NHS asks for heel cushioning plus arch support, not softness alone; very soft shoes may lack support over long distances.
- **"A steroid injection is the fastest fix."** This page gives no injection or medication advice. AAOS mentions steroid injections or shock-wave therapy when conservative treatment fails, but whether and when to use them is a clinical decision.

## When to seek care

NHS lists:

- Pain is severe or stopping you doing normal activities.
- Heel pain is getting worse or keeps coming back.
- No improvement after **2 weeks** of self-treatment.
- Tingling or loss of feeling in the foot.
- Diabetes with foot pain, because foot problems can be more serious.

Obvious swelling, sudden severe pain after an injury, or inability to bear weight should prompt urgent or orthopaedic assessment rather than more self-treatment. **This page does not diagnose and does not recommend painkiller types, doses, or injections.**

## Frequency, progression, and review

- Stretching: once or twice daily at the 10-second × 20-repetition dose, for 6–8 weeks before reassessing.
- Ice: 3–4 times daily during the painful phase, never more than 20 minutes at a time.
- Mileage: use morning pain and the 24-hour response as the gate, not the calendar.
- Review: seek care if there is no improvement after 2 weeks, or sooner if swelling, numbness, or night pain appears.

## Self-check

- [ ] Is the worst moment the first step after sleep or prolonged sitting?
- [ ] Does it ease after a few minutes, then worsen after long standing or running?
- [ ] Is there no obvious swelling, heat, numbness, or inability to bear weight?
- [ ] Have you recently increased mileage, switched to hard surfaces, or changed to thin-soled shoes?
- [ ] Have you scheduled stretching and icing today rather than relying on painkillers alone?
- [ ] Do your everyday shoes have heel cushioning and arch support?

## Scope and limits

This is health education, not a substitute for imaging, physical examination, or clinical diagnosis. Heel pain can also come from the Achilles tendon, metatarsals, nerves, or other structures. NHS and AAOS differ slightly on icing method (bag versus bottle) and duration (15–20 minutes); this page follows their shared principle of short, spaced, cloth-barrier application. Children, pregnant runners, people with diabetes, post-operative runners, and anyone with altered sensation should be assessed clinically first.
