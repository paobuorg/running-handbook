## What this page solves

A flat 5:30/km does not answer whether you can “run” a 15% climb. Most people cannot, and do not need to. Trail pacing should be expressed as vertical metres per hour plus flat-equivalent pace plus technical descent pace, not road pace forced onto a mountain.

This page is an execution framework and a reproducible check, not a substitute for course rehearsal or coaching.

## Why flat pace fails

Uphill, the same speed requires more vertical work; metabolic cost rises nonlinearly with gradient. Descent is not free either: eccentric contraction, braking and landing impact accumulate in the quads, calves and ankles. Technical terrain adds lateral movement and attention cost.

| Terrain | Main cost | Pacing strategy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5% easy grade | Slightly above flat | Flat feel, slightly slower |
| 5–15% climb | Vertical work, rising heart rate | Walk-run by breathing |
| 15–25% steep | Strength and posture | Brisk walk, hands on knees |
| 0–10% descent | Eccentric load, impact | Short steps, high cadence, no charging |
| >10% technical descent | Braking, balance, attention | Slow down; safety first |

## Three speeds: climb, descent, technical

**Climb speed:** the effort you can sustain for 20–30 minutes without blowing up. Breathing is fast but you can speak short sentences: run. Cannot speak: walk. Walk with hands on knees, short steps, no hunched back.

**Descent speed:** quiet landing, high cadence, able to stop at any time. Do not lengthen stride to chase someone; slow technical descent preserves quads and ankles.

**Technical speed:** rocks, roots, wet ground and night. Pace at the limit where you can see the next foot placement; do not trade eyes and foot position for pace.

## Vertical-speed table

The table is a planning start, not a race target. Calibrate with your own training: find a known climb, record time and heart rate, then adjust.

| Climb rate (m/h) | Intensity | Suitable | Sustainable |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 300–500 | Easy | Long distance, opening | Hours |
| 500–800 | Steady | 100K middle | 2–4 hours |
| 800–1,200 | Hard | Short climb, key race segment | 30–90 minutes |
| Above 1,200 | Sprint/technical | Short slope | Minutes to 20 minutes |

“Sustainable” changes with altitude, weather, load and technique; at 3,000 m or with an 8 kg pack, reduce the whole table by 20–30%.

## Walk-run switches: breathing, not pride

| Grade | Recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| 0–5% | Run, slightly below flat feel |
| 5–10% | Run 1–2 min / walk 30 s, or switch by breathing |
| 10–15% | Walk 30–60 s / run 30 s if able |
| Above 15% | Brisk walk, hands on knees, short steps |
| Very steep/technical | Walk, use hands if needed |

Switching is not a fixed watch interval; it is “run when breathing recovers, walk when it runs away”. Walking a climb is not embarrassing; blowing up on it is the real cost to the second half.

## Descent: technical pace and eccentric load

Descent pace should be slower than flat unless the course is a wide fire road. Short steps, high cadence, foot landing under the centre of mass, no heel braking; look two steps ahead in turns, not at your feet.

| Descent type | Pace | Focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wide, 5% | Near flat | Relax, do not charge |
| Steep 10% | Clearly slower | Short steps, high cadence |
| Technical gravel | Slower | Eyes ahead, stable foot placement |
| Wet/night | Safety first | Headlamp, shorter stride |

Quads and calves ache 24–48 hours after descent; do not trade time by charging down when climbs and flat remain.

## Two worked examples

**50K, 2,500 m climb, 7-hour goal.** Average climb rate about 360 m/h, but unevenly distributed: first 10 km climbs 800 m at 500–600 m/h walk-run; middle easy grade at 6:30/km; final 10 km descends 600 m at 6:00–7:00/km technical pace. Add segment times; do not multiply flat pace by distance.

**100K, 5,000 m climb, 16-hour goal.** Average 312 m/h; reduce 20% at night. At every aid station record arrival time, climb, breathing and feet. If two consecutive segments are 15% behind, lower the goal rather than covering it with caffeine.

## Race execution: put the vertical ledger into segments

| Segment | Distance | Climb | Target time | Check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0–15 km | 600 m | 2:10 | Breathing, heart rate |
| 2 | 15–30 km | 900 m | 2:30 | Fuel, legs |
| 3 | 30–45 km | 700 m | 2:20 | Feet, night light |
| 4 | 45–50 km | 300 m | 1:00 | Safe finish |

In each segment, check only “on plan or downgrade”; do not recalculate the whole race at the station.

## Boundaries

This is a pacing and execution framework, not a substitute for course rehearsal, a coaching plan or medical judgement. Gradient cost varies with individual, surface, altitude and load; the table is a start, not a guarantee.

## Course calibration

A vertical-speed table only works against your own course. Before racing, find a climb similar to the race: 1–3 km, similar grade and surface. Walk-run at the planned effort and record time, climb, heart rate and breathing. The climb rate you can sustain for 30 minutes becomes the ceiling for race climbs.

| Measure | Record | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Climb rate | m/h | Calibrate the table |
| Heart rate | Average/peak | Avoid a fast start |
| Breathing | Can you speak short sentences? | Walk-run switch |
| Descent time | min:sec per km | Technical pace |
| 24-hour response | Soreness/pain | Whether load was excessive |

Retest every 4–6 weeks; as fitness improves, heart rate at the same climb rate falls, rather than “you must go faster”.

## Altitude, load and weather corrections

The same climb rate costs more at 2,500 m, with a 6 kg pack or at 30°C. Adjust in order: reduce target 10–20% for altitude/load, another 10% for heat/cold/wet, and only then consider caffeine. Do not stack all three and still use the sea-level table.

## Race-day segment table

Split the course by aid stations into 4–6 segments; each gets distance, climb, target time and check item. At the station, check only that segment; do not recalculate the whole race. Two consecutive segments 15% behind means lower the overall goal, not “catch up” later.

## Common errors

- Multiplying flat pace by distance to estimate trail finish time.
- Running every climb hard and being forced to walk after 30 minutes.
- Charging descents and destroying the quads early.
- Using daytime technical pace at night.
- Recalculating the whole race at an aid station and losing rhythm.
- Treating the vertical table as a guarantee without altitude and load.

Trail pacing is budget plus execution, not courage plus luck.

## Training the walk-run ratio

Uphill walk-run is not a race-day trick. Once a week, find a 10–15% slope and do 6–8 reps of “run 2 min / walk 1 min”, recording breathing recovery; walk easy for 1 minute between reps. The goal is not more speed but a switch that does not break rhythm.

Practise descent technique too: a short technical descent, 4–6 × 2 minutes, focusing on quiet landing, short steps and eyes ahead. Check quads and lower abdomen at 24 hours; too much reaction means fewer reps.

## Poles and terrain

Poles share leg load on steep climbs but need rehearsal. First use on race day, wrong length or inability to stow them on technical descent all add risk. Use poles, shoes and pack in long runs before racing.

Technical terrain rewards “stable”, not “fast”. On rocks and roots, look two steps ahead and find secure foot placements; ten seconds slower beats a twisted ankle. Trail pace is a whole-race budget, not heroics in one segment.

## Breathing threshold for walk-run

Run when you can speak a short sentence, walk when only single words, stop when you cannot speak. This threshold is more reliable than any pace table, especially with altitude, load and weather.
