## What this page covers, and when it applies

This page covers how to judge shoe fit, the relationship between cushioning materials and injury risk, and when to replace shoes. It assumes no unassessed foot deformity, acute injury or postoperative recovery; in those situations shoe choice should follow advice from a clinician or physiotherapist. Material engineering details of sole geometry, uppers and carbon plates are in [Shoe technology and materials](/en/knowledge/shoe-technology/); this page does not go into materials engineering.

## Fit first, cushioning second

A good fit means about a thumb's width (roughly 1 cm) between the longest toe and the toe cap, a deep forefoot that does not press on the toes, a secure heel wrap, and laces or straps that tighten at the arch. Try both feet; most people have one slightly larger foot. Feet swell slightly through the day, so late-day fitting is closer to how they feel after an afternoon session or a long run. Narrow, pointed toe boxes squeeze the toes and can over time cause nail problems, corns or nerve compression.

Cushioning feel is subjective: the same shoe feels too soft and lossy to one runner, right to another. A few minutes in the shop cannot represent long-run performance; the first 20-30 km of break-in matter more. A concrete buying checklist is in [Choosing running shoes](/en/knowledge/running-shoes/); this page focuses on judgement criteria and evidence limits.

## What cushioning and injury actually relate to

An observational study of 527 recreational runners compared stability and neutral shoes: injury rates were about 42% in stability users and 39% in neutral users, with no clear advantage for either shoe type within that study. Current evidence supports an association between fit, comfort and lower injury risk, but it does not prove that switching to one shoe type prevents injury.

Injury results from training volume, intensity, recovery, form, surface, prior injuries and footwear together. A softer or thicker midsole does not automatically offset a rapid volume jump; wear patterns alone cannot diagnose pronation or supination, which need clinical assessment. Training load principles are in [Load and overtraining](/en/knowledge/load-and-overtraining/).

## Upper, heel and last check

Fit is not only about length. The upper should wrap the instep without pressing on it; the heel cup holds the heel without sliding up and down on landing and push-off; laces or straps tighten at the arch while the toes stay free in the forefoot. The sole at the ball should cover the widest part of the foot; a too-narrow sole squeezes the outside of the forefoot. Try on in usual running socks, lace both shoes, walk a few steps then jog; feet sliding inside the shoe or a single persistent pressure point mean the size or last does not fit.

Last width varies by brand and model, and the same EU size can be wide in one model and narrow in another. When switching brands, match measured foot length, width and fit rather than the label size. Arch support, upper wrap and heel stability are design differences; whether a support structure is needed should follow a foot assessment, not self-diagnosis from sole wear.

## How to do it: replacement and fitting process

1. Measure foot length and width; match the brand last in centimetres rather than relying on your usual EU or US size.
2. Fit late in the day or after a long run, wear your usual running socks, lace both shoes and walk and jog a few steps.
3. Put new shoes through the first 20-30 km on easy runs, watching for pressure or friction on toes, forefoot and heel.
4. If pain persists, stop the break-in and go back to a size or model that fits; do not force a shoe to break in.

Rotating two shoes spreads material fatigue and makes comparison easier; shoe life is mainly signalled by midsole compression, outsole wear and feel, not mileage alone.

## Quick reference: needs and shoe focus

| Need | Focus | No need to force |
|---|---|---|
| Daily easy running | Fit, stability, durability | Carbon plate or maximum stack |
| Tired late in long runs | Compare midsole rebound and weight | Race shoes as everyday trainers |
| Arch or prior-injury concerns | Assess before choosing support design | Self-diagnosing from wear |
| Trail or wet surfaces | Lug grip and puncture resistance | Road thin-soled shoes |

## Worked example: shoe size and toe allowance (hypothetical)

Assume measured foot length is 26.0 cm. With about 1 cm of room beyond the longest toe, the target insole length is about 26.0 + 1.0 = 27.0 cm. EU, UK and US labels convert differently between brands, and the same label can vary half to a full size between manufacturers, so match measured centimetres and fit rather than copying labels across brands. If one foot is longer, size to the longer one; a good fit leaves toes free to move after lacing with no heel slip.

## Worked example: mileage bookkeeping for two rotating shoes

Suppose one shoe runs 28 km per week and the other 12 km per week for 8 weeks: the first accumulates 28 × 8 = 224 km, the second 12 × 8 = 96 km, a gap of 128 km. If the first is expected around 500-600 km before replacement, at the current pace another (500 − 224) ÷ 28 ≈ 9.9 weeks remain. That mileage band is a practical reference; the real replacement signals are compressed midsole, worn-flat outsole and a harder feel, not hitting a mileage number.

## Common misconceptions

- Softer and thicker always better. An overly soft midsole can feel lossy or change landing stability; comfort and support need balancing, and materials are not injury-prevention proof.
- Judging pronation or supination from sole wear. Wear patterns depend on surface, form and gait and cannot support a self-diagnosis; abnormal pain or alignment issues need clinical assessment.
- Treating race carbon shoes as daily trainers. Thin-soled, max-stack and plated shoes serve different purposes; racing them in training changes daily intensity and feel.
- Deciding long-run performance after a few minutes of trying on. The first 20-30 km and one moderately long run are more reliable.

## When to stop

Stop breaking in the shoe and cut running for persistent foot, knee or leg pain, swelling, numbness, black toenails or obvious limping. Acute sprain, severe pain, deformity or inability to bear weight needs medical attention; do not address red-flag symptoms by swapping shoes or adding insoles. General boundaries for running-related injury are covered in [Return to running](/en/knowledge/return-to-running/).

## Frequency and progression

Check midsole compression and outsole lugs every 4 weeks and record mileage for both shoes. When replacing, change one variable at a time: either brand/model or shoe type, not shoe, socks and form together. Shoe choice for beginners follows the NHS beginner running guidance; runners with prior injuries settle load and rehab first, then discuss shoe technology.

## Buying checklist

- Measured foot length and width, fitted late in the day.
- About 1 cm room beyond the toes, no forefoot pressure, no heel slip.
- Both feet tried in usual running socks.
- Break new shoes in over 20-30 km of easy running first.
- Keep the receipt for in-period returns.

## Scope and limits

This page does not diagnose foot deformity, alignment abnormality or stress fracture, does not prescribe orthotic insoles, and does not judge whether a shoe suits an individual injury. Shoes are one part of the training system; persistent pain, swelling or restricted movement warrants medical review. Shoe specs and lifespan follow the manufacturer's official documentation; the studies cited have limited samples and do not extend to every runner.
