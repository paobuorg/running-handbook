## Definition and conditions of applicability

Fit means the last matches the foot in length, width and volume; cushioning means how the midsole absorbs impact. This page applies to runners choosing shoes for daily running and races. Solve fit first; only with a good fit does cushioning matter, and soft foam cannot rescue a shoe that rubs.

## Fit tests, timing and pronation labels

Three shop checks: toe space, heel hold, forefoot width. Leave about a thumb's width ahead of the longest toe, stop the heel sliding when laced, and keep the forefoot unsqueezed. Sizes vary between brands, so ignore the number.

| Check | Pass | Fail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Toe space | A thumb's width ahead | Toes jammed, black toenails |
| Heel hold | No sliding when laced | Heel lifts each step |
| Forefoot width | Toes can spread | Pressure outside |

Try shoes at the usual training time, in running socks, walking then jogging a few steps. The NHS suggests a shop fitting and notes expensive shoes are not essential. A pair that pinches should go back. Judging "overpronation" by eye or sole wear is unreliable. Without pain or a functional problem, no label requires a corrective shoe. Recurrent sprains, marked instability, numbness or a structural foot problem need professional assessment. Shoes change load distribution, not strength.

## Cushioning evidence: association, not cause

A secondary analysis of a randomised trial with 527 recreational runners found moderate or high perceived heel cushioning associated with lower injury risk, and higher overall shoe appreciation also associated with lower risk. This is an association, not proof: softer shoes are not shown to prevent injury, and softer is not always better.

## Replacement signals and review interval

The NHS lists unsupportive or worn-out shoes among factors contributing to heel pain; signs are a worn outsole, collapsed cushioning, a damaged upper and reduced comfort. Check the main pair's outsole and heel every eight weeks, and do not debut new shoes in a race.

## Blisters: friction, moisture and fit

Most blisters heal in about a week without seeing a GP: keep them clean, cover with a soft plaster, and do not burst them. Poorly fitting shoes are a common cause of friction; moist skin and warmth raise risk.

## How to do it: three steps

1. Pass all three fit checks before comparing anything else.
2. Try shoes at the usual training time, in running socks, walking and jogging a few steps.
3. Use the pair for two or three easy runs, logging blisters, rubbing and next-day feel.

## Worked example: two-pair mileage bookkeeping (hypothetical)

Assume 40 km a week and a 65 kg runner: 28 km easy in pair A, 12 km quality in pair B. After eight weeks A has about 224 km and B about 96 km, a gap of 128 km. If A's heel outsole is worn to the midsole, retire A, let B cover two weeks of easy running (about 56 km), and start testing a new pair. These figures are hypothetical, not findings.

## Common misconceptions

- "Softer is always better": this is an association, and very soft shoes can feel unstable.
- "Mileage alone decides replacement": outsole, foam and comfort are more direct.

## When to stop and seek care

Stop and seek medical or physiotherapy assessment for persistent pain, swelling, numbness, recurrent sprains, difficulty walking, or a blister that becomes red and discharging. NHS advice is not to run while in pain, and to seek advice when pain is severe, persistent or comes with swelling.

## Checklist

- All three fit checks pass, tried at running time.
- No blisters or rubbing over two or three easy runs.
- Mileage and use case logged for each pair.
- Outsole, heel and upper checked every eight weeks.
- Races and long runs use tested shoes and socks.

## Scope and limits

This page does not diagnose foot type, gait or injury, or recommend a brand. Shoes cannot replace strength work, load management or medical assessment; reduced sensation or skin-healing problems need professional guidance.
