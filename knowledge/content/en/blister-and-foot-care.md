## What this page solves

Blister causation is not mysterious: friction, moisture, temperature and pressure separate epidermis from dermis. The hard part is controlling those three in long distances, rain, sand or wet socks, and treating a blister without turning it into an infection.

This is field care a runner can execute, not a substitute for a clinician. Runners with diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, immunosuppression or reduced sensation should treat any foot break as a medical problem first.

## Where blisters come from

| Factor | Effect | Common setting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Friction | Repeated sliding between shoe, sock and skin | New shoes, wet socks, tight laces |
| Moisture | Softened skin, higher friction | Rain, river crossings, sweat |
| Temperature | Local heat accelerates tissue damage | Asphalt, desert, long descent |
| Pressure | Local high-pressure points | Narrow toe box, wrinkled tongue, sock seams |
| Shear | Layers move relative to each other | Braking on descents, sharp turns |

Foot shape is only one factor. Most blisters come from “new shoe + wet sock + long descent”, not from a defective foot.

## Three-layer prevention

**Layer 1: shoes and socks.** Race shoes must be broken in over at least 2–3 long runs; leave half to one finger's width at the toe, lock the midfoot and relax the forefoot laces. Choose quick-drying socks without coarse seams; cotton is worst when wet. Pre-tape or lubricate known hot spots before pain starts.

**Layer 2: moisture.** After rain or river crossings, change to dry socks as soon as possible; if none, dry the feet, apply a small amount of talc or anti-friction powder, and put the socks back on. Check toe webs and arch every 2–3 hours, not only the sole.

**Layer 3: pressure and shear.** On descents, shorten stride and raise cadence to reduce sliding; do not over-tighten laces across the instep. Feet swell over distance; half a size of room is more realistic than “perfect fit” at the start.

## Treating a blister

| Situation | Action | Do not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small, intact, not affecting gait | Clean, cover with anti-friction/blister dressing, monitor | Pop it routinely |
| Large, tense, changing gait | Disinfect, drain at the edge, keep roof, cover with sterile dressing | Tear off the entire roof |
| Ruptured, clean base | Clean, antibacterial ointment, non-adherent dressing | Touch repeatedly with dirty fingers |
| Pus, redness, heat, worsening pain | Stop racing, seek medical care | Strap it tighter and continue |
| Diabetes/circulation problem | Do not self-treat; hand to medical staff | Self-drain or self-medicate |

Drain with a sterile needle from the side, one small opening, letting fluid out slowly; the roof is the best natural dressing. Do not use hair or thread, and do not cut the whole roof away.

## Black toenails and blood blisters

Black toenails usually come from repeated toe-box impact, common on long descents and short toe boxes. If an acute subungual haematoma is tense and too painful to wear a shoe, medical staff should decompress it; do not use a heated needle yourself. Afterwards, enlarge the toe box, shorten descent stride and lock the midfoot to prevent recurrence.

Blood blisters follow the same principles: keep the roof, drain, cover. If a blood blister sits on a main weight-bearing point, downgrade the goal to “walk to the finish” rather than continuing at pace.

## Infection and when to stop

Stop racing and seek care for any of: spreading redness, fever, pus, a red line up the calf, severe throbbing, inability to weight-bear, fever or chills. A diabetic runner should treat even a small break as high risk, not “see tomorrow”.

Do not mask infection with antibiotic ointment; do not wrap the foot airtight to continue. Infection is not rare in ultra runners; stopping early is more likely to save the season.

## Two worked examples

**Rainy 100K, blister between toes.** Change socks at 30 km, dry the foot and apply a blister dressing. At 60 km, a tense blister is drained from the side after disinfection, roof preserved, non-adherent dressing applied. At 80 km, spreading redness or throbbing means stop. Do not stuff cotton between toes; it creates a new friction point.

**Desert multi-day, hot spots on the sole.** Apply anti-friction balm before each start and check hot spots in the afternoon. An intact small blister gets a dressing, not drainage. If day three makes weight-bearing painful, switch to a shoe half a size larger, reduce running and walk; pus means withdraw and seek care.

## After the race

Wash, dry and inspect every toe and web; change blister dressings every 24–48 hours and watch redness and exudate. Do not soak in hot water for more than 10 minutes or massage hard over broken skin. Return to running only when the wound is closed and walking is pain-free, then wear the race shoe again.

## Foot-care kit: what and where

The kit does not need to be large, but it must open in rain, at night and with shaking hands. Pack in a waterproof pouch, keep it in the pack's top pocket or crew drop bag, and restock before every race.

| Item | Quantity | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sterile needles with caps | 2–3 | Drain large blisters |
| Blister/anti-friction dressings | 4–6 | Cover hot spots and blisters |
| Non-adherent dressings | 3–4 | Ruptured blisters |
| Small saline/antiseptic | 1 | Clean |
| Antibacterial ointment | Small tube | Protect open wounds |
| Dry socks | 2 pairs | After rain/river |
| Small scissors | 1 | Trim dressing, not skin |
| Gauze/tissue | Several | Dry, press |

Do not carry folk remedies such as petrol, glue or tobacco; they damage skin and raise infection risk.

## 24 hours before

Do not trim calluses, do not tear old skin, do not try new shoes. Callus is natural protection; removing it exposes deeper skin. Cut toenails 1–2 weeks early; on race eve, clean and apply anti-friction balm only. If you blister easily, pre-tape hot spots, but not so thickly that it creates a new pressure point.

## 72 hours after

| Time | Action | Watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–24 h | Wash, dry, inspect every toe web | Redness, exudate |
| 24–48 h | Change dressings; avoid hot soaking | Pus, fever |
| 48–72 h | Walk test in wide shoes | Is pain falling? |

If redness spreads up the calf, fever or throbbing appears, treat as infection and seek care; do not keep “soaking and disinfecting”. Return to running only when the wound is closed and walking is pain-free.

## Common errors

- Leaving an intact blister until it changes gait.
- Threading a blister with cotton; the thread becomes an infection path.
- Tearing off the entire roof and doubling the pain.
- Running on wet socks with macerated toe webs.
- Trimming calluses or cutting nails too short before a race.
- A diabetic runner self-draining a blister.

None of these require experience to avoid; a checklist beats improvisation.

## Laces and socks in detail

Tighter laces are not more stable. Lock the midfoot, leave the toe box relaxed so toes can spread; retie before a long descent so the foot does not slide into the toe cap. Socks must be smooth, with no seam across toe webs or arch; a tight cuff changes calf circulation, especially late in a long race.

New socks need breaking in too. A new sock, insole or shoe on race day can create friction exactly where blister risk is highest. “Race kit” is ready only after it has been used in 2–3 long runs.

## How often to check

Check every 2–3 hours in a long race; immediately after rain or a river crossing. Order: toe webs, arch, heel, sole hot spots. Earlier detection means simpler treatment; once pain changes gait, the cost has already grown.

## Taping and lubrication order

Clean and dry the foot first, apply the anti-friction patch to the hot spot, then lubricate the shoe-sock interface and put the sock on. Never tape over broken or infected skin. More than two layers creates a new pressure point; cotton or wool between toes absorbs sweat and hardens into a friction source. Test the exact combination in a long run before race day.

## Wet feet and multi-day sock rules

In multi-day races, change socks whenever feet are wet, dry the toes fully before the new sock, and if no dry pair exists, dry the foot and air the shoe first. Store wet socks in a waterproof bag, never back in the shoe. Two dry pairs in rotation matter more than a spare shoe. In sustained rain, a sock hung outside overnight is still wet; treat it as wet.
