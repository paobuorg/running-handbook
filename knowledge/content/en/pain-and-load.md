## Definition and who this applies to

Running-related pain is local discomfort during or after running linked to training load; it may be sharp, dull, tender or swollen. It applies to local pain without obvious trauma or fever where walking is still possible. The approach holds when you reduce the provoking load and record the response instead of testing pain tolerance. The NHS advises not running while in pain and seeking medical or physiotherapy advice when pain is severe, persistent or accompanied by swelling. This page does not diagnose.

## Pain is information, not a willpower test

When pain appears, stop or reduce intensity. Observe whether it worsens with continued running, changes your gait, or comes with swelling, weakness or a joint that catches. Pushing through does not become safe through adaptation. Location, timing and provoking movement guide the next step better than a vague label.

## Rule out signals that need urgent action

After a twist, fall or collision, check weight-bearing, deformity and rapid swelling. Inability to bear weight, deformity, fever, pain waking you at night or steadily worsening pain need assessment, not self-observation. Chest pain, fainting, unusual palpitations or severe breathlessness are emergencies: stop and contact local emergency services.

## Record five dimensions of pain

Record location, start time, provoking movement, duration and previous load. Provoking movement may be uphill, downhill, acceleration, first steps after sitting or a shoe change. “Knee pain” becomes useful as “starts at 3 kilometres, worse downhill, eases after 10 minutes of rest”. Also record effects on walking, stairs and sleep; a 0–10 score is rough, not a substitute for function.

## How to do it

1. Stop or reduce load for an assumed two or three days, avoiding the provoking speed, gradient and surface; keep pain-free walking or cycling and observe.
2. Test with run-walk: assume one minute running plus one minute walking, ten times, about 20 minutes. If the run and the next day do not worsen, continue.
3. Increase distance or pace only after several pain-free sessions, one variable at a time; if pain returns, step back.

## Load variables at a glance

| Variable | Adjustment during pain | What to watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distance | Cut to half or less | Pain 24 hours later |
| Pace | Drop quality sessions | Sharp pain when accelerating |
| Gradient | Avoid continuous downhill | Swelling after descending |
| Frequency | Run fewer days | First steps the next day |
| Surface | Use flat, familiar ground | Instability on uneven ground |

“Half” and “drop quality sessions” are directions, not a prescription.

## Frequency, cycle and return to running

During pain, run less often and keep pain-free activity. Assume a two-week window: pain-free activity first, short run-walk second. If pain does not settle in two weeks or function worsens, seek assessment. Return frequency and duration before pace and gradient; assume three or four sessions at the same load first. Do not raise distance, speed and climbing together.

## Worked example (assuming 32 kilometres weekly)

Assume a runner did four runs and 32 kilometres weekly, then developed front-knee pain but could walk. Weeks one and two: two 30-minute run-walk sessions, about 12 kilometres weekly, no speed or downhill. After three sessions without worsening, add about 10% weekly, roughly 1.2 kilometres; by week eight, return to about 20 kilometres. If pain lasts into the next day, step back one week. These numbers are assumptions; real plans depend on assessment.

## Pain and weekly load: a coupled criterion (a practical frame, not a universal threshold)

Pain and weekly volume are read together, not one at a time. Assume the runner cuts weekly volume from 32 to 24 kilometres (about −25%) in weeks one and two, the score falls from 4/10 to 2/10, and walking is unaffected; only after three sessions at the same load without worsening does volume rise to 24 × 1.10 ≈ 26.4 kilometres (about +10%). The criterion holds when three things move together: the pain score drops, the 24-hour and next-day first-step response do not worsen, and volume changes by one small step in one direction. If any reverses, hold the prior week's volume and do not add distance, pace and gradient together. This site has not verified a universal "stop running above this pain score" or "injury above this volume percentage" threshold; the 0–10 score is a rough reference, and a changed gait matters more than the number.

## Common misconceptions

Across 36 studies and 23,047 runners, 26.2% reported a running-related injury, but evidence linking weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity or recent changes to injury was conflicting; the authors caution against one optimal progression.

- “Pain settles if ignored.” Persistent or worsening pain may take longer to resolve when training continues.
- “Rest means complete stillness.” Relative rest keeps activity within a pain-free range.
- “Shoes or stretching solve every pain.” They may be factors but do not replace assessing load, function and history.

## Stop signals, risk and seeking care

The NHS advises not running in pain and seeking advice when pain is severe, persistent or accompanied by swelling. Inability to bear weight, deformity, fever, night pain, worsening pain, chest pain, fainting, palpitations or severe breathlessness need prompt care; emergencies require local emergency services. Persistent fatigue with clear performance decline should raise overtraining syndrome; there is no single diagnostic marker, assessment is clinical, and management is rest plus correcting the training-recovery imbalance.

## Self-check list

- [ ] Location, provoking movement, duration and previous load are recorded
- [ ] Provoking speed, gradient and distance are reduced or removed
- [ ] Pain at 24 hours and on next-day first steps is recorded
- [ ] No inability to bear weight, marked swelling, fever or night pain
- [ ] Load rises only after several pain-free sessions, one variable at a time

## Scope and limits

This page does not diagnose, recommend medication, replace imaging or examination, or promise that form, shoes or stretching will cure pain. Causes may involve previous injury, bone stress, tendons, muscles or other medical issues; do not delay assessment. Judging persistent fatigue and performance decline needs clinical context.

## Green, amber, red: grade by function, not by a pain score

Pain intensity alone does not decide whether you can run. Function matters more: can you walk normally, is your gait changing, is the pain worse the next day, does it affect sleep and daily life? Record these three bands in your training log; they are more useful than memorising a 0–10 score.

| Band | Presentation | Training response | Review |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Green | Mild discomfort, settles within 24 h, normal gait | Keep load, reduce speed and climbing | 3–5 days |
| Amber | Pain rises during running, persists beyond 24 h, gait slightly changed | Cut volume 30–50%, stop quality, cross-train | 48–72 h |
| Red | Rest/night pain, limping, swelling, numbness, sudden weakness | Stop running and jumping; seek assessment | Same day |

Amber is a loading window, not permission to push through. If it escalates to red, or does not improve in two weeks, get professional assessment.

## Return-to-run gates: walk before run

| Stage | Content | Pass criterion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Walk 20–30 min normally | No increase next day |
| 2 | Walk 5 min / jog 1 min × 4–6 | Pain ≤2/10 at 24 h |
| 3 | Continuous easy jog 10–20 min | Normal gait, no next-day flare |
| 4 | Easy running every other day | Stable for a week before quality |

Repeat each stage 2–3 times; do not progress daily. If pain rises during the run and does not settle afterwards, go back one stage.

## Red flags differ by site

“Knee pain” can involve the patellofemoral joint, IT band, meniscus or a stress injury, and these do not behave the same. This article cannot diagnose, but it can flag signals that should not wait.

| Site | Common load-related pattern | Seek prompt assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Front of knee | Pain on stairs or squatting | Locking, marked swelling, cannot weight-bear |
| Outside knee | Outer pain after volume increase | Night pain, rapid worsening |
| Inner shin | Focal tibial tenderness | Pinpoint severe pain, night pain, throbbing |
| Achilles | Morning stiffness, pain with loading | Sudden “kick” sensation, cannot single-leg raise |
| Sole/foot | First-step pain | Persistent swelling, limping, numbness |
| Hip/glute | Pain after sitting, outer hip when running | Fever, night pain, progressive weakness |

## Two worked examples

**Amber knee pain:** a runner doing 60 km/week develops front-of-knee pain that rises from 3/10 to 5/10 and is still present at 36 hours, with near-normal gait. Response: cut the week to 35–40 km, remove intervals and downhill, keep easy running; glute medius and quadriceps strength twice; review on day 5. If pain settles below 2/10 within 24 hours, add back about 10% per week.

**Red shin pain:** focal severe pain on the inner shin, pain at night and a limp. Stop running and jumping; do not “test” it. Seek prompt assessment for a stress injury. None of the return gates apply until a diagnosis and imaging opinion are available.

## Four load variables to review

Pain changes rarely come from one session. Write down the last seven days: total volume, climbing and descent, number of quality sessions, sleep and life stress. If pain appeared after more climbing, cut descent and speed first. If it appeared in a poor-sleep week, repair recovery first. Change one variable and observe 5–7 days so you can identify the driver.
