## Conclusion: which to choose when

The first principle of a lighting setup is redundancy before brightness. The dominant risk in night trail running and pre-dawn starts is the lamp failing suddenly, not the lamp being dim. Most race mandatory clauses write spare batteries, a second lamp or a backup power source rather than a minimum lumen figure: secure the redundancy first, then choose output and beam. The main lamp solves seeing the ground and trail ahead; the spare lamp or battery covers you if the main lamp drops out or batteries sag in the cold; the rear red light makes you visible to other runners and vehicles.

Short decision: night ultras and pre-dawn starts carry a fitted main lamp plus an independent backup; where the category rules require two lamps, comply with that; for urban night runs under streetlights and traffic, the priority flips to being seen, where reflective clothing and a rear light matter more than a long-distance beam. Read lumen ratings against a standards protocol and together with mode burn time: in one official sample the 300 lm top mode lasted about 2.5 hours, while the 7 lm lowest mode reached 100 hours. The visibility background for running near roads is covered in [Night running and traffic](/en/knowledge/night-running-and-traffic/).

| Race condition | First setup | Alternative | Decision point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Night ultra, pre-dawn start, unlit mountain trail | Main lamp + independent spare lamp / spare batteries | Two interchangeable main lamps | Redundancy first, then brightness; rules may require two lamps |
| Winter training, cold conditions | Main lamp + spare batteries carried against the body | Swappable rechargeable model | Cold-weather sag is the main failure; keep backup power off the pack exterior |
| Urban night run, streetlights and traffic | Reflective clothing + rear red light | Lightweight main lamp | Being seen first; a long beam adds little on roads |
| Route-finding in low-visibility fog | Flood-oriented main lamp | Spot supplement | Reading nearby markers and the ground beats long throw |

## Option by option

The **main lamp** is the only part of the system responsible for seeing the trail. In the official Petzl Tikkina sample: 300 lumens under the ANSI/PLATO FL 1 protocol, 92 g, wide beam, IPX4 weather resistance, running on three AAA/LR03 cells (included) or an optional CORE rechargeable battery, compatible with alkaline, lithium and Ni-MH cells. Its performance table separates brightness, distance and burn time by mode. On three AAA cells: low mode 7 lm, 10 m, 100 h; standard mode 100 lm, 40 m, 10 h; max power 300 lm, 65 m, 2.5 h, with 20 h reserve lighting on the higher modes. On the CORE battery: 7 lm 120 h, 100 lm 7 h, 300 lm 3 h. The key reading: lumens are the instantaneous output of one mode, not the sustained brightness for the whole race; estimate burn time for the mode and cells you will actually use.

The **spare lamp and spare batteries** are redundancy, not a second luxury lighting system. On the rules side: Lavaredo 120K/80K requires one headlamp with spare batteries, or two headlamps with batteries; UTA Miler, 100 and 50 require a headlamp plus a small backup light; MIUT Legend and Advanced require two headlamps with spare batteries or a power bank, plus a flashing red rear light, while Discover requires one headlamp with spare batteries or a power bank. The backup must be electrically independent of the main lamp: do not mix spare and main cells in one compartment, and the spare lamp should fit on your head or clip to the vest on its own.

**Price and risk boundary.** A pricier headlamp mainly buys beam optics, battery compatibility, switch workmanship and cold-weather discharge stability; for safety, a backup arrangement matters more than the main lamp's top brightness. A lightweight basic model creates no risk on a short, streetlit urban outing. On long night runs, in cold conditions, or where rules require two lamps, dropping the independent redundancy is the real risk.

The **rear red light** solves being seen, not lighting the trail. FHWA-cited NHTSA data show that about half of pedestrian fatalities occur between 6 p.m. and midnight; in night crashes with pedestrians, eight in ten drivers reported they did not see the person; a pedestrian in dark clothing may first be seen about 55 feet away. The same logic applies to following runners, cyclists and support vehicles on a trail: a flashing red dot facing rearward materially lowers rear-end risk. In fog and dense woods, light scatters off moisture and leaves, and a flood beam makes you easier to spot at close range than a narrow spot.

## Conditions for choosing a main lamp

**Choose a flood-oriented main lamp if:**

- You run mountain paths and singletrack and need to read rocks, roots and nearby markers underfoot;
- The pace is fast and the lamp sweeps the trail as your head turns, where a wide beam is more useful;
- You accept dropping to standard mode through a long night to trade brightness for safety margin.

**Choose a spot or mixed beam if:**

- The route is open with straight sections where distant descents and turns need reading early;
- You already have a flood-trained base and spot is a supplementary mode;
- The official specs offer a switchable beam rather than a single long-throw lamp.

## Conditions for choosing a backup

**Carry spare batteries or a spare lamp if:**

- The race crosses night, starts pre-dawn, or the rules explicitly require redundancy;
- Temperatures are low and mid-race cell sag is plausible;
- The main lamp uses removable cells and you can carry the same model separately;
- The section is far from an exit, so lamp failure cannot end the race immediately.

**Add a rear red light if:**

- The trail shares space with following runners, cyclists or support vehicles;
- You are mid-pack or back of pack and easy to overtake;
- The current rules require a rear light, or you often start in low visibility.

## What not to do

1. **Choose by top-mode lumens alone.** Ignoring mode burn time, cell chemistry and beam type can leave you with a lamp that is "bright for ten minutes"; estimate runtime for the mode you will use.
2. **Store spare and main batteries together and skip fresh cells before the start.** Redundancy means an independent power source; one sag should not take out both main and backup.
3. **Test the spare lamp only at the finish line.** The switch, head clip and cell compartment must have been used in training; fumbling in the dark does not go smoothly.
4. **Use a long-throw lamp instead of trail lighting.** A beam aimed far ahead leaves your feet in a dark zone, hiding rocks and puddles; a trail main beam should cover the near ground first.
5. **Ignore fog and forest scattering.** In dense fog a too-strong beam reflects back at you and cuts visibility; lower the output and widen the beam instead.
6. **Carry urban night-run logic onto trails.** Roads have streetlights and traffic, where being seen is the core; unlit mountain trails are about reading the ground, and the weightings differ.

## Common failure modes and early signals

- **Cold-weather battery sag.** Early signals: sudden drop on the charge display, dimming lamp, low mode not reaching its expected runtime; alkaline cells show this more in the cold. Stop rule: switch to backup power, keep cells against the body, and record actual runtime by temperature afterward.
- **Main lamp drained with no redundancy.** Early signals: main lamp down to low glow, backup buried where you cannot reach it; continuing leaves you disabled in a dark section. Stop rule: carry the spare where one hand can reach it, and plan a cell swap once main charge passes halfway.
- **Beam only lights your feet, not the trail ahead.** Early signals: late reactions on descents, frequent head-lifting to find the route, markers at the edge of vision; usually a beam set too low or too narrow. Stop rule: adjust the lamp tilt, switch to mixed/flood, and fix the most comfortable angle in training.
- **Fit loosens or slips.** Early signals: sweaty band slipping, lamp body sinking with stride, repeated hand adjustments; especially dangerous on descents. Stop rule: retighten the band, check the sweat strap and clip points, and replace a worn band after the event.

## Worked examples: two night setups (assumed)

**Example one: a 10-hour mountain ultra with a 3 a.m. start near freezing.** Assume rules require dual lamps or a headlamp plus spare batteries. Use a wide-beam main lamp with fresh cells at the start; carry same-model spare cells in a vest pocket against the body, plus a small spare headlamp. Run standard mode early to preserve runtime, raise output in dense forest and descents; keep the rear red light on facing back. If the main lamp dims at hour six, swap spare cells first, then use the spare lamp to finish if still short.

**Example two: a 12 km urban greenway night run with streetlights and a few cyclists.** Assume no wilderness trail lighting is required. Prioritize reflective clothing and a rear red light, with a lightweight spare main lamp; brightness is enough to read the path edge, not a long throw.

Both examples are instructional assumptions. Actual runtime depends on temperature, mode, cell aging and pace; run a full night section in training to validate, and pair route reading with [Trail navigation](/en/knowledge/trail-navigation/).

## Boundaries

This page is running gear and safety education, not electrical modification advice. Weather ratings follow the official label; IPX4 means weather resistant, not immersion proof. Race mandatory equipment can change year to year; redundancy clauses, rear-light requirements and cell specifications follow the current official handbook. Lighting choice cannot replace route familiarity and weather judgment; the broader safety framework is in [Route safety](/en/knowledge/route-safety/).

## What would change this conclusion

- **Section environment:** unlit trails, urban roads, dense forest and fog night weight output, beam and visibility differently.
- **Temperature:** cold directly compresses usable cell capacity, changing backup power and insulation planning.
- **Race rules:** dual-lamp, spare-cell, power-bank or rear-light requirements are published by category and weather, and may shift before the start.
- **Gear updates:** LED efficiency, cell chemistry and lamp weight keep evolving; the page is revised when official specs change.
- **Personal vision and pace:** age, dark adaptation and pace affect the brightness and beam width you need.
