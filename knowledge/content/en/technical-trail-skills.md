## Bottom line

This page is for runners already comfortable on established trails who need to handle scree, roots, mud, stone steps and wet rock. It does not apply with acute ankle or knee pain, poor balance, or when a first trail outing lands on an unknown technical section. The decision order is: read the ground and pick the foothold first, then decide whether to run or walk; when footing is uncertain, walking is safer than running. Ankle sprains are the most common acute trail injury, and they mostly occur when control drops under fatigue and form changes on variable terrain. Keep four rules: short stride, stable landing, low steady centre of mass, eyes a few steps ahead. If any of these breaks down, slow down or walk. This is a technique and risk framework, not coaching, navigation training or medical assessment.

## Definition and preconditions

Technical terrain means discontinuous trail with uncertain footholds and grip that changes with wetness and vegetation: scree slopes, tangled roots, mud, stone steps and wet rock. This page covers sections walkable without climbing or deep water; scrambling, deep crossings and ice are outside its scope. Preconditions: the route is known in advance or has an alternative; no acute joint pain that day; navigation backup, water and basic insulation are carried. If these fail, shorten the route or move to an established path. Gait and eccentric load on descents are covered in detail on the [downhill running technique](/en/knowledge/downhill-running-technique/) page.

## Why sprains are the most common acute trail injury

A trail-running review reports that most acute and chronic musculoskeletal injuries occur in the lower leg, mainly the knee and ankle; over 70% are overuse injuries, and ankle sprains are the most common acute injury. The key mechanisms are inadequate neuromotor control, balance and coordination, running through fatigue, and abnormal kinematics on variable terrain. For technical sections the implication is direct: sprains rarely happen when warmed up, familiar with the ground and in control; they cluster late in a run, in poor sightlines, with rushed footfalls or when pushing through fatigue. An ankle sprain stretches supporting ligaments past their limit and tears them; severity depends on how many ligaments are involved and how much they tear. Inability to bear weight, marked instability or deformity needs prompt medical assessment.

## Terrain-action-risk table

| Terrain | Action | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scree / loose dirt slope | Small light steps, feet offset across the slope texture, pick each point; do not reach across loose rock | Sliding out, ankle inversion, sideward fall |
| Roots / tangled roots | Read the bump or hollow first, plant on the firm root base; lift over rather than scraping past; fast walk in dense roots | Tripping, stepping into a hollow, unstable landing |
| Mud / wet grass | Slow down, plant on tussocks or firm ground, short flat steps, less vertical push; avoid deep foot sinks | Slipping, sinking, low grip leading to an ankle roll |
| Stone steps | Read each height and edge, steady descent; no continuous step-running; side-step or walk on broken or slick edges | Missing a step, tripping, edge slip, braking load on the knee |
| Wet rock / moss | Walk, use hands for stable points; avoid shiny water-covered planes; route around if needed | Sideways slip, fall, wrist injury from bracing |

## Surface-by-surface detail

**Scree and loose dirt.** Gravel moves under the foot, so land light and short, testing before loading weight. Across a scree slope, keep an angle to the hillside and spread weight over both feet; do not load one foot with the centre of mass tilted sideways. On scree descents do not stride long: a bigger stride means a bigger slide per step.

**Roots and tangles.** Lift the foot over raised roots and land on the root base where it meets the ground. Roots hidden under leaves are probed or pressed firm before landing. In dense root sections break the rhythm into one confirmed step at a time, using a hand on a trunk or side support when needed.

**Mud and wet grass.** Grip is low, so keep movements flat and light, avoiding high-centre-of-mass pushes; favour exposed tussocks, stones and firm ridges. Read wheel ruts and shallow pits before stepping in rather than dropping straight in.

**Stone steps.** Step height changes more abruptly than on dirt, so scan the next two or three steps. Bend the knee to absorb each drop; do not lean back and brake with the shin alone. Rounded or mossy edges are treated as wet rock.

**Wet rock and moss.** This is the surface where running most often should stop. With water or moss, grip drops sharply and walking can still slip. Shrink the step to the minimum, plant on rough, dark, non-reflective patches; if no secure landing exists, route around rather than traversing along a rock edge.

## When to walk rather than run

The criterion is not how steep the slope is but whether foothold and grip are controllable. Walk when the ground is wet or mossy and the next landing cannot be read; when continuous roots, steps or scree need point-by-point confirmation; when quads and calves are already burning and foothold choices slow; when attention is split by fatigue, wind or conversation; or on a narrow trail with a drop beside it. Dry, firm, clearly visible gentle slopes and dirt tracks can keep a compact controlled run. The review also notes that real-time adjustment of cadence, step length and knee flexion on the trail may reduce impact-related risk; when adjustment is no longer possible, walking is the most direct adjustment available.

## High-risk movements for ankle sprain

These movements most often trigger an inversion sprain and should be avoided on technical ground:

- **Reaching the foot forward onto loose gravel, then rolling the ankle.** A far landing forces the supporting foot to invert on an uneven surface before the lower leg can react.
- **Long strides over ditches and roots while fatigued.** Balance and reaction drop late in a run; an unstable landing lets the ankle roll over.
- **Loading the outside foot while side-hilling.** The outside foot bears weight at a large inversion angle, concentrating load on the supporting ligaments.
- **Running down steps continuously and missing one.** A missed step forces the foot to twist on the step edge.
- **Quick direction changes or stops on slick ground.** With low grip the ankle turns before the body does.
- **Looking only at the foot under you.** Hidden roots, loose stones and hollows are missed, and the landing becomes a passive choice.
- **Earphones blocking environmental sound and splitting attention.** Reactions to ground noise and partner warnings slow down.

## Decision table: state, terrain and switch

| State / terrain | Running call | Action points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fresh, dry dirt, gentle slope | Controlled running OK | Short stride, landing under the body, eyes ahead |
| Fair, scree or root section | Fast walk or run-walk mix | Confirm each point, use a hand when needed |
| Late-run fatigue, any technical section | Default to walking | Keep balance and reaction; do not practise skills tired |
| Wet rock, moss, deep mud | Walk, route around if needed | Small steps, low centre, stable support points |
| Tight ankle or discomfort on a previously sprained side | Stop running, assess before continuing | Do not push through a technical section on a brace alone |

## Worked example: pace and walk-run switch on a technical section (assumptions stated)

Numbers below are a hypothetical demonstration, not a route standard. Assume a 12 km trail route with 3 km of mixed scree and roots; the runner's flat pace is 6:00 per km and ordinary dirt descent 7:00 per km. On the technical section, walking pace is about 10:30-12:00 per km. Forcing 7:00 per km there increases footfalls and per-step slip risk, and adds fatigue earlier in the run. Assume 800 m of wet stone steps within the section; at a 12:00 per km walking pace that takes about 16 minutes. The time cannot be made back by accelerating, because a sprain or slip costs far more than a few minutes. Actual time varies with slope, stride and fatigue; the order is to confirm safe footing first, then talk about speed.

## Common misconceptions

- **Hard technical ground is cleared on speed.** Higher speed enlarges the displacement of a single mistake; the goal on technical sections is stable passage, not split time.
- **Cushioned soft shoes solve scree.** Shoe shape and lug depth affect grip and protection but cannot replace foothold choice; a rock plate matters only on routes with sharp rock and roots.
- **A brace lets a previously sprained ankle run a technical section.** Bracing reduces recurrence risk, but incomplete strength and balance rehabilitation is the common cause of chronic instability; a brace does not replace training.
- **Following the person ahead is enough.** Their line may not match your stride and landing; smooth passage for them when fresh does not mean you can repeat it.
- **Pushing hard in mud makes you more stable.** A strong push in soft mud sinks and slips; keep the action light and flat.

## Failure modes and stop rules

1. **Heavier landings, starting to trip.** Early signs: dragging steps, catching roots or gravel. Shorten the stride immediately, switch to fast walking, and focus on footholds.
2. **Tightening ankle or slight giving way.** If either ankle feels soft or uncomfortable, especially a previously sprained side, stop running and walk it out; persistent pain, swelling or inability to bear weight is treated as an acute sprain and assessed medically.
3. **Form changes under fatigue.** Burning thighs, unstable single-leg support, slower foothold choice: leave the technical section; do not first attempt an unfamiliar hard move late in a long run.
4. **Weather or surface turning worse.** Rain-wet rock, gravel under leaves, rising shallow ditches all change the risk level; re-judge at the more conservative setting and turn back if needed.

## Scope and limits

This page is a trail technique and risk framework; it does not grant passage rights, real-time conditions or rescue guarantees for any specific route, and private land, reserves and race routes set their own rules. Advice rests on group patterns from reviews and clinical guidelines; individual strength, balance, sprain history and gear differ widely. Anyone with cardiovascular, joint or balance conditions should consult a professional first. Fracture signs, obvious deformity, numbness, cool or discoloured skin, or inability to bear weight require emergency or orthopaedic assessment outside this framework.

## What changes this advice

- **Surface and weather.** Wetness, leaf cover, moss, snow and water level change grip and risk; the same terrain takes a different action grade in different conditions.
- **Body state and fatigue.** Late in a run, after poor sleep or with muscle soreness, balance and reaction drop; technical sections default to the more conservative mode.
- **Gear choice.** Lug depth, drop, rock plate and ankle brace change landing feel and protection, but cannot replace strength and technique.
- **Evidence updates.** Trail injury epidemiology, ankle sprain prevention and footwear research keep evolving; this page is updated when higher-quality evidence changes the guidance.
