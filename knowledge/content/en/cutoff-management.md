## What this page covers, and what it does not

In a long race, whether you finish often depends less on your target pace than on the gap between you and a chain of cut-off times. This page treats the cut-off timeline as a calculable, reviewable pre-race exercise: read the official table correctly first, convert each checkpoint deadline into the slowest legal pace for that segment, work out the buffer left by your target finish, and set the conditions under which you switch from racing a target time to simply finishing.

This page does not replace three things. First, it does not replace that edition's official regulations, race guide, or on-course timing officials; any specific clock time follows the current edition's announcement. Second, it does not replace medical judgment: red-flag symptoms outrank the cut-off line, and withdrawal and medical procedures are covered on the [DNF decision](/en/knowledge/dnf-decision-making/) page. Third, it does not replace on-course feel and weather adjustment; heat, climbs, night fatigue and aid-station queues all change real arrival times, and the even-pace projection is only a starting point.

The page is written for runners of road marathons, road ultras and trail ultras who have, or can find, an official segment cut-off table. Backyard-style loop events use a different time structure, explained separately below.

## Three forms of cut-off table

Race time limits appear in three forms, and each must be read differently. Confirm which form you are entering before planning pace.

| Form | Timing basis | How it is judged | Typical setting |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Single overall limit | Personal gun time or last-wave start time | Only whether the finish is reached within the total limit | Some road marathons set only a finish cut-off |
| Checkpoint table | Natural clock or personal gun time, listed point by point | Missing any checkpoint deadline means leaving the course | Trail ultras, road ultras, staged marathons |
| Loop elimination | Fixed duration per loop, restarted by a bell | Failing to return within the loop time eliminates you | Backyard-style loop races |

The first form puts pressure on the final segment only. The second places consecutive deadlines at mid-race aid stations, usually concentrated where climbs are longest and waits are slowest. The third has no finish-time concept at all: elimination happens at each loop's bell. World Athletics records the marathon distance as 42.195 km (26 miles 385 yards), but a shared distance does not mean shared cut-off rules; those are set by the organizer.

## Read the basis first: natural clock or personal gun time

The first thing to read on a cut-off table is not the clock times but the timing basis. Times may run on the natural clock, on your personal gun time (when you cross the start mat), or, in multi-wave events, from the last wave's start.

- **Natural-clock basis**: the cut-off is a wall-clock time. Runners in later waves who cross the mat later have less usable time than the first wave. The 2026 Shanghai Marathon rules list segment cut-offs on the natural clock: 10 km 08:50, 15 km 09:35, 20 km 10:20, 25 km 11:00, 30 km 11:45, 35 km 12:30, 40 km 13:15, finish 13:30; the marathon starts at 07:00 and medals go to finishers within 6 hours.
- **Personal gun-time basis**: the deadline runs from when you cross the start mat, so later waves are timed on their own clock. UTMB-series rules state that, when a race starts in several waves, the maximum allowed time and cut-off times are calculated from the start of the last wave.
- **Uniform cross-wave basis**: some road ultras time everyone from the last group's start rather than each runner's gun time; the 2026 Comrades edition used this basis.

Reading the basis wrong shifts the whole buffer budget. Before the race, look for "cut-off", "closing time", "gun time", "chip time" and "wave start" in the regulations or guide. If the basis is not stated, plan on the more conservative natural-clock assumption, and write your wave and mat-crossing time on the back of your bib.

## Departure time or arrival time

The second easy misreading is whether a cut-off constrains reaching the point or leaving it. In trail ultras the two differ substantially.

The Western States official aid-station table states explicitly that the listed cut-off is the latest time to **leave** the station, not to arrive; anyone leaving after the cut-off is disqualified, and it is enforced strictly. Cut-offs marked with an asterisk are default cut-offs equal to the next aid station's cut-off. The organizers also state that these cut-off times are not intended as a pacing guide. You may stop to drink, sort gear or work through stomach problems, but that stay counts against the cut-off budget; you must depart before the listed time.

Road marathon segment cut-offs are usually judged on arrival at the timing point: crossing that mat after the time means being picked up. Which basis applies at a given station follows the current edition's rules. When the rules do not say, plan conservatively for a departure-time basis and reserve the station stay inside your buffer.

## Converting the table into segment legal pace

Once you have a checkpoint table, the most useful step is turning clock times into pace, so on the course you can judge risk from watch pace instead of mental clock arithmetic.

Basic formulas (even pace within the segment):

- Segment time window = next checkpoint cut-off − previous checkpoint cut-off
- Segment cut-off pace = segment time window ÷ segment distance (minutes per km)
- Target segment pace = target total finish time ÷ total distance
- Buffer at that point = cut-off time − your predicted arrival

Three cautions. First, use official segment distances, not watch-track estimates. Second, the window must cover climb, technical surface and station stay; trail segments are legally slower than road segments, and marathon pace cannot be carried over directly. Third, buffer is a number of minutes, not a percentage: later segments are shorter but steeper, so the same 10 minutes means different things in two segments.

The following is a pure arithmetic example (assumed values, not a promise for any edition): assume 42.195 km, first wave 07:00, target finish 5:45:00, target average pace about 8:11 per km. At 35 km the cut-off is 12:30; on target pace arrival is around 11:46, leaving about 44 minutes' buffer. At the finish, cut-off 13:30, predicted arrival around 12:45, buffer about 45 minutes. A positive value means arriving before the cut-off; a negative value means the point has been missed and the regulations require leaving the course. Real per-point buffers can be computed directly in the [cut-off planner tool](/en/tools/cutoff-planner/); even splits can be checked against the [splits tool](/en/tools/splits/).

## Buffer budget: where the margin gets eaten

Buffer is not a fixed number; it is a curve that changes from start to finish. Pre-race, allocate the margin to the things known to consume time, rather than spending every minute on the most comfortable pace.

Common buffer consumers: slowdown on climbs and technical terrain; aid-station queues, bag pickup, toilet stops and gear sorting; strength drop in heat and humidity; cautious descending after dark; junction waits and crowd congestion; slower pace or stops from stomach problems. Heart rate drifts with heat and dehydration: pace can look stable while heart rate rises, so watch pace alone should not be the state check — see [heart-rate drift](/en/knowledge/heart-rate-drift/).

The budgeting method: first compute predicted arrival and buffer at each point on your target finish; then reserve non-running time per segment for climb, station density and weather; finally identify the points with the smallest buffer. The dangerous point is usually not the finish but a mid-race stretch of consecutive climb with no crew access. Make that minimum-buffer point the main focus of the pre-race plan instead of staring at the finish clock.

## When to switch from target time to finishing

There are normally two paces on race day: the target-time pace and the finish-at-all-costs pace. The switch is triggered by a buffer review, not by emotion.

Suggested switch criteria, written into the plan before the race and executed on course:

- **Keep chasing target time**: buffer at the current point is above the planned reserve line; feel, stomach and fueling are normal; later conditions are expected to hold.
- **Switch to finishing**: buffer at the current point has fallen below the reserve line, or the next section is a known long climb, heat block or night segment. Actions: drop pace to leave margin above the cut-off pace, prioritize fluids and food, cut non-essential station time, and stop pursuing the target.
- **Must leave by the rules**: a checkpoint has been missed, or a medical red flag appears. Do not try to "make it up" from there.

Fix the criteria before the race rather than weighing them on the road. Analysis of split times from a large cohort of Boston Marathon finishers found even pacing associated with the fastest finishes; strong positive splitting (fast early, slow late) produced markedly slower times, and variable pacing had the highest "wall" incidence. Suppressing early speed and keeping later buffer is itself cut-off management; charging out with the crowd most often eats the buffer in the middle miles. Note that in a modeled elite scenario with pacers and drafting protection, a positive split could save some time, but that is a protected-phase model result and does not transfer to mass-participation racing.

## Course cut-off versus course closure

Two finishing procedures are easily confused, and their rules differ.

- **Cut-off**: runners past a point deadline leave the course and are carried by official sweep transport to the finish area or a designated point; boarding the transport records a DNF and you cannot rejoin. Comrades rules state that a runner who misses a route or finish cut-off must immediately retire, leave the route as directed and use official withdrawal transport where instructed; on the 2026 edition, rescue buses ran at designated cut-off points and along the course, and boarding one recorded the number and marked the runner DNF.
- **Course open until**: the course stays closed until a time, after which closure and sweep are no longer guaranteed; medical support may remain on course after the cut-off, but some race amenities may be removed and the finish area may begin breakdown. The NYRR generic policy closes the finish line at a 20-minute-per-mile pace plus the 20 minutes the start remains open; it also states that medical support remains available on course after the cut-off, though some amenities may be removed and breakdown may begin at the finish.
- **Loop elimination**: a backyard ultra has no finish cut-off table. Each loop starts precisely one hour after the last, with 3-, 2- and 1-minute warnings; failing to finish within the hour, or not being in the corral at the bell to start, eliminates you. Except for the winner, all runners are recorded as DNF. Big's official rules also bar personal aid during a loop, exclude non-competitors (including eliminated runners) from the course, and do not allow pacing.

Read the regulations to tell them apart: continuing after a closure time puts risk on the runner personally; continuing past a cut-off point violates the rules. Medical arrangements differ for the two, and both must be confirmed in the official handbook before race day.

## Cut-off structure across four race types

| Race type | Time structure | Basis to confirm | Buffer focus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Road marathon | Usually a finish overall limit; some set 5 km segment cut-offs | Natural clock or gun time per race; later waves have less usable time | First 5 km crowd surge; wall after 35 km |
| Road ultra | Often checkpoint cut-offs with traffic-light judging | May run from the last group; miss a point and retire | Long rhythm, fuel and electrolytes, afternoon cooling |
| Trail ultra | Aid-station departure table, including night stations | Often departure time; default cut-off equals next station | Climb sections, technical descents, night fatigue, station stays |
| Loop elimination | Fixed duration per loop, bell restart | No fixed total distance; a missed loop eliminates | Buffer eroded loop by loop; inter-loop fuel and rest |

Real route pages show how these structures run in practice: the [Shanghai Marathon](/en/routes/shanghai/) uses a natural-clock segment cut-off table; [Comrades Marathon](/en/routes/comrades-marathon/) pairs a 12-hour overall limit with intermediate checkpoints as a road ultra; [Western States 100](/en/routes/western-states/) judges by departure time at each trail station; [Big's Backyard Ultra](/en/routes/bigs-backyard-ultra/) restarts the clock each hour in a loop elimination format.

## Pre-race plan: turning the table into a pace plan

1. Download that edition's regulations, course map and cut-off table; confirm the timing basis (natural clock / gun time / last-wave) and judgment basis (arrival / departure).
2. Using official segment distances, convert each cut-off into segment cut-off pace; mark the two or three points with the smallest buffer.
3. Project arrival at each point on your target finish and compute buffer per point; reserve climb, queue, night and weather losses inside the margin.
4. Write down two paces: target pace and finishing pace, plus the switch trigger.
5. Record key cut-off points, sweep route, medical points and meeting places on the back of the bib or in watch notes.
6. For long events, plan fueling together with the cut-off budget: carbohydrate, fluid and electrolyte rhythm is covered in [race-day fueling](/en/knowledge/race-day-fueling/) and [hydration](/en/knowledge/hydration/); heat-day downgrade rules are in [heat and humidity](/en/knowledge/heat-and-humidity/).

## On-course review: what to check at each point

At each checkpoint, review in a fixed order instead of looking only at total time.

- **Time**: what is the current point's cut-off, what time is it now, how many minutes of buffer remain.
- **Pace**: actual segment pace against the segment cut-off pace; inside the reserve or eating into it.
- **Consumed time**: how much this segment cost in queue, stay and climb slowdown; how much climb and technical surface remain next.
- **Body state**: feel, stomach, fluids and food; when state slides, judge buffer one conservative notch lower.
- **Next node**: whether the next point is an aid station, medical point or pickup; if withdrawal becomes necessary, where the nearest reachable point is.

The review decides pace and withdrawal timing; it does not override the rules. On-course timing officials and medical staff take priority over these projections.

## After a cut-off: procedure, record and what not to do

The standard procedure after missing a cut-off follows the race rules:

1. Stop at the nearest cut-off point or pickup location, report to staff or a timing official, and confirm how the withdrawal or DNF is recorded.
2. Use official withdrawal transport; do not continue along the course or cross barriers back onto it.
3. Notify crew or family of the withdrawal location and arrangements; liaison division for trail ultras is covered in [crew and pacer planning](/en/knowledge/crew-and-pacer-planning/).
4. After the race, check the result record and DNF notation; raise any objection through the regulations' appeal process, not by arguing on course.

Do not: assume "another stretch will catch the cut-off"; leave on an unofficial vehicle and rejoin; conceal a cut-off record and keep following; or push through to the next station when unwell — medical care outranks finishing.

## Three failure modes and early signals

| Failure mode | Early signal | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surge early with the crowd, spending buffer first 5 km | First 5 km pace clearly faster than target; first station buffer already below plan | Buffer turns negative on a mid-race climb or heat block, and you are swept |
| Watch only the finish clock, skipping segment review | No per-point check of segment cut-off pace; station stays grow longer | You discover the minimum-buffer point has already slipped in mid-race |
| Treat cut-off pace as a pacing guide | Planning rhythm around "how slow I can go" with no reserve for weather and surprises | A queue, slowdown or night fatigue pushes you over the time |

Matching actions: hold early pace; review per point by the formula; reserve buffer conservatively. The overall long-distance rhythm framework is in [ultra-running basics](/en/knowledge/ultra-running-basics/); the early braking principle on race day is in [race-day pacing](/en/knowledge/race-day-pacing/).

## Scope and items the official source does not publish

This page makes no cut-off promise for any specific edition. Times listed on route pages come from that edition's regulations or timing table, and rules may change year to year. The following are marked unknown on this site: next-edition checkpoint locations and times for each race; arrival-versus-departure basis where the rules do not state it; exact usable time for each wave under a natural-clock basis; per-bus sweep times. Until that edition's guide is obtained, do not treat last year's table as this year's rules. This page does not diagnose, prescribe training plans or recommend medication.

## Handoffs and cut-off time

Cut-offs depend not only on your pace but on whether crew and pacer handoffs eat time. For handoff points, gear changes and night pacing, see [ultra crew and pacer handoff](/en/knowledge/ultra-crew-pacer-handoff/); put handoff time into the split sheet so the station does not close on you.
