## Definition and scope

**Jet lag** is the set of symptoms produced when the body clock is temporarily out of step with local time after a flight across time zones. The NHS defines it as disrupted normal sleep patterns after long-haul flights, usually improving **within a few days** once the body adjusts to the new time zone. It is not a matter of willpower, and it is not fixed by "catching up on sleep"; the body clock can only move so fast.

This page is for: runners racing or training across more than three time zones; anyone with a fixed race date who has to plan travel backwards from it; anyone asking "how many days early should I arrive?"

It is **not** for: shift workers (who need a different schedule plan), individualised planning for infants or travel during pregnancy, or anyone using it as a diagnosis of a sleep disorder. If you take prescription medication or have a sleep disorder or mental health condition, talk to a doctor before you travel.

## Symptoms and duration

The CDC Yellow Book chapter on Jet Lag Disorder lists jet lag as a group of symptoms: difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, cognitive impairment, daytime sleepiness, general malaise and gastrointestinal disturbance. The NHS adds specific features: difficulty falling asleep and difficulty getting up in the morning, tiredness and exhaustion, difficulty staying awake during the day, poor sleep quality, problems with attention and memory; sometimes indigestion, nausea, constipation, appetite changes and mild anxiety.

There is no single duration figure, because adaptation speed depends on how many time zones you cross, the direction of travel, and light exposure and routine after arrival. The NHS wording is "usually improves within a few days"; the CDC Yellow Book gives average adaptation rates of **1.5 hours per day westward and 1 hour per day eastward**. At those rates, crossing 6 time zones eastward takes about 6 days and westward about 4 days; crossing 9 time zones eastward takes about 9 days and westward about 6 days (calculated from the average rate, not guaranteed).

One survey found that **68% of international business travellers regularly experience negative symptoms** (CDC Yellow Book). A runner usually has one race, so "how many days remain after arrival" matters more than "how many days it takes in total".

## East versus west: why eastward is harder

The average intrinsic period of the body clock is slightly longer than 24 hours, so people naturally drift later more easily than earlier. The CDC Yellow Book concludes that **westward travel is easier to adapt to than eastward travel**, at average rates of 1.5 hours per day westward and 1 hour per day eastward.

A review collected by the CDC (Eastman & Burgess) gives finer estimates: in real travellers, phase shifts average **92 minutes per day westward and 57 minutes per day eastward**; in laboratory studies that shifted schedules by 12 hours, the first 4 days produced cumulative delays of 9.6 hours (2.4 hours per day) and cumulative advances of 6.2 hours (1.6 hours per day). The review also notes that, with timed bright light after arrival, travel plans commonly assume **1.5 hours per day of advance and 2 hours per day of delay**.

The two sets of numbers do not match (1/1.5 versus 1.5/2), which shows that these are estimated ranges, not laws. This site's approach is: **plan with the CDC Yellow Book figures of 1 hour (east) and 1.5 hours (west), and treat the Eastman figures as an optimistic upper bound**; then judge actual adaptation from your bedtime and morning state on two consecutive days after arrival.

## Direction and timing of light

Light is the strongest time-giving signal for the body clock. The CDC Yellow Book principle is:

- **Morning light advances**: bright light after the circadian "nadir", roughly **2–4 hours before habitual wake time**, pushes the phase **earlier** (better for eastward travel).
- **Evening light delays**: bright light in the evening generally pushes the phase **later** (better for westward travel).

In practical terms:

| Direction | Goal | Seek light | Avoid light |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eastward (advance) | Earlier bedtime, earlier waking | Local **early morning** to late morning after arrival | Local evening to bedtime |
| Westward (delay) | Later bedtime, later waking | Local **evening** into the night (bright indoor light counts) | Bright light too early in the local morning |

The NHS advice is simpler: after arrival, **go outdoors during the day**; natural light helps the body clock adjust, do not sleep during the day, and sleep only at night. The two are not in conflict: the NHS gives the general direction, the Yellow Book gives the direction- and time-specific detail.

## Shifting sleep in the 2–3 days before departure

The CDC Yellow Book states explicitly that in the **2–3 days** before departure you can shift sleep toward the destination time zone by **1 hour per day** — 1 hour later each day for westward travel, 1 hour earlier each day for eastward travel — which can shorten adaptation after arrival. The NHS agrees: before departure you can start going to bed and getting up earlier or later than usual, moving closer to the destination time zone.

Supporting flight-day arrangements (NHS + Yellow Book):

- Rest before departure; do not board with a sleep debt.
- Drink water, stretch and move regularly in flight; if it is night at the destination, sleep according to destination night; an eye mask and earplugs can help.
- Keep alcohol and coffee low: alcohol shortens sleep latency but increases sleep fragmentation, and both caffeine and alcohol can dehydrate; both worsen jet lag (Yellow Book).
- For short trips (2–3 days per the NHS, fewer than 2 days per the Yellow Book) you can skip the adjustment, avoiding a second adjustment on the return.

## What to do after arrival

CDC Yellow Book advice after arrival:

- Stay awake during the local day to build up "sleep pressure" and help you fall asleep that night.
- If you need a boost, take a **20–30 minute** short nap; longer daytime sleep interferes with nighttime sleep.
- Schedule meals, sleep and light exposure by destination time, not origin time.

NHS advice after arrival:

- Switch sleep to the new time zone as soon as possible, and set an alarm in the morning so you do not oversleep.
- Go outdoors during the day for natural light.
- Do not sleep during the day; sleep only at night.

Training advice after arrival (this site's practical suggestion, not a research finding): on the first day after arrival do only low-intensity activity and an easy run, no quality session; on the second day decide from your sleep state whether to resume tempo work; do not add a new intensity stimulus in the 48 hours before the race. If sleep has been less than half your usual duration for two consecutive nights, prioritise sleep rather than making up missed sessions.

## How many days before the race should you arrive?

Working backwards from the CDC Yellow Book average adaptation rates:

| Time zones crossed | Days needed eastward (1 hour/day) | Days needed westward (1.5 hours/day) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 | 3 | 2 |
| 5 | 5 | 3–4 |
| 6 | 6 | 4 |
| 8 | 8 | 5–6 |
| 9 | 9 | 6 |

The days in the table are "average adaptation time", not "mandatory lead time". In practice:

- **Eastward**: subtract the number of time zones from race day and allow at least that many days; for example, crossing 6 time zones eastward, arriving 6 days early is reasonably safe.
- **Westward**: same method, but fewer days are needed; crossing 6 time zones westward needs about 4 days.
- **Buffer**: add 1 day on top of those figures for registration, course viewing, and adapting to climate and food (this site's practical suggestion).
- **When you cannot arrive early enough**: do not pretend you have adapted. Plan the race as one run with a body clock that has not fully switched — no high intensity before the race, and start by feel rather than by pace target on race day; if the trip is shorter than 2 days, both the Yellow Book and the NHS suggest you may simply not switch schedules at all.

All figures in the table are **calculated** estimates from the Yellow Book average rates, not predictions for an individual; individual variation, how well you execute light exposure, and flight times all change the result.

## Melatonin: the boundary only

The CDC Yellow Book lists **timed melatonin** as one of the standard treatments for frequent travellers in the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) guidelines, and gives the directional principle: taken when the body clock thinks it is "morning" it produces a phase delay (helpful for westward travel); taken when the body clock thinks it is "early evening" it produces a phase advance (helpful for eastward travel).

The NHS position is different: **melatonin is not recommended for jet lag within the UK National Health Service**, because the evidence is not strong enough to show it works.

The two positions disagree. This site does not pick a side and gives no dose or timing. What can be stated as a boundary is: melatonin falls into the category of "this practice exists and there is directional research"; whether to use it, how much, and when depends on individual circumstances and should be discussed with a doctor. Do not combine sleeping tablets and melatonin on your own; the NHS notes that sleeping tablets can be addictive and should be used only short-term, when symptoms are severe.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Just sleep when you arrive, local time"**: a long daytime sleep delays bedtime that night and lengthens jet lag; if you sleep, keep it to 20–30 minutes (Yellow Book).
- **"East and west are the same"**: eastward needs a phase advance, which is harder and slower on average (1 hour/day versus 1.5 hours/day).
- **"More alcohol helps you sleep"**: alcohol increases sleep fragmentation and dehydrates; the Yellow Book explicitly advises keeping it low in flight.
- **"Arriving 2 days early is enough"**: crossing 6 time zones eastward needs about 6 days at the average rate; 2 days gives only partial adaptation.
- **"Melatonin is a universal fix"**: the NHS explicitly does not recommend it for jet lag; the Yellow Book describes directional research protocols, not a solution that suits everyone.
- **"The more daytime sleep the better"**: long daytime sleep weakens the drive for nighttime sleep.

## When to seek medical care

Jet lag itself usually needs no treatment; the NHS says symptoms generally improve within a few days. Seek medical care in the following situations (this site's practical boundary, not diagnostic criteria):

- Sleep problems persist for several weeks without improving, or clearly affect work, training and mood;
- Persistent or worsening anxiety or depression;
- You need sleeping tablets to get to sleep, or you are already using them long-term;
- You have a known sleep disorder such as sleep apnoea or restless legs, and it clearly worsens after crossing time zones.

Do not use alcohol or self-increased sleeping tablets to manage jet lag.

## Scope and boundaries

This page is travel and training-schedule education. It provides no diagnosis, prescription or dose advice. The Yellow Book and NHS advice is written for general travellers; race planning also has to combine the training cycle, climate adaptation and taper, and cannot look at jet lag alone. Every "days" figure is an estimate calculated from average rates, not a medical prediction for an individual.
