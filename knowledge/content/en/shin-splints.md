## Definition and who this page applies to

"Shin splints" is runner shorthand, usually meaning **medial tibial stress syndrome** (MTSS). AAOS defines it as inflammation of the muscles, tendons, and bone tissue around the tibia, with pain along the **inner border of the tibia**, where muscles attach to bone. NHS describes it more simply: pain and tenderness along the front of the lower leg after exercise such as running.

This page is for people who develop diffuse pain along the inner shin edge after recently increasing mileage, frequency, or intensity, who can still walk but hurt when running. It is **not** for a single sharply localized point of severe pain, night pain, obvious swelling, inability to bear weight, or exercise-induced calf tightness that becomes numb — those need stress fracture and chronic exertional compartment syndrome ruled out.

## Recognition: a diffuse bone-edge pain, or one small point?

| What you observe | More consistent with MTSS | More suggestive of stress fracture or other problems |
|---|---|---|
| Area | A band along the inner tibial edge, diffuse | One very small point you can pinpoint with a finger |
| Timing | Appears with exercise, eases with rest | Pain at rest, pain at night |
| Weight-bearing | Can walk, hurts when running | Walking hurts, single-leg hop hurts |
| Swelling | Mild or absent | Obvious swelling, local heat |
| Course | Improves over weeks with reduced load | Keeps worsening despite reduced load |

AAOS explicitly warns that **stress fracture, tendinitis, and chronic exertional compartment syndrome** can all present as shin pain and may need examination and imaging to rule out. Chronic exertional compartment syndrome typically comes on with exercise and settles quickly when you stop; diagnosis requires measuring compartment pressures. **Do not use "run and see" to tell these apart.**

## When to seek care

- Pain is concentrated in one small point, or present at rest or at night.
- Obvious swelling, local heat, inability to bear weight, or a clearly altered gait.
- Pain keeps worsening after 2–3 weeks of reduced load.
- Calf tightness, numbness, or weakness during exercise that resolves only after stopping (possible chronic exertional compartment syndrome).
- Recent trauma.

NHS advises seeing a GP if self-care fails, pain worsens, or it does not improve; contact NHS 111 if pain is severe or the shin is injured. AAOS advises a doctor visit if rest and other measures do not help, to confirm whether something else is causing the pain.

## Contributing factors: load change comes first

AAOS attributes the condition to muscle and bone tissue being overworked by repetitive activity, and lists three kinds of sudden change:

1. **Frequency** — more exercise days per week.
2. **Duration** — longer single sessions.
3. **Intensity** — for example moving from flat to hilly running.

Other factors include flat feet or abnormally rigid arches, and improper or worn-out footwear. NHS adds starting exercise after inactivity, running or jumping on hard surfaces, and poor running technique. Runners are at highest risk; dancers and military recruits are also commonly diagnosed.

The practical implication: the target is **slowing the rate of change**, not only changing shoes or stretching.

## Three steps: unload, manage locally, rebuild

### Step 1: Stop the causative load while keeping aerobic work

NHS is explicit: do not continue the exercise that caused the pain, and do not rush back to the previous level. AAOS advises several weeks of rest from the aggravating activity, substituting swimming, stationary cycling, or an elliptical.

In practice:

- Stop running for 7–14 days, or keep only a very short distance that does not worsen pain.
- Use swimming, cycling, or the elliptical for aerobic work.
- Avoid hard surfaces and hills.
- Use "does a single-leg hop hurt?" and "does walking hurt?" as recovery gates.

### Step 2: Ice, compression, and support

- Ice: both NHS and AAOS give **20 minutes, several times a day**, with a cloth barrier.
- Compression: AAOS mentions an elastic compression bandage to limit further swelling; do not wrap until numb.
- Shoes: NHS asks for trainers that support the feet; AAOS advises well-cushioned daily shoes and, for flat feet or recurrent problems, shoe inserts or orthotics.
- Painkillers: this page gives no drug or dose advice; ask a pharmacist or clinician if needed.

### Step 3: Rebuild only after a pain-free period

AAOS sets a specific gate: be **pain-free for at least 2 weeks**, then return at lower frequency, shorter duration, and lower intensity; change one variable at a time. If the same pain returns, stop immediately, ice, rest for 1–2 days, then restart at a lower level. NHS gives the same message: build the routine back up slowly rather than returning to the starting point in one step.

## A two-week load and return framework

| Stage | Running | Substitute exercise | Gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Days 1–3 | No running | Swimming/cycling, pain-free | Walking and single-leg hop pain-free |
| Days 4–7 | No running, or a very slow 10–15 minutes | Keep 2–3 cross-training sessions | No worsening 24 hours later |
| Days 8–14 | Short runs every other day on flat ground | Keep 1–2 cross-training sessions | Two consecutive pain-free weeks |
| Return phase | Add frequency, then duration, then intensity | Keep 1 cross-training session | No worsening 24 hours after any increase |

## A worked example you can adapt

Example runner: a beginner who went from three runs (20 km per week) to five runs (35 km per week) in two weeks and added two hill sessions; pain now starts at 3 km along the inner shin and walking hurts afterwards. The load arithmetic: weekly volume rose from 20 km to 35 km, an increase of 15 km, or (35−20)÷20 = 75%, while frequency, session duration and hill intensity all changed at once.

Adjustment example (not a prescription):

1. Stop running for 10 days; swim or cycle 30–40 minutes every other day.
2. Ice for 20 minutes once or twice daily.
3. Replace worn running shoes; wear cushioned shoes for daily walking.
4. From day 11, run 3 km on flat ground every other day; add 1 km only if the 24-hour response is clean.
5. Do not return to three runs per week until at least two pain-free weeks; add hills last.

This example shows the order — frequency first, then duration, then intensity — and does not promise recovery in two weeks. The 75% is arithmetic for this example only, not a universal injury threshold.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Shin splints mean I just need to stretch my calves more."** Stretching may help some people feel better, but AAOS centres care on load reduction and gradual rebuild. Stretching without slowing down leads to repeat episodes.
- **"It will run itself off."** NHS explicitly says not to continue the exercise that caused the pain. "Running it off" may only mask it.
- **"A new carbon-plate shoe lets me keep increasing mileage."** Shoes are one factor; load change is the main driver. New shoes do not change "too much, too soon".
- **"Barefoot running cures shin splints."** AAOS states there is **no clear evidence** that barefoot running reduces the risk of any injury; if tried, it must be introduced very gradually.
- **"Once the pain goes away, go straight back to the old mileage."** AAOS gates return on at least two consecutive pain-free weeks, then lower frequency, shorter duration and lower intensity; feeling better does not mean tissue recovery is complete.

## Frequency, progression, and review

- Ice: several times daily during the painful phase, 20 minutes each.
- Cross-training: 2–3 times weekly until pain-free running resumes.
- Return: frequency first, then duration, then intensity; one variable at a time.
- Review: if there is no improvement after 2–3 weeks, or pain becomes a single point or occurs at night, seek care earlier.
- Prevention: gradual mileage increases, alternating hard and soft surfaces, timely shoe replacement, calf and foot strength, and a form check.

## Self-check

- [ ] Is the pain a band along the inner shin, rather than one small point?
- [ ] Are walking and a single-leg hop essentially pain-free?
- [ ] Have you increased frequency, distance, or hills in the last two weeks?
- [ ] Are your running shoes worn or less cushioned than they were?
- [ ] Have you replaced today's causative running with cross-training?
- [ ] Have you completed at least two pain-free weeks before returning?

## Scope and limits

This is health education, not a substitute for examination or imaging, and it provides no diagnosis, medication or rehabilitation prescription. Causes of shin pain can overlap, and stress fracture and chronic exertional compartment syndrome need professional assessment. AAOS and NHS differ on rest duration ("several weeks" versus "7–14 days"); this page uses the more conservative gate of at least two pain-free weeks before return. Children, adolescents, pregnant runners, post-operative runners, and people taking medication that affects bone metabolism should be assessed clinically first.

## Stress-injury exclusion table

Medial shin pain is not always “shin splints”. Early recognition of a stress injury decides whether you miss weeks or months. This is not a diagnosis; it flags when soft-tissue management is no longer enough.

| Signal | Soft tissue (shin splints) | Exclude stress injury |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain area | Diffuse along the inner bone | Focal, fingertip-localised |
| Night pain | Rare | Common, may wake you |
| Single-leg hop | Possible discomfort, can complete | Severe pain on landing |
| Swelling | Mild, after activity | Local hardness, persistent swelling |
| Walking | Possible, worse after running | Limp, painful in daily life |
| Tender point | Several, superficial | One point, deep severe pain |

Any “exclude” signal on the right: stop running and jumping and seek imaging assessment; do not “run two more days to see”.

## Four-stage return table

| Stage | Content | Pass criterion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Walk 20–30 min | No pain next day |
| 2 | Walk 5 min / jog 1 min × 5 | Pain ≤2/10 at 24 h |
| 3 | Continuous easy jog 10–15 min | Normal gait, no next-day flare |
| 4 | Easy 20–30 min every other day | Stable for a week before adding volume |

Repeat each stage 2–3 times; if pain rises during the run or lasts beyond 24 hours, go back one stage. Keep calf raises and ankle stability, no box jumps or sprints.

## Shoes and surface

Shoes are not a cure, but they change load. Sudden switches from thick to thin, road to trail, or a new shoe for a long run can overload the shin within days. Return in a broken-in shoe; do not change shoe, volume and climbing in the same week.
