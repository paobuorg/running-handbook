## Conclusion first

The core message of running hydration is not "drink a fixed number of litres per hour". It is: estimate your own sweat loss and weight change first, then replace fluid in divided doses according to conditions, duration and how you feel; do not deliberately restrict fluids for performance, and do not force fluids until urine runs perfectly clear or body weight rises. The ACSM 2007 position stand on exercise and fluid replacement notes considerable variability in sweating rates and sweat electrolyte content between individuals, and recommends individualized fluid replacement programs aimed at preventing dehydration beyond 2% of body weight and excessive changes in electrolyte balance. The 3rd International Consensus on EAH (2015) identifies overconsumption of hypotonic fluids relative to exchangeable sodium, likely combined with non-osmotic AVP secretion, as the primary etiology and pathophysiological mechanism behind EAH and all known fatalities (Grade 1A).

Three actions come first. First, estimate your sweat rate from pre- and post-exercise weighing rather than copying a per-hour fluid figure from a label or another runner. Second, let thirst guide intake, checked against weight change and urine colour; do not chase perfectly clear urine. Third, headache, nausea or vomiting, confusion, seizure, marked face or hand swelling, or collapse mean stop exercise and large-volume fluid intake, and seek emergency care.

This page is exercise nutrition and safety education, not diagnosis. People with cardiovascular or kidney disease, or those taking diuretics, blood pressure medication and other drugs, should have fluid plans assessed by a clinician first.

## Definition and premises

- **Dehydration:** fluid loss reflected in pre/post exercise weight change; treat 1 kg of weight difference as about 1 L of water. Weight loss above 2% is the reference boundary for excessive dehydration (ACSM 2007).
- **Sweat rate:** fluid lost per unit time, in L/h, derived from pre/post weight, fluid intake and urine volume; see the [sweat-rate estimation tool](/en/tools/sweat-rate/) for the calculation convention.
- **Sweat electrolytes:** sweat loses sodium and chloride, plus a small amount of potassium; sweat sodium varies widely between individuals. To convert mmol sodium to milligrams, multiply by 23 (1 mmol sodium ≈ 23 mg sodium). Detail is in [Electrolytes and sodium](/en/knowledge/electrolytes-and-sodium/).
- **Exercise-associated hyponatremia (EAH):** serum sodium below about 135 mmol/L during or after exercise, mostly dilutional from relative body-water excess; severe EAH is an emergency.
- **Thirst:** the subjective signal that fluids are needed; it is one adequate guide, but in long, intense or hot sessions it must be checked against weighing rather than used alone.

This page covers daily training, long runs, road marathons and ultras. Short, low-intensity activity usually needs only water plus daily food. Combined heat and humidity risk is in [Heat and humidity](/en/knowledge/heat-and-humidity/); carbohydrate and electrolyte rhythm in long sessions is in [Long-run fueling](/en/knowledge/long-run-fueling/).

## How runners lose water and electrolytes

Running produces muscle heat; the body sheds heat mainly by evaporating sweat. Sweat is not a simple filtrate of plasma: water and electrolytes leave through sweat glands, and sweat-gland sodium reabsorption, heat acclimation, energy expenditure, exercise mode and environment all affect sweat sodium concentration. Baker 2022's retrospective analysis of 1,944 sweat tests from 1,304 subjects gives a reference population distribution:

| Measure | Mean ± SD | Median | Range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Whole-body sweating rate (L/h) | 1.09 ± 0.50 | 1.03 | 0.19–5.64 |
| Whole-body sweat sodium (mmol/L) | 42.0 ± 12.6 | 40.3 | 17–106 |

Two points must be read out. First, sweating rate spans 0.19 to 5.64 L/h — nearly thirtyfold — and sweat sodium spans 17 to 106 mmol/L — about a sixfold difference; one label dose or one per-hour litre figure cannot fit everyone. Second, the regression models explained only 17%–23% of sweat-sodium variation; age group, race/ethnicity, fluid balance, sodium intake and relative VO2max showed no association, so a population average cannot replace individual testing. Higher energy expenditure went with higher sweat sodium, and warmer months (a proxy for heat acclimation) with lower sweat sodium.

The ACSM 2007 target is to prevent excessive dehydration, defined as more than 2% body weight loss from water deficit, and excessive electrolyte balance changes; beverages containing carbohydrate and electrolytes can benefit over water alone under certain circumstances, depending on the exercise task and weather. The World Athletics 2020 medical guidelines treat fluid replacement as part of the event medical plan: athletes should replace fluids in long or hot events to prevent excessive dehydration, but the plan must match individual sweat rate, sweat sodium and conditions.

## Sweat rate and sodium loss: a reproducible worked example

Without sweat patches or whole-body washdown testing, start by computing sweat rate from pre/post weighing, then use the population range as a conservative sodium-loss estimate. A personal sweat sodium concentration needs patch testing or laboratory sweat analysis.

**Step convention** (same as the [sweat-rate estimation tool](/en/tools/sweat-rate/)):

1. Empty the bladder before the run; weigh in dry, close-fitting clothing (or nude), record the time.
2. Record the weight difference of drink bottles before and after; estimate any urine passed.
3. Dry off after the run, weigh in the same clothing, record duration.
4. Sweat loss (L) = pre weight − post weight + fluid intake (L) − urine (L); sweat rate = sweat loss ÷ hours.
5. Sodium loss (mg/h) = sweat rate (L/h) × sweat sodium (mmol/L) × 23.

**Worked example (teaching assumption):** pre-run 60.0 kg, post-run 58.8 kg, 0.6 L drunk on route, no urine, 90 minutes (1.5 h) running.

- Weight deficit = 60.0 − 58.8 = 1.2 kg, which is 1.2 / 60.0 = 2.0% of pre-run body weight — right at the ACSM reference boundary for excessive dehydration.
- Sweat loss = 1.2 L + 0.6 L = 1.8 L; sweat rate = 1.8 / 1.5 = 1.20 L/h, above the population mean of 1.09 L/h.
- At 17 mmol/L sweat sodium (low end): sodium loss = 1.20 × 17 × 23 ≈ 469 mg/h, about 0.70 g over 90 min.
- At 42 mmol/L (population mean): sodium loss = 1.20 × 42 × 23 ≈ 1,159 mg/h, about 1.74 g over 90 min.
- At 106 mmol/L (high end): sodium loss = 1.20 × 106 × 23 ≈ 2.92 g/h, about 4.38 g over 90 min.

At the same sweat rate, high versus low sweat sodium changes hourly sodium loss by about sixfold. This example shows that a label's "milligrams of sodium per hour" is an average figure: it may be insufficient for a high-sodium sweater and unnecessary for a low one. Fluid replacement need not match every millilitre; mild weight loss in long runs is common and acceptable. The example is not a personal salt prescription; do not take large-dose salt tablets from these numbers before a measured sweat sodium test.

## Decision table: choosing a fluid rhythm by condition

| Condition (if) | Action (then) | Evidence boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single session under 60–90 min, modest sweating, controlled pace | Water plus daily food suffices; eat normally after the session to replace | ACSM 2007: short, low-intensity activity usually needs no dedicated electrolyte drink |
| Session over 90 min, or warm conditions, heavy sweating | Drink in small divided doses; record pre/post weighing and check whether weight loss approaches or exceeds 2% | ACSM: target prevention of excessive dehydration, individualized program; weighing is the starting point for sweat estimation |
| Weight unchanged or up, puffy hands/face, pale urine with nausea during a long session or race | Stop further large fluid intake; return to thirst-guided intake, check by weighing; be alert for EAH | EAH consensus: primary etiology is overconsumption of hypotonic fluid relative to exchangeable sodium; symptom severity, not a single sodium value, guides treatment |
| White salt streaks on clothes, salty-tasting sweat, recurring early fatigue in long sessions | Trial an electrolyte drink or small salty supplement in training first, and validate tolerance | Salt streaks are only a weak clue toward high sweat sodium, not a lab test; see [Electrolytes and sodium](/en/knowledge/electrolytes-and-sodium/) |
| Hot and humid, long gaps between aid stations | Carry water or choose a loop course; prepare fluid by sweat rate, and consider electrolytes and carbohydrate in long events | ISSN ultra stand: about 450–750 mL/h, roughly 150–250 mL every 20 min; electrolyte needs rise in heat and humidity |
| Headache, vomiting, confusion, seizure, collapse, marked swelling | Stop exercise, do not keep drinking large volumes, seek immediate medical care | EAH consensus and NWS heat guidance: these are emergency red flags; "walk it off" is not an option |

## Scenario tiers: short runs, long runs, ultras and heat

| Scenario | Fluid position | Electrolyte position | Check method |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daily 30–60 min easy run | Normal fluids before; drink after by thirst; water suffices | Usually unnecessary | Urine clear pale yellow; weight back to baseline next day |
| 60–90 min tempo run or early long run | Carry a small amount, drink by thirst, avoid one large chug | Usually unnecessary; electrolyte drink optional with heavy sweating | Pre/post weighing; keep weight loss within about 2% |
| 2–3 h long run | Divided doses by sweat rate; treat the ISSN ultra range 450–750 mL/h as an upper interval and scale to personal tolerance | Consider electrolyte drinks in long or heavy-sweating sessions; reassess small salt addition for high sweat sodium | Weighing + how you feel + urine colour; no new drink on race day |
| Ultra, hot and humid race | Small frequent intake by aid-station rhythm; use drinks and volumes validated in training | Electrolyte needs rise; the dose depends on personal sweat sodium and tolerance; see [Long-run fueling](/en/knowledge/long-run-fueling/) | Check weight sensation, gastric emptying, dizziness and nausea at each station; dropping out stays an option |

## Two schools: planned fluid intake versus thirst-guided drinking (side by side, no averaging)

Two positions coexist in the exercise hydration literature. They are presented side by side here, not folded into one "standard answer":

- **Planned/weighing-guided school:** The ACSM 2007 position stand recommends an individualized fluid program based on sweat rate, with planned divided intake of fluid and electrolytes in long, hot or heavy-sweating scenarios, targeting dehydration within about 2% of body weight. The World Athletics 2020 medical guidelines and the ISSN ultra stand likewise give per-hour fluid reference ranges (450–750 mL/h) for event medical and aid arrangements. The rationale: waiting for strong thirst can lag behind fast sweat loss.
- **Thirst-guided school:** The 3rd International EAH Consensus (2015) considers thirst an adequate drinking guide, and identifies overconsumption of hypotonic fluid relative to exchangeable sodium as the primary EAH cause. Reported EAH risk factors include inexperience, slow pace, extreme BMI and readily available fluids. This school worries that fixed timetables push athletes into overdrinking and dilutional hyponatremia.

The two are not wholly opposed: the purpose of a plan is to prevent excessive dehydration, but the plan must defer to personal sweat rate, weight change and thirst. If weighing shows weight unchanged or up, or bloating and nausea appear, pause fluids even if the schedule says "time to drink". Both schools reject the two extremes: forcing fluids until weight rises, and deliberately restricting fluids to hold weight down.

## When plain water, and when an electrolyte drink

- **Plain water suffices:** under 60–90 min, modest sweating, cool conditions. The NHS daily position is that clear pale yellow urine is enough; perfectly clear is not required.
- **Consider an electrolyte drink:** events beyond 2.5–3 h, heavy sweating in heat and humidity, confirmed high sweat sodium (clothing salt streaks, prior cramps or hyponatremia). Sports drinks are typically about 10–38 mmol/L sodium, far below serum at about 140 mmol/L; they cannot prevent EAH when fluid intake stays excessive.
- **Carbohydrate drinks are a separate question:** high-intensity exercise beyond about 1 h also needs carbohydrate; dose and rhythm are in [Long-run fueling](/en/knowledge/long-run-fueling/) and [Race-day fueling](/en/knowledge/race-day-fueling/). Do not treat a sports drink as both fluid and the full carbohydrate plan.
- **Gut tolerance first:** any new drink, salt tablet or gel must be tried in training; race day is not the first exposure. GI distress links to high-osmolarity solutions and high carbohydrate intake intensity; see [Gut training for runners](/en/knowledge/gut-training-for-races/).

## Urine, thirst and weight: how to read your own state

- **Urine colour:** the NHS daily guide is clear pale yellow. Persistently dark yellow suggests fluid deficit; long-term clarity with nausea and puffy hands calls for caution about excess. Urine colour is affected by B vitamins, some foods and medication, and cannot be used alone as a diagnosis.
- **Thirst:** the first subjective signal, but early dehydration thirst can lag sweat rate; in hot long sessions check against weighing.
- **Weight difference:** same-condition pre/post weighing is the most direct measure. A loss approaching or above 2% signals insufficient replacement; weight unchanged or up after the run calls for a fluid-intake review.
- **Pace and speech:** the CDC talk test helps judge intensity. An easy run should allow full sentences; if the old pace leaves you able to say only a few words before a breath, intensity is high, and in heat that means larger fluid and cooling pressure as well.

## Failure modes and early signals

- **Forced scheduled drinking:** early signals are weight unchanged or up, puffy hands, pale urine with persistent nausea. Rule: return to thirst-guided intake and check by weighing; do not let "drink by the clock" override how you feel.
- **Plain water only in a long, heavy sweat session:** early signals are accelerating fatigue, muscle weakness, lingering tiredness after the session. Rule: validate an electrolyte drink in training for long sessions; do not debut it on race day.
- **Restricting fluids for performance or pace:** early signals are heavy legs, dark urine, dizziness, unexplained pace drop. Rule: keep dehydration within about 2%; in heat, performance defers to finishing safely.
- **First new drink or salt tablet on race day:** early signals are bloating, reflux, stomach cramp. Rule: all fuel choices come from a training-validated list; gut training is a gradual process over two or more weeks.
- **Treating a cramp automatically as salt deficiency:** local muscle spasm may relate to fatigue, neuromuscular control and local load; current evidence does not equate cramps simply with dehydration or sodium deficit. Recurring cramps call for a review of load, sleep and energy intake; see [Electrolytes and sodium](/en/knowledge/electrolytes-and-sodium/).

## Red flags: stop exercise and seek care

With any of the following, stop exercise, do not keep drinking large volumes of fluid or sports drink, and seek immediate medical care: confusion, slurred speech, seizure, fainting, persistent vomiting, marked face or hand swelling, breathing difficulty, chest pain, collapse. With suspected EAH, do not self-administer dilute salt water or hypertonic fluids. The NWS heat-illness sequence is to call first and cool while waiting; do not leave someone alone in the heat, and do not substitute "walk it off" for assessment. Those with prior heat illness, hyponatremia history, pregnancy or medication use should be assessed clinically before racing.

## Applicable boundaries

This page is general exercise nutrition and safety education, not a diagnosis, treatment plan or medication advice. Sweat rate and sweat sodium figures come from specific population datasets and cannot be converted into a precise personal loss; observational studies, consensus stands and official guidelines are flagged separately per claim. Kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, pregnancy, lactation, or diuretic/antihypertensive medication call for clinician-assessed fluid and sodium plans. This site does not measure serum sodium or replace emergency judgment; personal salt, supplement and medication decisions follow clinical assessment.

## What would change this advice

- **Duration and intensity:** under 60–90 minutes water usually suffices; ultra-endurance beyond 2.5–3 h raises carbohydrate and electrolyte needs, and the fluid rhythm must be rearranged accordingly.
- **Environment:** heat and humidity accelerate sweat and sodium loss and reduce cooling efficiency; seasonal change, first hot-weather race, or shifts in wind and sun all call for re-testing by weighing.
- **Individual state:** age, sex, exercise mode, energy expenditure, prior EAH or cramp history, and dietary sodium intake all change the plan; sweat rate and sweat sodium usually shift with heat acclimation.
- **Evidence update:** EAH management, sweat-sodium predictors and fluid dosing are active research; the page adjusts if later high-quality consensus or reviews differ.
- **Disease and medication:** underlying conditions or drugs change the safe range for fluid and sodium; clinical assessment takes priority over the general figures here.
