## Definition and scope

Running hydration means planning fluid and electrolyte intake to offset sweat and breathing losses while avoiding over-drinking. It is for everyday, long-distance and hot-weather runners, and anyone tired of "a fixed number of litres per hour". It applies when no kidney, heart or endocrine condition restricts fluids and you adjust by observable signals, not copied numbers. One case where it holds: a run under 60 minutes at moderate intensity, where drinking to thirst is enough.

## Daily hydration first, training second

The NHS suggests drinking enough for urine to be clear pale yellow, and notes that long activity or heat may raise needs. That is a daily observation, not a race-day formula. Needs also depend on intensity, sweat rate, clothing, altitude and heat adaptation, so amounts differ between winter and summer, or 10 km and 30 km.

## Sweat rate and weight change

Learn your range by weighing before and after a long run, using the difference alongside what you drank. Do not drink large volumes to "prevent" dehydration in one session; excessive fluid can create a low-sodium risk, especially in long events. The aim is to return body weight to a sensible range, not to fill the deficit at once.

## More electrolytes is not always better

Sweat loses water and salt, but volume and sodium concentration vary widely. Normal food and drink are usually enough for short, moderate runs; electrolyte drinks may help in long, hot or high-sweat situations but should be trialled in training. Salt tablets cannot substitute for recognising heat symptoms and should not debut on race day.

## How to: three steps to find your range

1. Record a baseline: choose a steady 60-minute run, weigh before and after in similar clothing, and note intake.
2. Convert to a range: use the weight difference plus intake to estimate hourly losses; spread that over three or four small drinks next time.
3. Add variables: re-estimate for heat or longer distance, trial electrolytes in training, and race with them only after two or three comfortable sessions.

## Table: what matters in each setting

| Setting | Priority |
| --- | --- |
| 30-minute easy run | Drink to thirst; no extra plan |
| 60 to 90 minutes | Small, frequent drinks; record weight change |
| Over 2 hours | Plan fluid and electrolytes together |

## Worked example: a 90-minute long run (hypothetical)

All figures are hypothetical. Assume 60 kg before and 58.8 kg after, having drunk 0.6 litres. Total loss is about 1.8 kg, close to 3% of body weight; over 90 minutes that is about 1.2 kg per hour. The next similar session could aim for 0.6 to 1.0 kg per hour, split into four drinks of about 150 to 250 ml. Above a 2% deficit with dizziness, reduce intensity and rehydrate; with bloating, lower concentration and speed.

## Common misconceptions

- Copying "one litre per hour"; it may be too much or too little for the person and conditions.
- Assuming more electrolytes is better; high concentration can cause gut discomfort.

## Low sodium and over-drinking

Drinking only water without salt over many hours, or large volumes quickly, can dilute blood sodium. This differs from dehydration but still needs assessment. Stop drinking and seek care for headache, nausea, confusion, seizures or unusual swelling; diagnosis belongs to clinicians.

## When to stop and seek care

Stop and seek help for altered consciousness, persistent vomiting, chest pain, severe headache, seizures, markedly reduced urine output or obvious swelling. In heat, confusion, unusually hot skin or cold sweat may be heat stroke: call emergency services first and cool the person while waiting. People with kidney or heart conditions, or on diuretics, should plan fluids with a clinician.

## Frequency and progression

Treat hydration as a weekly habit: weigh around one long run each week and build your range over three weeks; re-estimate when a season or phase changes; fix the race plan two to three weeks out. Stabilise volume and rhythm first, then electrolyte concentration, then aid-station placement.

## Check yourself

- Daily urine is roughly clear pale yellow.
- Long runs are weighed before and after, with intake noted.
- Electrolytes have been trialled in training before a race.

## Scope and limits

This page does not prescribe individual fluid volumes, salt doses or electrolyte formulas, and does not diagnose dehydration or low blood sodium. People with kidney or heart conditions, those taking diuretics or other medication, and pregnant runners should consult a clinician.
