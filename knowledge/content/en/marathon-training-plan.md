## Conclusion: what to do first

Preparing for a 42.195 km road marathon starts with three inputs rather than someone else's mileage chart: your average weekly mileage over the past four weeks, how many weeks remain before race day, and how many days per week you can realistically allocate to running and recovery. The main line of the plan is to build aerobic base with low-intensity running, keep quality sessions to a small number each week, add one progressively longer long run, and reduce volume while preserving intensity during the final three weeks. Any week that brings persistent pain, clearly worse sleep, life events that eat recovery, or conditions too hot to train safely should return to the last stable weekly volume instead of pushing on with the schedule.

Three actions come first. First, record weekly mileage, longest run, number of quality sessions and the 24-hour post-run response to establish a progression baseline. Second, place easy runs, the quality session, the long run, strength work and rest days on the calendar, with at least one easy or rest day between hard sessions. Third, begin the taper roughly three weeks before the race: cut total volume, keep intensity and frequency, and test no new shoes, fuel or route on race day.

This page is training education, not a personal prescription. Where a coach, doctor or physiotherapist is involved, their assessment takes precedence. Chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness or worsening persistent pain should be treated as an emergency or care-seeking boundary.

## Who this is for, and who it is not for

**For you if** you can already run continuously for 60 minutes, have four stable weeks of roughly 20 km or more per week, and your target race is 18–24 weeks away; you are extending a half-marathon base to the full distance; or you coach athletes and need a periodization framework.

**Not for you if** you are new to running and cannot yet complete 30 minutes continuously — use the beginner and 5K/10K paths first. This page also does not apply during acute injury, post-surgical rehab, pregnancy, or with cardiovascular, metabolic or respiratory conditions, where intensity must be set by a clinician or qualified coach. If current mileage is already high enough to disturb sleep, appetite and daily function, the task is recovery, not race build-up; see [Load and overtraining](/en/knowledge/load-and-overtraining/).

Elite mileage must not be copied. Haugen 2022 reports that elite marathon runners reach 160–220 km per week in mid-preparation with 11–14 sessions per week; that follows many years of adaptation, not a starting point for a recreational runner. The Fokkema 2020 questionnaire of 441 marathon entrants only shows a directional association between weekly volume and finish time; it does not set a mileage threshold you must reach.

## Decision branches: from current mileage to target mileage

Start with a realistic inventory, then choose the path. The table below routes by current base rather than by a desired finish time.

| Current situation | Appropriate path | Likely plan length | First watch-out |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fewer than 3 runs/week, under 20 km/week, longest run under 10 km | Return to beginner and half-marathon paths; do not open a marathon plan directly | No 18-week marathon block | Build frequency and continuous running first; see [Return to running](/en/knowledge/return-to-running/) |
| 3–4 runs/week, 20–35 km, longest run 10–16 km | Thin-base marathon cycle; stretch phases, change one variable per week | 20–24 weeks | Progress the long run more slowly than quality work; build aerobic base first |
| 4–5 runs/week, 35–50 km, longest run 16 km+ | Standard race-preparation cycle | 18–20 weeks | Focus on specific long runs, race-pace sessions and taper |
| Previous finish, stable 50 km+ week | Performance-oriented cycle | 16–20 weeks | Control accumulated fatigue; do not push on previously injured sites |

Target mileage is derived from your recovery capacity, not from elite data. Completion rate, the 24-hour response, sleep and stable mood are the gates that permit added load. In Fokkema 2020's marathon sample, weekly volume below 40 km was associated with a slower finish and above 65 km with a faster finish; this is observational grouping. Runners who cover more may also have more years of training and better base fitness, so it cannot be read as "reach 65 km and you will go fast."

## Decision table: the 18–24 week phase structure

The table below is an illustrative assumption; phase lengths are editorial advice, not a validated uniform protocol. Haugen 2022 describes elites starting their taper 7–10 days before the main competition; Bosquet 2007 and the 2023 PLOS ONE taper meta-analysis support roughly two weeks at a 41%–60% volume reduction. Recreational cycles widen the taper to three weeks as a conservative arrangement.

| Phase | Weeks (example) | Volume direction | Quality sessions | Long run | Deloads |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Base | 6–8 | Small rise from current baseline | 1/week: strides, hills or short threshold bouts | Longest easy run of the week, gradually extended | Light week around week 4 |
| Specific | 8–10 | Peak weekly volume | 1–2/week: threshold, race-pace segments, short intervals | The cycle's longest runs; practice fueling and pace | Light week every 3–4 weeks |
| Taper | 2–3 | Down to 40%–60% of peak | Keep short, fast bouts; do not cut intensity | Clearly shortened | This phase is the deload |
| Race week | 1 | Low | 2–3 short easy runs plus strides | None | Execute the race |

The public evidence gap should be stated plainly: no study compares injury rates or finish outcomes between 18-week and 24-week amateur plans. A thinner base or less available life time argues for a longer cycle, not for compressed phases that backfill mileage later.

## The weekly template: easy run, quality session, long run, strength, rest

This is a reusable seven-day pattern (distance and time are illustrative). Intensity distribution follows Seiler 2010's observation of competitive endurance athletes: about 80% of sessions low intensity and about 20% high intensity. The Casado 2022 systematic review notes marathoners lean toward a pyramidal distribution (zone 1 volume above zone 2, zone 2 above zone 3) and recommends a hard-day/easy-day basis. Recreational runners need fewer quality sessions than either group.

| Day | Session | Intensity | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Rest, easy walk or recovery activity | Very low | Absorb last week's load |
| Tuesday | Easy run 40–60 min | Full sentences; about 40%–59% heart-rate reserve (ACSM) | Aerobic base |
| Wednesday | One quality session (threshold or intervals; see below) | Moderately hard to hard | Speed and tolerance stimulus |
| Thursday | Rest or [cross-training](/en/knowledge/cross-training-for-runners/) | Very low | Recovery, no running impact |
| Friday | Easy run 30–45 min | Easy | Maintain mobility |
| Saturday | One long run | Easy, slightly slow; practice fueling rhythm | Sustained-run tolerance |
| Sunday | Rest or easy recovery run | Very low | Recovery |

Add 1–2 strength and stability sessions per week on easy or cross-training days, never on the same day as a quality session or long run (see [Strength training for runners](/en/knowledge/strength-training-for-runners/)). Only one day each week is genuinely hard; two hard sessions back to back merge into one low-quality session.

## The long run: cap and progression rules

The long run is the part that most distinguishes a marathon plan from 5K/10K training. In Fokkema 2020's half-marathon sample, a longest endurance run above 21 km was associated with a faster finish and less late-race pace decline; marathon long runs showed the same directional association. This is correlation, though, not a prescription to reach a fixed distance.

Workable rules:

1. **Start from your current longest run.** Extend in small steps alongside weekly progression, watching the 24-hour response; do not copy someone else's long-run chart.
2. **Place it on the most stable day of the week**, preceded and followed by an easy or rest day. If you are still markedly fatigued the next day, the next long run does not grow.
3. **Keep long-run pace easy:** you should be able to converse; do not chase pace in a single long run. Race-pace work lives in specific-phase segment sessions.
4. **Practice fueling on the long run:** for steady exercise over 60 minutes, carbohydrate intake of about 30–60 g/hour (ISSN) should be rehearsed in training; race-day gels, fluids and breakfast are validated there first (see [Long-run fueling](/en/knowledge/long-run-fueling/)).
5. **No public source sets a uniform long-run distance cap for recreational runners** (unknown). Common arrangements keep the cycle's longest run below the race distance; whether you need to approach race distance depends on your base, recovery and goal, decided with a coach or experienced runner.

## Two quality-session paradigms: threshold versus intervals

A quality session is not "as fast as possible"; it is one of two distinct stimuli. The table below says which to pick and when one can be swapped out.

| Paradigm | Purpose | Typical form (example) | Relative intensity | Phase | Swap condition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Threshold (tempo) | Sustained tolerance near lactate threshold, matching late-marathon pressure | 20–40 min continuous, or 2–3 bouts of 10–20 min | Hard but controlled, near 10K to half-marathon pace range | Late base through specific phase | When recovery is short, shorten the bouts or convert to easy running |
| Intervals | VO2max-related ability, cadence and neural activation | Repeated short bouts with full recovery | Hard, around the 90% VO2max stimulus Seiler 2010 describes | Specific phase, at most once a week | If the week already carries a long run or hill load, intervals yield; do not stack two hard quality sessions in one week |

Seiler 2010 also found no convincing evidence that raising the proportion of high-intensity intervals gives long-term performance gains in already trained athletes; Casado 2022 similarly recommends traditional periodization and hard-easy days for highly trained runners. Quality sessions are the minority stimulus in the plan, not the weekly main line. For intensity boundaries, see [Heart-rate zones](/en/knowledge/heart-rate-zones/) and [Training pace zones](/en/knowledge/training-pace-zones/); pace-conversion formulas carry error and give a starting range only.

## The taper: three-week structure and preserved intensity

The most common taper error is cutting intensity along with volume. The Bosquet 2007 meta-analysis of 27 studies found performance gains came with an exponential volume reduction of 41%–60%, while intensity (effect 0.33±0.14) and frequency (effect 0.35±0.17) did not need to change. The 2023 PLOS ONE meta-analysis of 14 studies likewise associated a 41%–60% volume cut, preserved intensity and frequency, and durations up to 21 days with improved time-trial performance; groups overloaded before tapering gained more.

| Taper week | Volume direction | Intensity | Long run | Watch-outs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 weeks out | About 20%–30% below peak | Keep one short threshold or pace bout | Clearly shortened | No new stimulus |
| 2 weeks out | About 60% of peak | Keep short, fast bouts | Short easy run | Watch sleep and leg heaviness |
| 1 week out | 40%–50% of peak | 2–3 strides for activation | None | No new shoes, fuel or route |

Taper duration varies between individuals; PLOS ONE 2023 notes recovery speed and optimal taper length differ and call for individualized research. Persistent leg heaviness, worse sleep or declining performance suggests the pre-taper peak was too high, not that the taper was too short.

## Evidence and conflicts

### The 10% rule: a discussion heuristic, not a physiological law

"Never add more than 10% weekly mileage" is the most widely circulated rule, but research does not treat it as a fixed safety threshold. Buist 2008's GRONORUN RCT randomized 532 novices to a 13-week graded program based on the 10% rule or a standard 8-week program; injury incidence was 20.8% versus 20.3% (P=.90), with no significant injury reduction. An injury was defined as lower-extremity or back complaint restricting running for at least one week. Nielsen 2014 analyzed 873 novices with 202 injuries, grouped by progression under 10%, 10%–30%, and over 30%; no significant difference in overall injury rates appeared, and only distance-related injuries showed an upward trend above 30% (HR 1.59, 95% CI 0.96–2.66, P=.07, not statistically significant). The authors advise novices to keep weekly distance progression under 30% over a two-week period.

**Evidence conflict box:** popular guidance treats 10% as a hard threshold; the RCT did not show it reducing injury, and the cohort's >30% hazard ratio crosses 1 with P=.07. The usable line is: progression rate is a risk prompt, not a safety permit. Staying below a band does not guarantee no injury, and exceeding it does not guarantee injury; tendon tolerance, prior injury history, sleep and life stress all change capacity.

### Volume, long runs and injury: the association is inconsistent

Fokkema 2020 found no association between weekly volume or longest endurance run and running injury. The systematic review of 36 studies and 23,047 runners reports conflicting evidence linking weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity or recent changes to injury, and cautions against recommending one optimal progression for every runner. This page therefore does not claim "more mileage means more injury" or "more mileage means safer"; the 24-hour response and completion rate are the progression gates.

### How much elite data can be borrowed

Haugen 2022's descriptive elite figures (160–220 km/week, 11–14 sessions, ≥80% low intensity) can inform the **intensity distribution structure**, not the mileage. Elite runners race 6±2 times a year with full recovery support; recreational cycles should not copy their absolute volume.

### Which preparation practices currently lack evidence

- **Daily stretching for injury prevention:** in the Lauersen 2014 meta-analysis the stretching confidence interval crossed 1 with no protective effect (see [Running injury prevention](/en/knowledge/running-injury-prevention/)).
- **Raising the quality-session share above about one fifth:** Seiler 2010 found no convincing long-term benefit in trained athletes.
- **Wristwatch load scores as progression decisions:** devices give estimates and trends, not diagnosis; a good score does not prove tissues have adapted.

## Worked example: 30 km/week, 10% progression plus a four-week regression, week 16

The following is an assumed example illustrating the arithmetic of progression and deload, not a recommended plan. Baseline: a runner currently at 30 km/week, no current pain, about 7.5 hours of sleep. Rule: non-deload weeks multiply the prior week by 1.10; every fourth week is a regression week at 0.70 of the prior week.

| Week | Volume (km) | Calculation | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 30.0 | Baseline | Record |
| 2 | 33.0 | 30.0 × 1.10 | No worsening at 24 h |
| 3 | 36.3 | 33.0 × 1.10 | Stable |
| 4 | 39.9 | 36.3 × 1.10 | First peak |
| 5 | 28.0 | 39.9 × 0.70 | Regression week |
| 6 | 30.7 | 28.0 × 1.10 | Stable |
| 7 | 33.8 | 30.7 × 1.10 | Stable |
| 8 | 37.2 | 33.8 × 1.10 | Second peak |
| 9 | 26.0 | 37.2 × 0.70 | Regression week |
| 10 | 28.6 | 26.0 × 1.10 | Stable |
| 11 | 31.5 | 28.6 × 1.10 | Stable |
| 12 | 34.7 | 31.5 × 1.10 | Third peak |
| 13 | 24.3 | 34.7 × 0.70 | Regression week |
| 14 | 26.7 | 24.3 × 1.10 | Stable |
| 15 | 29.4 | 26.7 × 1.10 | Stable |
| 16 | 32.3 | 29.4 × 1.10 | End-of-cycle volume |

Reading the numbers: week 16 lands at about 32.3 km, only about 7.7% above baseline. This shows two things. First, a regression to 70% of the peak every four weeks is very conservative; practice light weeks usually sit at 70%–80% of the prior week, and their purpose is absorbing adaptation, not wiping out cycle progress. Second, a fixed 10% weekly progression nets slow growth when regressions are stacked on top. A real build adjusts by completion rate, 24-hour response and life rhythm rather than treating the percentage as a mechanical formula. Any week with persistent pain stops at that week's volume; do not continue the table. Use the [Injury risk check tool](/en/tools/injury-risk-check/) to organize pain, sleep and load variables.

## Common failure modes and early signals

1. **Mileage, long run and quality session all step up in the same week.** The watch mileage changes little, but tissue load changes substantially. Early signals: easy runs slow, perceived effort at the same pace rises, soreness extends into the next day.
2. **Hard sessions back to back.** With insufficient recovery, the second session degrades while accumulated fatigue still stacks up. Early signals: threshold bouts drop pace, interval recovery between bouts slows.
3. **Long runs never approach race pressure, then the race is forced.** Fueling, pacing and sustained-run tolerance have not been rehearsed, and problems concentrate late. Early signals: clear late-long-run pace drop, stomach distress after fueling.
4. **Cutting intensity during the taper.** Two weeks of easy jogging alone leave insufficient neural activation on race day. Check the taper table: volume drops, intensity does not.
5. **Catching up missed sessions.** Stacking missed sessions into the next week reshapes the whole week's load. Early signals: completion rate swings, weekend long runs get compressed.
6. **Reading fatigue as willpower.** Persistent fatigue, plateau or declining performance, and sleep or mood changes should first prompt lower load and illness exclusion. Overtraining syndrome has no single diagnostic marker; assessment is clinical, reviewing training, recovery and health factors (see [Load and overtraining](/en/knowledge/load-and-overtraining/)).

## When not to follow this table

The conditions below pause or redesign the plan; the table is not a "test with one more run" gate.

| Situation | Action | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Swelling, deformity, redness or heat at a joint or tendon, or fever; joint giving way or locking; inability to bear weight | Stop running and seek clinical assessment first; do not add load per plan | NHS running-injury guidance: do not run in pain; severe pain or swelling means prompt care |
| Same-site pain after three consecutive runs, or pain that does not return to baseline within 24 hours | Stop progression, return to the last stable weekly volume, follow the relevant injury page | 24-hour response gate; see [Pain and load](/en/knowledge/pain-and-load/) |
| Two consecutive weeks of worse sleep, elevated resting heart rate, declining performance | Reduce volume to recover, exclude illness, seek professional assessment if needed | Overtraining guide: persistent fatigue and performance decline call for recovery shortfall and cause review |
| Family, work or travel events disrupt routine | Keep minimal activity volume; return from the stable weekly volume after the event; do not backfill mileage | When recovery conditions change, sustainability comes first |
| Heat, humidity or poor air quality make safe training impossible | Move indoors or to a low-impact alternative, shorten duration; do not force a quality session in heat | See [Heat and humidity](/en/knowledge/heat-and-humidity/); conditions change intensity choices |
| Menstrual disturbance paired with weight loss and repeated stress injuries in women | Assess energy availability rather than adjusting mileage alone | See the female runner and RED-S pages |

## What would change this advice

- **Population:** novices, returning runners, older runners, women and previously injured runners differ in tolerance and risk background; the same weekly structure does not transfer across groups.
- **Phase:** base, specific and taper phases follow different load logic; altitude, heat and repeated travel all change recovery conditions, and phase priorities must be reordered.
- **Evidence update:** progression thresholds, taper duration and intensity distribution remain active research areas; conclusions adjust if later high-quality cohorts or reviews differ.
- **Health status:** runners with arthritis, tendon history, cardiovascular or metabolic conditions should have the plan assessed clinically first.
- **Race type:** this page targets road marathons; trail and ultra demands for climbing, night running, carrying load and back-to-back long sessions differ, see [Trail and ultra training plan](/en/knowledge/trail-ultra-training-plan/).

## Applicable boundaries

This page is general running training education, not a personal plan, diagnosis, treatment prescription or medication advice.
