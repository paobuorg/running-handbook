## What this page covers

This page is about **weekly running volume** — the total distance you run in a week — and how it changes over several weeks. It is for people who can already run continuously for 20–30 minutes and want to build from their current level, including runners rebuilding volume after an injury. It does **not** apply during an acute injury, during the two-week taper before a race, or when weekly volume is already disrupting sleep, appetite or daily functioning. In those situations, address load or recovery first.

Weekly volume is one handle, not the whole load. Frequency, session duration, intensity, gradient, surface, shoes and life stress all change what the body actually absorbs. Recording weekly volume gives you a comparable baseline; it does not reduce training to a single number.

## What the common rules actually show

| Common advice | Evidence | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Increase by no more than 10% a week | The 2008 GRONORUN randomised controlled trial: 532 novice runners preparing for a 4-mile (6.7 km) event; injury incidence was 20.8% in the 13-week graded group and 20.3% in the 8-week standard group, P = .90 | A discussion starting point, not a personal safety line |
| Increasing more than 30% within two weeks is riskier | A 2014 one-year cohort: 874 novice runners recruited, 873 analysed; no significant difference in overall injury rates across three groups (below 10%, 10–30%, above 30%); distance-related injuries had a hazard ratio of 1.59 (95% CI 0.96–2.66, P = .07) | The authors advise less than 30% within two weeks; this is observational and cannot prove causation |
| No single progression formula fits everyone | A 2022 systematic review: 36 studies and 23,047 runners, conflicting evidence, no universal recommendation | It explains why this page offers a framework rather than a number |

GRONORUN defined injury as a lower-limb or back complaint that restricted running for **at least one week**. That is much stricter than "a bit sore after a run", and it is a reminder that percentages from different studies rest on different definitions, populations and follow-up periods. The trial's 20.8% sits below the 30–79% incidence range quoted in its own introduction precisely because definitions and populations differ; the larger range should not be presented as the "true" injury rate.

## Why the 10% rule did not pass its test

GRONORUN's logic was simple. If the body needs more time to adapt, spreading the same total distance over 13 weeks instead of 8 should produce fewer injuries. The two groups ended almost identical (20.8% versus 20.3%, P = .90). The authors concluded that a graded programme built on the 10% rule did not reduce injury incidence in novice runners.

The same paper's background reports two frequently quoted figures: injury incidence across studies ranges from 30% to 79%, or 7 to 59 injuries per 1000 hours of running, and an estimate that about 60% of running injuries can be attributed to training errors — running too much, too soon. Together these figures say that progression speed is an important factor, but not the only one. A single percentage rule cannot explain all injuries.

## Three signals that beat a percentage

1. **Your own baseline.** Take the average weekly distance over the past four weeks, excluding clearly exceptional high and low weeks. Progress from that baseline, not from what someone else runs.
2. **The 24-hour response.** At the same time the next day, are pain, stiffness and overall fatigue back to pre-run levels? This gate is closer to your body than any percentage.
3. **One variable at a time.** Change only frequency, session duration or intensity. If you add distance and speed work together, you cannot tell which one caused a problem.

## Weekly decision table

The percentages below are experience-based ranges, not research conclusions. Their job is to turn "add, hold or reduce" into an executable choice.

| Last week | 24-hour response | Next week |
|---|---|---|
| Completed as planned, no worsening | Pain and fatigue back to pre-run levels | Add one variable, about 5–10% |
| Completed more than 80%, mild soreness gone within 24 hours | Essentially recovered | Hold at last week's volume |
| Completed less than 80%, or pain lasts beyond 24 hours | Not recovered | Reduce by 20–30% and restore completion first |
| Same-site pain on three consecutive runs | Red flag | Stop progressing, follow the relevant injury page and consider assessment |

## A four-week framework

1. **Week 1.** Run as usual and only record: sessions, total distance, longest run, how it felt. Do not add volume.
2. **Week 2.** Pick one small change — an extra very short easy run, or 5–10 extra minutes on one easy run.
3. **Week 3.** Hold week 2's volume and watch the 24-hour response.
4. **Week 4.** If weeks 2 and 3 were stable, take another small step. If any week was unstable, return to the last stable volume.

Every three to four weeks, schedule a lighter week at roughly 70–80% of the previous week. This is common training practice rather than a research conclusion; it creates room for adaptation and makes accumulating fatigue easier to spot.

## A worked example (hypothetical)

Runner: 30 km a week over four sessions for the past four weeks, with a 10 km long run. Goal: 40 km a week in eight weeks, with no race pressure.

| Week | Weekly volume | Change | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 30 km | Record baseline | — |
| 2 | 32 km | Add 2 km to the long run | No worsening at 24 hours |
| 3 | 32 km | Hold | Stable |
| 4 | 34 km | Add 2 km to one easy run | Stable |
| 5 | 24 km | Lighter week (about 70%) | Planned reduction |
| 6 | 36 km | Add 2 km to the long run | Stable |
| 7 | 38 km | Add 2 km to one easy run | Stable |
| 8 | 40 km | Goal reached | — |

This example shows the order — small steps, one variable, a lighter week — and is not a prescription. A real plan follows the 24-hour response. If any week produces persistent pain, stay at that volume instead of pushing on with the table.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The 10% rule is a proven safety line."** A 532-runner randomised trial did not find that it reduced injury rates. It is better treated as a conservative discussion starting point.
- **"Staying under 30% means you cannot get injured."** That figure comes from an observational cohort whose confidence interval crosses 1; the authors themselves called the result exploratory.
- **"Progressing volume means adding distance."** Frequency, session length and intensity all change load. Changing one at a time is what makes the response readable.
- **"Follow the table even when it hurts."** The table assumes a 24-hour recovery. Pain that persists into the next day means the table no longer applies.
- **"A lighter week is laziness."** A planned lighter week is part of the framework, not an interruption.
- **"The watch's load score replaces judgement."** Devices estimate trends; they do not diagnose, and a good score does not mean tissue has adapted.

## When to stop progressing and seek care

- Pain changes your walking pattern, causes a limp, or is present at rest or at night;
- Sharp focal pain, obvious swelling, local heat, or a recent injury;
- The same-site pain appears on three or more runs and does not improve after two reduced weeks;
- Completion keeps falling alongside worse sleep, a clearly elevated resting heart rate or low mood;
- A female runner has menstrual disruption together with weight loss and repeated stress injuries — assess for low energy availability (see the female runners and RED-S page);
- Chest pain, fainting, unusual palpitations or unresolving breathlessness — treat as an emergency.

## Checklist

- [ ] Do I know my average weekly volume over the past four weeks?
- [ ] Did I change only one variable this week?
- [ ] At 24 hours, have pain and fatigue returned to pre-run levels?
- [ ] Have I scheduled a lighter week in the last three to four weeks?
- [ ] Am I using someone else's volume as my baseline?
- [ ] If pain persists, do I stop progressing instead of following the table?

## Limits

This page is training education, not a personal plan or medical advice. The two studies involved novice runners preparing for a 4-mile event and self-structured novice runners over one year; they do not transfer directly to highly experienced runners with high volume or to someone in the middle of a race build. "Conflicting evidence" in the systematic review means no specific percentage is settled. Pregnant or postpartum runners, people recovering from surgery, people with cardiovascular or metabolic disease, and people taking bone-active medication should be assessed by a clinician or a qualified coach first.
