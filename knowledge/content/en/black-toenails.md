## Definition and scope

**Black toenail** is a description, not a diagnosis. In runners, the usual cause is a **subungual haematoma**: blood collects between the nail bed and the nail plate and appears red, dark red or black through the nail. It may follow a single impact, or a period of vigorous activity with no injury remembered — PCDS clinical guidance specifically notes that toenail subungual haematomas **often have no clear history of trauma**.

This page is for: runners who have developed a black toenail, a lifting nail or a lost nail after increasing mileage; runners with recurrent black toenails who want to work on shoe fit and trimming; anyone asking whether to drain the blood.

It is **not** for: people with diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, reduced sensation or immunosuppression (these situations need earlier podiatry or medical assessment); people with nail colour change plus skin lesions, a bleeding disorder, or anticoagulant treatment; or anyone seeking a diagnosis, drug or device prescription from this site.

## First, separate blood from other colour change

Most runner black toenails are subungual haematomas, but "black" does not always mean "blood". PCDS's nail disorders chapter offers some direction:

- **Subungual haematoma**: usually red or red-black, moves distally as the nail grows; the colour may be split into patches rather than forming a neat band.
- **Longitudinal melanonychia**: a brown/black band running from the nail matrix to the tip. **Any new or changing single black/brown band** should raise the question of subungual melanoma, especially if pigment extends onto the surrounding skin (Hutchinson sign).
- **Onychomycosis (fungal nail infection)**: more often yellow-white, usually starting at the distal free edge and advancing proximally; a subungual haematoma instead moves distally with nail growth, so the colour and starting pattern differ.

NHS "Nail problems" gives a straightforward boundary: **see a GP if a nail has changed shape, changed colour or fallen off and you do not know why**. That applies to runners too. If a black nail appears without a change of shoe, an increase in load, a descent or an impact — or if it does not move outward as the nail grows — it should not be explained away as "a runner's haematoma".

## Causes: shoes, descents, nail length and laces

NHS podiatry material attributes black toenails to **injury**: either a direct impact or **badly fitting footwear**. RDaSH's foot-care page is more specific: black toenails are caused by injury, which may be one direct injury from dropping an object, but can also come from **badly fitting shoes**; the discolouration is a bleed under the nail. For runners, the mechanisms usually fall into four groups.

### 1. Not enough room in the toe box

NHS Forth Valley's footwear advice states that shoes must be **long enough, deep enough and wide enough**; pointed or narrow shoes squeeze the toes and can cause corneas and joint problems. Guy's and St Thomas' shoe guidance adds that the toe box must be deep enough not to press on the tops of the toes, the front should be round or square rather than pointed, and the sole should be wider than the widest part of the foot. A narrow, shallow or pointed toe box pushes the toes into the front of the shoe on every landing, repeatedly loading the nail bed.

### 2. Descent impact and repeated microtrauma

On descents the foot slides forward inside the shoe more easily, and the toes repeatedly hit the front; if the shoe also has extra length, the "slide-and-jam" distance is longer. PCDS describes subungual haematoma as something that can occur after **vigorous activity with no remembered trauma**; RDaSH also lists badly fitting footwear. Descent is not the only cause, but it magnifies the toe-box problem. **This mechanical explanation is the site's interpretation of a common mechanism, not a verbatim conclusion from the official sources.**

### 3. Nails too long or too short

- **Too long**: the nail hits the front of the shoe first, acting as a lever that pulls against the nail bed with every step.
- **Too short**: NHS's ingrown toenail page explicitly warns not to cut toenails too short, because this increases the risk of ingrown nails and damage to the surrounding skin; RDaSH also advises leaving a little of the white nail edge.

Both ends are wrong. The aim is **straight, with a small white edge, not embedded into the sides**.

### 4. Laces tightened over the forefoot

Guy's guidance is clear: laces or Velcro should fasten the shoe at the **arch** of the foot, which leaves room for the toes to function; slip-on shoes without a fastening hold on by fitting tightly around the toes. Conversely, laces tightened over the front of the foot reduce the volume available in the toe box, making it easier for the toes to hit the front. NHS Forth Valley also lists an adjustable fastening as a feature of a suitable shoe.

**Practical suggestion from this site (not official guidance)**: if the toe box feels tight, try skipping the lowest pair of eyelets and lacing from the second pair, keeping the hold around the midfoot; after tying, you should be able to wiggle your toes gently rather than relying on "tying tighter" for stability. Re-tie before a long descent rather than using one tension for the whole run.

Other contributors: a sudden increase in mileage or climbing, foot swelling in heat, and socks that are too small or have bulky seams. Guy's suggests trying shoes late in the day when feet are larger; RDaSH warns against buying shoes "to grow into", because too-big shoes cause as much trouble as too-small ones.

## Prevention: fit allowance, toe box, lacing and trimming

Here is a checklist to work through; the details follow.

| Check | Target | Public source |
|---|---|---|
| Room in the shoe | About 1 cm, or a thumb's width, beyond the longest toe | NHS podiatry, Guy's |
| Toe box shape | Deep, wide, round or square; not pressing on the toes | Guy's, NHS Forth Valley |
| Lacing | Fastened over the arch, not by gripping the toes | Guy's |
| Nail length | Cut straight across, leave a little white edge, do not cut the sides | NHS, RDaSH |
| Training load | Spread increases in mileage, climbing and descending | Site summary |

**A practical example**: stand in your running socks and reach the longest toe forward. If a thumb (about 1 cm) still fits in front of the toe, the allowance is roughly right; if the toe is already touching the end, or there is a large empty gap, the size or shape of the shoe needs reassessing.

### How much room to leave

NHS podiatry guidance gives **about 1 cm**, or **a thumb's width**, between the longest toe and the end of the shoe. Guy's athletic footwear page adds a useful measurement: the foot lengthens by **about 5 mm** between standing and walking, so fit should be checked standing, not sitting.

In practice:

1. **Try shoes standing**, wearing the socks you actually run in; try both shoes and fit the larger foot.
2. Leave **about 1 cm / a thumb's width** beyond the longest toe — not "just touching", and not a large empty gap.
3. The toes should be able to **move gently** inside the toe box, the instep should not be compressed, and the heel should be held by the heel counter (Guy's also suggests being able to insert a finger behind the heel).
4. Try shoes late in the day when the feet are slightly swollen; do not buy oversized shoes "to grow into" (RDaSH).

### Toe box shape and sole

- The toe box should be **deep** enough not to press on the tops of the toes, and **wide and round/square** rather than squeezing the toes (Guy's, NHS Forth Valley).
- The sole should be wider than the widest part of the foot, and the heel should be stable at landing (Guy's).
- If the toe box is wide enough but the little toe is still squeezed, Guy's suggests looking at the shape of the sole: some shoes curve inward at the outer front edge and squash the little toe; a straighter shape helps.

### Lacing

- Choose shoes with **six or more eyelets** and an adjustable fastening (Guy's athletic footwear).
- Fasten over the **arch**, not by gripping the toes (Guy's).
- Do not tighten laces until the instep feels compressed; after tying, check that the toes can still move. If the toe box is tight, try skipping the lowest eyelet pair (site suggestion).

### How and when to trim

NHS and RDaSH agree:

- **When**: trim after a bath or shower, when the nail is softer and less likely to split.
- **How**: cut **straight across** with nail clippers; do not cut down into the sides; only round the edges slightly so they do not catch the next toe.
- **Length**: leave a little of the white edge; **do not cut too short**, because short nails increase the risk of ingrown nails.
- **Do not clean under the nail with sharp objects** (NHS "Nail problems" Don't list).
- If nails are too thick to cut, or you cannot reach them, NHS advises seeing a podiatrist rather than forcing it.

### Training load

Black toenails often appear in weeks when mileage, climbing or descending increases suddenly. Spreading the increase, checking laces and toe room before long descents, and allowing for foot swelling in heat is more effective than treating the nail afterwards. This is not a reason to avoid increasing load; it is a reason to make shoe and nail preparation keep pace with the load.

## What to do once there is blood under the nail

The short answer: **most small subungual haematomas need no active treatment.**

- NHS Borders' podiatry nail disorders page states "**No management required**"; RDaSH explains that the colour comes from bleeding under the nail, that the nail may loosen and eventually fall off, and that a new nail will grow back.
- **Stop or reduce running while the toe is painful**: RDaSH recommends avoiding sports until the pain in the toe subsides; open-toed or wider shoes may be more comfortable in the meantime.
- **Keep it clean and dry**; do not repeatedly press, probe or try to dig out the "clot".
- If the nail is already loose, NHS "Nail problems" advises **cutting the injured, loose nail back to where it is still attached**, so it does not catch on socks or shoes and tear further; do not rip the whole nail off.
- **Let it grow out**: RDaSH notes that a big toenail may take **about a year** to regrow completely; NHS's nail problems page states that toenails can take **up to 18 months** to grow back after an injury. Both are "may take", not a guarantee.
- The new nail **may be permanently deformed or thickened** (RDaSH). If that happens, it should be assessed by a podiatrist rather than sanded or treated with a device at home.

If pain is severe, the blood collection is large, the injury involved a heavy object or a kick, or the nail is clearly lifting, seek medical assessment **the same day**. StatPearls' clinical review notes that decompression of an acute subungual haematoma is usually recommended within **48 hours** of injury; after that window the blood may have clotted and whether to intervene is a clinical judgement. **That window is for clinicians, not for home treatment.**

## Why you should not drain it yourself

This is the most important boundary on this page: **do not use a needle, paper clip, blade, heated tool or any other "blood-letting" method on a black toenail yourself.**

The reason is not "it might hurt"; it is that this is a clinical procedure:

1. **It needs sterile conditions.** StatPearls describes the standard procedure as including cleaning and disinfection, sometimes a digital nerve block, and equipment such as electrocautery or an 18-gauge needle, with **sterile technique** to reduce procedure-related infection. A needle, nail clipper and kitchen table at home do not meet that standard.
2. **Fracture and nail-bed laceration must be excluded.** A subungual haematoma may accompany a distal phalanx fracture or a nail-bed laceration; StatPearls advises radiographs and, when needed, point-of-care ultrasound to assess the nail bed. You cannot assess these at home, and a procedure meant to repair the nail bed may be missed if the injury is treated as "just blood".
3. **It can turn a haematoma into an infection.** Reported complications include infection, nail loss and recurrence (StatPearls); the same source advises against soaking after the procedure because of bacterial contamination. Home drainage multiplies these risks.
4. **Non-traumatic "black nail" has no drainage benefit.** StatPearls states explicitly that **non-traumatic subungual haematomas** do not benefit from trephination; they may be due to a tumour (including junctional naevus or melanoma) or splinter haemorrhage, and need diagnosis rather than drainage.
5. **It can damage the nail bed and leave permanent deformity.** Advancing too deep can injure the nail bed; StatPearls warns against this. Home procedures offer no such control.

So **"pierce it and let the blood out" is not a home-care step for runners**. What you can do is limited to: reduce pain, keep the area clean, protect a loose nail, and watch whether it grows out as expected. What you should not do includes puncture, incision, corrosive agents, heated metal, or repeatedly digging under the nail. If the pain or size is enough to warrant decompression, hand it to a clinician.

## When to seek care

The boundaries below are drawn from NHS, PCDS and StatPearls public material; they are not diagnostic criteria.

**Infection signs (seek care promptly)**
- Increasing **redness, swelling, warmth and pain** around the nail;
- **pus** coming out;
- fever, chills or feeling generally unwell (NHS's ingrown toenail page lists "pus coming out" and "feel hot or shivery" as infection signs);
- pain that keeps worsening instead of settling after the acute phase.

**Nail-specific problems**
- A nail that has **changed shape, changed colour or fallen off and you do not know why** (NHS "Nail problems");
- skin around the nail that is sore, red, swollen and warm, suggesting paronychia or an ingrown nail (NHS "Nail problems");
- a nail that **does not move outward as it grows**, or a colour band that **widens, darkens or extends onto the surrounding skin** (PCDS melanoma clues; NHS Borders advises urgent dermatology referral for suspected subungual melanoma);
- a nail that becomes permanently thick or deformed, or repeatedly lifts (RDaSH advises seeing an HCPC-registered podiatrist).

**Recurrent black toenails**
- Black nails that keep coming back after changing shoes, lacing and trimming: assess shoe last, foot shape, gait and training load rather than simply tolerating them.

**Underlying disease (seek care earlier)**
- **Diabetes**: NHS's ingrown toenail page says people with diabetes and an ingrown toenail should see a GP, because foot problems can be more serious with diabetes; NHS Borders lists diabetes (moderate/high risk) or peripheral vascular disease as criteria for **urgent podiatry referral**.
- **Peripheral vascular disease, reduced sensation, immunosuppression, anticoagulant treatment**: any subungual haematoma or foot break should be assessed earlier than in an otherwise healthy runner.
- NHS England diabetes foot material advises **checking the feet every day** for blisters, breaks, pain or infection signs (swelling, heat, redness), and contacting a podiatrist or GP within 24 hours if a break is found. NHS blister advice adds: cover with a soft plaster or padded dressing and **do not burst it yourself**.

**When melanoma must be excluded**
- A new or changing single black/brown longitudinal band;
- pigment involving the skin around the nail (Hutchinson sign);
- a black nail with no trauma history that does not move outward as the nail grows.
These should be **referred urgently to dermatology**, not observed as "a runner's haematoma".

## Common misconceptions

- **"A black nail is fungal — just use an antifungal."** Fungal infection is more often yellow-white and advances from the distal edge; a subungual haematoma is usually red-black and moves distally with growth. The two can coexist, and either can look atypical. Unexplained colour change should be assessed rather than treated as fungus by assumption.
- **"Draining it makes it heal faster."** Trephination is a clinical procedure requiring sterile technique, equipment and assessment for fracture or nail-bed injury; home drainage adds infection and nail-bed damage. Non-traumatic black nails do not benefit from drainage and may be melanoma.
- **"If the nail is loose, pull it off."** NHS advises cutting a loose nail back to where it is still attached to avoid catching it; do not tear it off.
- **"A bigger shoe is always safer."** RDaSH warns that too-big shoes cause as much trouble as too-small ones; aim for about 1 cm of room, not maximum space.
- **"Cut nails shorter so they cannot hit the shoe."** Too-short nails increase ingrown-nail risk; cut straight across, leave a white edge, and do not cut into the sides.
- **"If it doesn't hurt, it doesn't need checking."** Unexplained nail colour or shape change needs assessment; early melanoma may not hurt.
- **"Once a black nail, always a black nail."** Toe-box room, lacing, trimming and descent load can all change; recurrence means at least one of these has not been addressed.

## Scope and limits

This page is education about foot care and runner self-management. It gives no diagnosis, treatment, drug or device recommendation, and does not replace assessment by a podiatrist or doctor. All statements about regrowth time and fit allowance are ranges or clinical experience from public sources, not guarantees for an individual. Diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, reduced sensation, immunosuppression, anticoagulant treatment, fungal nail infection and subungual melanoma must be managed by the relevant professional. No puncture, incision, debridement or instrument-based procedure should be performed at home. If you are unsure whether a black nail is blood or another pigment, treat it as "unexplained" and seek care; that is the safest boundary.

## A grading and action table

| Presentation | Common cause | What you can do first | When to seek care |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A small dark red patch, no pain, nail plate still attached | Toe-box impact or downhill microtrauma | Switch to a wider toe box, reduce downhill volume, observe for 1–2 weeks | Colour spreads, throbbing pain or exudate appears |
| Blood covers more than half the nail, persistent throbbing | Repeated impact raises subungual pressure | Do not drain it yourself; loosen laces, cool, reduce impact | Severe pain, nail plate loosens or walking is affected |
| Nail plate lifts and nail bed is exposed | Nail-bed laceration or shoe upper pulling | Clean, cover and protect, stop pressing it | Bleeding does not stop, nail bed tears or weight-bearing fails |
| Nail fold is red, swollen, purulent or hot | Bacterial infection or ingrown nail | Do not squeeze or cut the nail yourself | Redness spreads, fever, diabetes or poor circulation |

## A downhill worked example

Example: 10 km downhill at 180 steps/min and 5:00/km gives about 4,500 ground contacts per foot; if the toe box leaves only 5 mm, each contact can shift the toe 3–5 mm and bruise risk accumulates with distance. The example shows the order of magnitude of clearance and contacts, not an individual force measurement; leaving about 10 mm of toe-box clearance and retying laces before a descent is more controllable than draining the nail afterwards.
