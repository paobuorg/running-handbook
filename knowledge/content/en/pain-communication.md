## Definition and preconditions

Pain communication is defined as describing location, timing, triggers and functional impact in structured language. The page applies to runners preparing for a consultation about running-related pain. It holds when you describe the phenomenon before naming it, can recall recent load changes, and accept that a professional makes the diagnosis. If you cannot bear weight or have a red flag, seek care directly instead of perfecting a log.

## Why “just a bit tight” does not help

“My knee feels tight” offers nothing checkable: which spot, what movement aggravates it, how long, whether there is swelling. The same pain can come from different tissues, and one popular label can cover different presentations. A review of 36 studies and 23,047 runners found 26.2% reported a running-related injury, while evidence linking weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity or recent changes to injury was conflicting.

## Six elements of a structured report

Location, onset, trigger, relief, severity from 0 to 10, and next-day response. Point to the sharpest spot with one finger and say whether it is superficial, deep or follows a line. Separate sudden from gradual onset, and name the trigger: stairs down, single-leg standing, a heel raise. Record what rest or a drill did. Running on the day but stiffer next morning differs from pain during the run.

| Element | What to write | Common omission |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Location | Sharpest point, depth, spread | “Knee” only |
| Onset | Date, sudden or gradual | “A while ago” |
| Trigger | Specific movement, load change | “Too much running” |
| Relief | Response to rest or drills | Concluding without trying |

## How to do it: three steps before the appointment

1. Keep a 7-day log: location, a 0 to 10 score, the worst movement, distance and sleep, one line a day.
2. Put 4 to 6 weeks of load on one page: weekly distance, longest run, quality sessions, shoe or surface changes, races.
3. Write the two questions that matter most, such as “can running continue” and “how long should a pause last”.

## Worked example: a knee-pain report

The case is hypothetical. Assume 10 days of pain at the front of the right knee: just below and slightly inside the kneecap, pointable with one finger, superficial; it began after a 6 km run and now starts about 3 km into each run; stairs down and a single-leg squat are worst; a rest day helps but 3 km brings it back; the score is 4 out of 10 on the day and 2 out of 10 next morning with stiffness; weekly distance rose from 25 km to 35 km over 3 weeks.

## Red flags: when to stop and seek care

Do not run while in pain: severe, persistent pain or pain with swelling calls for medical or physiotherapy advice. Seek care promptly for inability to bear weight, marked swelling, a locked or deformed joint, fever, night pain that wakes you, progressive numbness or weakness, bladder or bowel changes, chest pain, fainting or severe breathlessness; after a head impact, worsening headache, vomiting or altered consciousness is an emergency. Persistent fatigue with a performance decline or plateau should raise overtraining and exclusion of underlying illness; no single marker exists, so assessment is clinical. Ill-fitting or worn-out shoes are among factors in heel pain; fitting matters more than price.

## Common misconceptions

- “Just tight, so stretching will fix it.” Tightness is not a diagnosis, and stretching can aggravate some problems.
- Naming the condition before the clinician assesses it. A fixed conclusion makes contradictory details easy to omit.
- Masking symptoms with painkillers and running on.

## Frequency and review

Fill the log at the same time daily until the appointment; if symptoms persist, refresh the timeline every 2 weeks.

## Pre-appointment checklist

- Sharpest point shown with one finger, depth described.
- Timeline gives first appearance and latest flare.
- 7-day log complete with scores and triggers.
- Last 4 to 6 weeks of load on one page.

## Scope and limits

This page offers a communication framework, not a diagnosis, imaging reading, medication advice or rehabilitation prescription. A qualified professional judges the cause with an examination; pregnancy, surgery, chronic disease or neurological symptoms need specialist assessment.
