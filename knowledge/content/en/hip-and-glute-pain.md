## What this page solves

“Hip pain” is a location, not a diagnosis. A runner's hip or glute pain may come from gluteus medius/minimus load, proximal hamstring, hip flexor, the hip joint itself, or low-back and pelvic compensation. Load management differs, but the shared principle is: reduce load, rebuild capacity, not just stretch and take painkillers.

This page does not replace imaging or clinical examination; with red flags, seek care first.

## Three common load patterns

| Pattern | Typical presentation | Common trigger | First move |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Glute med/min load | Outer hip pain, pain lying on side, poor single-leg stance | Volume spike, descent, sitting | Cut volume; hip abduction and single-leg stability |
| Proximal hamstring | Pain under glute/ischium, worse sitting | Speed work, sprinting, climbing | Stop speed; avoid deep hip-flexion stretch |
| Hip flexor | Anterior groin pain, worse with knee lift/climb | Sitting, sudden climbing, excessive hip extension | Cut climbing; glute and trunk control |
| Hip joint itself | Deep groin pain, restricted motion | Structural issue, age | Clinical assessment; do not push through |

This is not a diagnostic table; it turns “where it hurts” into “which load to reduce first”.

## Red flags: not a load problem

Stop running and jumping and seek prompt care for any of: fever, night pain waking you, inability to weight-bear, marked swelling, progressive weakness, persistent deep groin pain with restricted motion, sudden severe pain after trauma, cancer history or unexplained weight loss. Do not mask red flags with painkillers.

| Signal | Why not wait |
| --- | --- |
| Fever + hip pain | Infection/inflammation must be excluded |
| Night pain waking you | Structural or systemic issue needs assessment |
| Cannot single-leg stand | Stability/structural problem |
| Progressive weakness | Neurological or muscle problem |
| Cannot walk after trauma | Fracture/dislocation must be excluded |

## Self-check: three movements, not a diagnosis

1. **Single-leg stand 30 s**: does the pelvis drop markedly, does the body sway; compare sides.
2. **Side-lying hip abduction ×10**: does it reproduce outer hip pain; can you control the pelvis?
3. **Single-leg bridge ×10**: do the glutes work, or do the thigh/back compensate?

One clear asymmetry or pain does not diagnose anything, but it suggests reducing load and training basic stability.

## Load bands

| Band | Presentation | Volume | Strength | Review |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Green | Mild during running, settles within 24 h | Maintain; cut descent/speed | Hip abduction, bridge, calf raise | 3–5 days |
| Amber | Rises during running, >24 h after | Cut 30–50%; no quality | Pain-free isometrics and single-leg stability | 48–72 h |
| Red | Rest/night pain, limp, weakness | Stop running/jumping | Pain-free movement only | Same-day care |

Amber is not permission to push. If it escalates after reducing, or does not improve in two weeks, get professional assessment.

## Four return stages

| Stage | Content | Pass criterion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Walk 20–30 min + single-leg stand | No next-day flare |
| 2 | Walk 5 min / jog 1 min × 4–6 | Pain ≤2/10 at 24 h |
| 3 | Continuous easy jog 10–20 min | Normal gait, no compensation |
| 4 | Easy 20–30 min every other day | Stable for a week before quality |

Repeat each stage 2–3 times; if pain rises during the run or does not settle afterwards, go back one stage.

## Strength: glute med and calf before heavy load

| Movement | Dose | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Side-lying hip abduction | 2–3 × 10–15 | Glute med endurance |
| Single-leg bridge | 2–3 × 8–12 | Hip extension and pelvic control |
| Split squat | 2–3 × 6–8 | Single-leg weight bearing |
| Single-leg calf raise | 2–3 × 10–15 | Foot/ankle and gait stability |
| Dead bug/bird dog | 2 × 8–10 | Trunk anti-rotation |

In amber, work only in a pain-free range; in red, no loading. Strength is not “train harder through pain”; it is progressive load without pain.

## Two worked examples

**Outer hip pain after volume rising from 40 to 60 km/week.** Cut to 35–40 km, remove descent and speed; daily side-lying abduction and single-leg bridge, calf raises every other day. Review on day 5; if pain is ≤2/10 at 24 hours, add about 10% weekly. If side-lying pain persists, change the lying position and place a pillow between the knees.

**Proximal hamstring pain after speed work.** Stop speed and sprinting for two weeks; avoid deep hip-flexion stretching; use bridges, hip extension and trunk control; when walking is pain-free, follow the four stages. If sitting pain or night pain persists, seek care.

## Boundaries

Hip pain has complex causes. This page provides load management and return-to-run framing, not diagnosis, imaging interpretation or medication. Red flags override the training advice.

## One-week load rebuild table

After an amber reduction, do not return to previous volume because “it feels better”. Use one-week blocks: restore frequency, then duration, then climbing and speed. Example: previous 50 km/week over five runs.

| Day | Volume | Strength | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mon | Rest/walk 20 min | Pain-free isometrics | Observe morning |
| Tue | Easy 6 km | Hip abduction + bridge | No climbing |
| Wed | Rest | Calf raise + dead bug | Sleep well |
| Thu | Easy 7 km | Hip abduction + split squat | Flat |
| Fri | Rest/walk | Pain-free movement | Recheck pain |
| Sat | Easy 8 km | Light | No quality |
| Sun | Walk 30 min | None | Review week |

If pain is ≤2/10 at 24 hours and gait is normal, add 10–15% next week; if it rises, return to the previous week and do not “make up” missed kilometres.

## Sitting and daily life

Hip flexor and glute problems are amplified by sitting. Stand and walk 2–3 minutes every 45–60 minutes; do not sit on a wallet; straighten the car seat and avoid prolonged deep hip flexion. Daily changes do not “cure” hip pain, but they reduce continuous load.

## Sleep and pain

Sleep loss raises pain sensitivity and perceived fatigue. During amber, prioritise 7–8 hours; if sleep is short three days in a row, cut training another 20% rather than adding painkillers.

## When to reassess

No improvement after two weeks of reduced load, amber turning red, red flags, or failing every return stage means the load framework is insufficient and clinical assessment is needed. Do not keep adding load to the same strength routine “to see”.

## Hip pain and gait: load management before “form correction”

The relationship between gait and hip pain is not one-way. Overstriding, pelvic drop or knee collapse may suggest insufficient hip abduction control, but forcing a “correction” without reducing load often moves pain from the hip to the knee or shin. Reduce volume, train single-leg stability, then use shorter steps and quiet landing in easy runs; do not change cadence, shoes and volume at once.

Video analysis can inform, not replace clinical assessment. If gait changes come with escalating pain, follow the amber/red bands rather than “finding the feel”.

## Age and recovery

After 40, tendon and muscle recovery slows; the same load may need longer spacing. In amber, put strength on non-consecutive days and allow 48 hours; “I trained like this at 25” is not a volume argument. Age is not an excuse, but the recovery window is a fact.

## Three lines in a pain diary

After each run, write three lines: pain site and 0–10, volume/climb, and whether it settled at 24 hours. Two weeks of entries judge load better than “it feels a bit better”.

## Desk, driving and single-leg stance

Hip load is not only running. Long sitting shortens the hip flexors and lets the glutes switch off; driving keeps one hip flexed. Stand and walk for two to three minutes every 45–60 minutes, then do five standing hip extensions and ten single-leg stands. Do not stretch into pain; the aim is range, not a stretch contest.

Single-leg stance is the cheapest self-check and exercise. Brush teeth, wait for the kettle or read a phone while standing on one leg for 20–30 seconds, keeping the pelvis level and the knee over the second toe. If one side drops, train that side first, but never beyond pain-free range. Falling over costs more than the exercise is worth.

Set the driving seat upright, sit fully into the seat and put a small pillow behind the lower back. Keep the wallet out of the back pocket. At a desk, feet flat, knees slightly below the hips, and use a footrest rather than perching on the toes.

## Stairs and slopes: progressive exposure

In recovery, stairs and slopes often expose the problem first. Go up before down, use the rail before going without, one floor before three. On descents lean slightly forward and take short steps so one leg does not absorb a sudden eccentric load. Walk a gentle slope before running uphill; leave downhill until last.

| Stage | Stairs | Slope | Pass criteria |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Up one floor, holding rail | Walk gentle slope 5 min | No next-day flare |
| 2 | Up three floors, no rail | Run uphill 3 min | Normal gait |
| 3 | Down one floor, rail | Run gentle downhill | 24-hour pain ≤2/10 |
| 4 | Three floors up and down | Slope run 10 min | Stable for a week |

If the pelvis drops, the knee collapses inward or pain rises, go back one stage. Split stair work into several small doses; do not climb 20 floors as a “test”. Stop when pain rises, record the response, and train both sides rather than only the painful one.
