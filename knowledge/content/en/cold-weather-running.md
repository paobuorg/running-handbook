## Definition and scope

Cold-weather running means outdoor running in low temperature, wind, rain, snow or ice. It is for winter and early-morning runners. It applies when you have no heart or breathing condition needing assessment, the route is familiar with exits, and you will shorten, reroute or cancel. One case where it holds: a moderate, lit run with someone aware of your route, where layers and slowing keep exposure acceptable.

## Wind chill, frostbite and hypothermia

The National Weather Service warns that extreme cold with wind creates dangerous wind-chill values; exposure can cause frostbite and hypothermia. Uncovered skin, hands and feet are most prone to frostbite; hypothermia occurs when the body loses heat faster than it produces it. Check wind chill, precipitation, humidity and daylight, not temperature alone; if conditions exceed your kit, indoors or a shorter route is sensible.

## High wind: wind-chill frame and pacing cost

Wind chill and running resistance are different problems. Wind chill describes **heat loss**, and is defined only at 10°C (50°F) or below with wind above 3 mph; the NWS chart marks exposed-skin frostbite in 30, 10 and 5 minutes. At 0°F (about −18°C) with 15 mph wind, wind chill is about −19°F and exposed skin can freeze in roughly 30 minutes; bright sunshine can raise the wind-chill reading by 10–18°F without changing the wind.

Running resistance describes **energy cost**. Pugh’s classic measurements showed that the extra oxygen cost of overcoming air resistance rises with the square of wind speed; at 21.5 km/h (about 5,000 m race pace) air resistance is about **8%** of total energy cost, and about **16%** for a 10-second 100 m. A 2025 study found metabolic rate rises linearly with the square of relative air speed, with different relationships for headwind and tailwind: a 30 km/h headwind adds about **37%** to energy cost, while the same tailwind saves only about **9%**.

| Condition | Main risk | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Wind chill inside the 30-minute frostbite band | Frostbite on exposed skin | Cover face and hands, shorten the route, check skin colour and sensation |
| Headwind section | Pace drops, energy cost rises | Run by RPE, do not chase pace; lean from the ankles and shorten the stride |
| Tailwind section | Early saving, later overconfidence | Do not spend the whole tailwind gain; keep a margin for the return leg |
| Gusts and crosswind | Balance disturbed, wind chill spikes | Avoid open exposed sections and temporary structures; reroute or go indoors |
| Rain or snow plus wind | Wet clothing accelerates heat loss | Prioritise a windproof, waterproof shell; change into dry clothes immediately afterwards |

**There is no universal “cancel the race at X wind speed” threshold (unknown).** Events decide from local warnings, course hazards (trees, signage, coastal embankments) and medical capacity. For an individual, three hard limits work: wind chill inside the 30-minute frostbite band, inability to keep the torso dry, or gusts that already affect walking and balance. Any one of them makes going indoors or postponing the sensible choice.

## Layers, ice and route

Layer rather than wear one thick garment: a wicking base, insulating mid-layer and wind- or water-resistant shell. Gloves, a hat, warm socks and grippy shoes help more than an extra coat; cotton holds water and dries slowly. NPS winter guidance recommends traction on ice and snow, snowshoes in deep snow, checking conditions and turning around when they deteriorate; snow hides markers, so other tracks are unreliable. Urban runners add icy pavement, snow-clearing vehicles and short daylight, so choose familiar, lit routes with exits.

## Navigation and return plan

NPS advises not relying on cell phones: carry a map and compass or GPS and know how to use them. The Ten Essentials list navigation, sun protection, clothing, illumination, first aid, fire, repair kit, food, water and treatment, and shelter; a physical map backs up electronics, and a headlamp needs spare batteries. In practice: paper map or offline track, headlamp with spares, and one person who knows your route and return time.

## Cold air and breathing

Cold air can irritate airways, causing a dry throat, cough or breathing discomfort. A longer warm-up, a buff or mask and lower intensity may help but do not treat asthma. With asthma, a heart condition or marked cold sensitivity, ask a clinician; use "can speak a full sentence" as the ceiling.

## How to: three steps before you leave

1. Check conditions: temperature, wind chill, precipitation, humidity and sunset, then choose route length.
2. Pack by layers: gloves, hat, spare socks, traction, headlamp with spares, map or offline track, water and food.
3. Leave a margin: tell someone your route and return time, set a latest turnaround, and turn back at it.

## Table: adjust the plan

| Condition | Adjustment |
| --- | --- |
| Strong wind, low wind chill | Sheltered route or indoors, shorter |
| Icy surface | Traction, cleared paths |
| Wet snow or rain | Waterproof shell, dry clothes, less time |
| Short daylight | Headlamp, reflective clothing, earlier finish |
| Snow-covered trail | Map or offline track, not footprints |

## Worked example: a 10 km cold run (hypothetical)

All figures are hypothetical: −5 °C, wind 25 km/h, a 10 km route normally run in 60 minutes. At 15 seconds per kilometre extra it takes about 62 minutes 30 seconds; with ice and two checks, budget 70 minutes. Assume a 1.5 litre bottle, 2 kg of clothing and food, and a turnaround 30 minutes before sunset.

## Common misconceptions

- "More clothes is always safer": overdressing causes sweating, and wet base layers accelerate heat loss.
- Watching temperature only: wind and wet clothing change both feel and risk.
- Trusting phone navigation: cold drains batteries and signal may fail.

## When to stop and seek care

Violent shivering, slurred speech, clumsy movement, confusion or unusual drowsiness suggest hypothermia: stop, shelter, change into dry clothes, warm gradually and seek help. White, hard, numb skin may be frostbite; do not rub it or apply direct high heat. Chest pain, severe breathlessness, fainting or altered consciousness are emergencies; call local services.

## Frequency and progression

Arrange cold training by the week: three outdoor runs, with indoor cross-training or rest on other days. After two weeks of adaptation, add one session or a little distance. Progress kit and route, then duration, then intensity, changing one variable at a time.

## Pre-run checklist

- Forecast checked for wind chill, precipitation and sunset.
- Layers, gloves, hat and spare socks ready.
- Traction, headlamp and spare batteries packed.
- Paper map or offline track available, and someone knows your route.

## Scope and limits

This page does not give field treatment for frostbite or hypothermia or judge whether a temperature is safe. Severe frostbite, hypothermia, chest pain, altered consciousness or breathing difficulty are emergencies; call local services.
