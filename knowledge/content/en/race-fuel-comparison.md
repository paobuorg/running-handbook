## Conclusion: which to choose when

The first principle for choosing a fuel type is not "which is most popular" but "which type have you validated in training long runs, and can your race pace and supply conditions carry it". The four types have no universal winner. Gels give precise dosing and light carry, but taste monotonous and demand gut tolerance. Drink mix combines fluid and carbohydrate, depends on aid-station water, and an over-concentrated solution slows gastric emptying. Bars and biscuits offer chewing and satiety, but fibre, fat and dense texture can burden a fast pace. Whole food is familiar in the stomach and common in ultras, but heavy, imprecise per serving and perishable in heat.

Short decision: fast pace, small carrying space, precise carbohydrate dose — gel; heavy sweating with aid stations on course — drink mix; slow pace, need to chew and feel full — solid or whole food; ultras and self-supported racing usually mix several types. Regardless of type, the reference carbohydrate intake is about 30–60 g/h for 1–2.5 h and up to about 90 g/h beyond 2.5 h in ultra-endurance, adjusted to individual tolerance.

| Race condition | First choice | Alternative | Decision point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Road marathon, fast pace, small carry space | Gel | Station drink support | Precise dose, small volume; tolerance must be trained |
| Long distance, heavy sweating, aid stations on course | Drink mix | Gel | One drink covers carbohydrate and fluid; hold 6%–8% |
| Slow ultra pace, need chewing and satiety | Solid bar/biscuit | Whole food | Pick high-carb, low fibre and fat; more stable in cool weather |
| Trail self-support, longer segment stops | Whole food | Solid | Pick familiar daily foods; estimate by weight or count beforehand |
| Gut tolerates concentrated sugar poorly | Drink mix | Whole food | Do not trial a new form on race day |

## What the four types are

- **Gel:** a small sachet of thickened carbohydrate, typically 20–40 g carbohydrate per pack, some with caffeine or electrolytes; eaten straight from the packet and washed down with a few mouthfuls of water.
- **Powder or drink mix:** carbohydrate or carbohydrate-electrolyte powder mixed before or during the race into a 6%–8% solution, putting fluid and carbohydrate in one bottle.
- **Solid (bar, biscuit):** energy bar, rice cake, cereal biscuit and similar, about 20–30 g carbohydrate per serving, often carrying fat, protein and fibre.
- **Whole food:** banana, rice ball, potato, dates, nut pack and everyday foods; carbohydrate density and composition vary widely and must be estimated per item.

In the Costa 2017 two-week gut-challenge trial, both the gel and whole-food forms reduced gastrointestinal symptoms and improved the distance test; gut adaptation transfers across forms. That trial still used repeated exposure, so a form cannot be swapped in for the first time on race day.

## Type-by-type notes

**Gels** win on dose and volume: each pack maps to a fixed carbohydrate amount scheduled every 15–20 or 30–45 minutes. The cost is high sugar concentration, which must be validated repeatedly in training. Caffeinated gels are handled under the caffeine heading; see [Caffeine and running](/en/knowledge/caffeine-and-running/). In hot weather gels thicken and clump, so check packet state before the race.

**Drink mix** merges fluid and carbohydrate replacement, suiting long sessions in heat where aid stations are available. The ISSN line is a 6%–8% carbohydrate-electrolyte solution every 10–15 minutes; above that concentration, gastric emptying and absorption suffer. The downside is that without station water you carry the liquid yourself, and the weight cancels the powder's lightness.

**Bars and biscuits** offer a middle option for runners who need to chew: a feeling of something in the stomach. Bars often carry fat, protein and fibre that can sit heavy in a fast or late-race stomach; check the carbohydrate share on the label and do not treat a meal-replacement bar as an in-race carbohydrate source.

**Whole food** is close to everyday diet and common in ultras and trail races. Its strength is familiarity and psychological acceptance; its weaknesses are imprecise carbohydrate per serving, bulky carry and spoiling in heat. In self-supported events whole food can anchor a long segment, while fast segments still use gel or drink.

## When to choose gel / when to choose drink mix

**Choose a gel if:**

- The race is a road marathon or faster event with limited vest or belt space;
- You need a precise per-hour carbohydrate dose and can schedule it on a timetable;
- You have validated that brand, flavour and dose in at least two long runs without marked reflux, bloating or diarrhoea;
- Station fluid choices are limited and you mainly carry fuel yourself.

**Choose a drink mix if:**

- The event is long and you sweat heavily, wanting one drink to cover both carbohydrate and fluid;
- Aid stations on course provide water or drinks, and the concentration can be held at 6%–8%;
- You tolerate concentrated gels poorly but accept a hypotonic drink;
- A hot long session calls for electrolyte pairing; see [Hydration](/en/knowledge/hydration/) and [Sodium and electrolytes](/en/knowledge/electrolytes-and-sodium/).

## When to choose solid / when to choose whole food

**Choose solid (bar, biscuit) if:**

- Race pace is slow and duration long enough to allow brief chewing pauses;
- You feel nauseated on gels alone and need solid texture and satiety;
- The bar you pick has a high carbohydrate share on the label, low fibre and fat, and you have tried the same item in a long run;
- Weather is cool, with no melting or softening problem.

**Choose whole food if:**

- It is an ultra, trail or self-supported event with longer segment stops;
- The food is part of your regular training diet and your stomach knows it;
- You can estimate carbohydrate beforehand by weight or count, accepting imprecise dosing;
- Conditions are cool, food does not spoil, and carry and storage stay manageable.

## What not to do

1. **Try a new form, brand or flavour for the first time on race day.** Every form must have appeared in at least two training long runs; race day executes the proven combination only.
2. **Change every variable at once.** Do not switch gel brand, solid form and drink concentration in the same race; if something fails you cannot locate the cause.
3. **Eat high-fibre meal-replacement bars in a fast marathon.** Dense, high-fat, high-fibre solids are more likely to sit late in a fast, fatigued run.
4. **Drink only concentrated sugar water without fluid planning.** An over-concentrated solution slows gastric emptying and adds discomfort; fluid and carbohydrate follow sweat rate and the target g/h separately.
5. **Stack an untested caffeine dose on race day.** The commonly effective studied range is 3–6 mg/kg, with more side effects at very high doses; individual sleep and anxiety responses vary widely.
6. **Treat fuel type as the performance answer.** Form choice cannot replace aerobic fitness, pacing and long-run buildup; fuel solves substrate and tolerance, not inadequate training.

## Worked examples: combinations by race duration (assumed)

**Example 1: road marathon targeting 3:30 finish.** Assume one gel every 30–45 minutes with station water; about 50–60 g carbohydrate per hour. Two plain gels in the first half, one caffeinated gel in the latter half (caffeine validated in training). Solid and whole food are reserved for post-race recovery, not relied on in the race.

**Example 2: about 6 h ultra or trail.** First two hours: gel plus station drink at 60 g/h. Mid-section: drink mix for fluid and carbohydrate, half a banana or a rice cake. Late section: return to gel or whole food according to gut response, total toward 60–90 g/h. Every segment option comes from something validated in training; pre-race planning lists available station drink flavours and concentrations.

Both examples are editorial assumptions; actual g/h depends on sweat rate, pace, altitude and individual tolerance, and the same combination should be rehearsed in a pre-race long run.

## Applicable boundaries

This page is endurance nutrition education, not a medical diet plan. Carbohydrate g/h ranges come from position stands and guidance; individual tolerance, energy needs and digestive conditions differ. People with diabetes, food allergy, gastrointestinal disease or regular medication should consult a clinician or registered sports dietitian first. Brand ingredients, caffeine content and allergens vary widely; this page does not recommend specific products, and label information follows the official packaging. Form comparison cannot replace the stepped adaptation described in [Gut training for races](/en/knowledge/gut-training-for-races/).

## What would change this conclusion

- **Event duration and pace:** 10K/half, marathon, ultra and self-supported trail differ in carbohydrate target, pause space and carry conditions.
- **Environment:** heat and humidity make solids and whole food harder to carry and warm drinks faster; gels turn cloying. In cold weather liquid cools quickly and solids keep better.
- **Personal gut tolerance:** some only tolerate drink mix, others need a bar; tolerance is built in training, not re-tested on race day.
- **Supply conditions:** station density, water access and bag-drop availability determine whether drink mix and whole food are feasible.
- **Evidence update:** multi-sugar combinations, gut adaptation and form-specific performance remain active research; this page adjusts if later high-quality reviews differ.
