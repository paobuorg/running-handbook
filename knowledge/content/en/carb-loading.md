## What this page covers

Carb loading is a deliberate increase in carbohydrate intake before a race to store more glycogen in muscle and liver. It targets **sustained or intermittent exercise longer than 90 minutes**. The 2016 joint position statement from ACSM, AND and DC gives the protocol as 10–12 grams of carbohydrate per kilogram of body mass per day for the 36–48 hours before exercise.

It does **not** apply to a 5K, a 10K, most half marathons or everyday training. For moderate-intensity running lasting 60–90 minutes, the review states there appears to be no advantage to raising pre-exercise muscle glycogen, because a significant amount remains in the muscle afterwards. Treating "eat more carbs" as a rule for every race usually adds bloating, gut discomfort and extra water weight instead of performance.

## The evidence: dose, window and effect

| Question | Evidence | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| How much | ACSM 2016 joint position statement: 10–12 g/kg per day for the 36–48 hours before exercise longer than 90 minutes | About 700–840 grams per day for a 70 kg runner |
| What it buys | Beck 2015 review: fatigue delayed by about 20%, performance improved by about 2%–3% | A modest to moderate benefit, not a guaranteed personal best |
| Must glycogen be depleted first | Early protocols used 3 days of hard training with low carbohydrate, then 3 days of high carbohydrate; later work showed the depletion phase was unnecessary and 24 hours may be enough | No need to endure a depletion phase |
| Is loading needed for 60–90 minutes | Review: no clear advantage, and glycogen remains in the muscle after exercise | Most races shorter than a half marathon do not need it |
| What about events under 90 minutes | Review: 7–12 g/kg during the 24 hours before exercise | This is closer to a normal high-carbohydrate diet than to loading |
| What to eat in the last hours | Review: 1–4 g/kg in the 1–4 hours before exercise lasting more than 60 minutes | A normal meal or snack, not a loading dose |

The "about 20%" and "2%–3%" figures are estimates summarised by a review, not promises that apply to every runner. Populations, events and comparison conditions differ, so the percentages should not be transferred directly to your next race.

## How to arrange the 36–48 hours

| Timing | Priority | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| 3 days out | Eat normally and start raising the share of carbohydrate | Do not increase training at the same time |
| About 48 hours out | Enter the 10–12 g/kg range | Favour low-fibre staples such as rice, pasta, potatoes and bread |
| About 36 hours out | Keep carbohydrate high and reduce high-fibre, high-fat and spicy foods | This is not the time to try new foods |
| 12–24 hours out | Most people are still in the high-carbohydrate range | Drink to thirst rather than forcing fluid |
| 1–4 hours out | A meal or snack providing 1–4 g/kg | Keep it familiar and avoid feeling overly full |
| During the race | Continue hourly carbohydrate intake, as covered on the race-day fueling page | Loading does not remove the need to fuel during the event |

"Three days out" is not an exact countdown. Some runners do well starting 48 hours before, others need 36 hours; the position statement gives a 36–48 hour range, not an hour-by-hour prescription. Total intake, food familiarity and gut tolerance matter more than hitting a precise clock time.

## A worked example for a 70 kg runner (hypothetical)

Example runner: 70 kg, preparing for a marathon lasting more than 4 hours, no gastrointestinal disease, usually eats four meals a day.

| Item | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Daily lower bound | 70 × 10 | 700 g/day |
| Daily upper bound | 70 × 12 | 840 g/day |
| 36-hour total, lower | 700 × 1.5 | 1,050 g |
| 36-hour total, upper | 840 × 1.5 | 1,260 g |
| 48-hour total, lower | 700 × 2 | 1,400 g |
| 48-hour total, upper | 840 × 2 | 1,680 g |
| Average over four meals, lower | 700 ÷ 4 | 175 g per meal |
| Average over four meals, upper | 840 ÷ 4 | 210 g per meal |

These are arithmetic results only. A 175–210 gram carbohydrate meal is large for many people. In practice, runners often split the total across five or six feedings and use low-fibre staples, juice, sports drinks and easy-to-digest carbohydrate to close the gap. **Do not force food to hit a number on the last day.** Liquid carbohydrate is a common alternative when appetite fails, but it also needs to be practised.

## Why more is not better

The gut has a ceiling on carbohydrate absorption. The review describes glucose being absorbed through SGLT1, which becomes saturated at an intake of about 1 gram per minute; simultaneous fructose ingestion through GLUT5 raises exogenous carbohydrate oxidation to about 1.3 grams per minute, with performance benefits becoming apparent in the third hour of exercise.

That explains two common racing rules:

- For events longer than 2.5 hours, up to about 90 grams per hour, from **multiple transportable carbohydrates** such as glucose plus fructose;
- For events lasting 2–3 hours, about 60 grams per hour, from single or multiple sources.

The same logic applies to loading. "12 g/kg per day" does not mean "12 g/kg in one meal". Carbohydrate concentrated into a few large meals beyond absorption speed does not become extra glycogen; it becomes gut discomfort. The position statement also stresses that a registered sports dietitian should individualise the plan rather than copy generic numbers.

## How loading connects to race-day fueling

| Phase | Goal | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| 36–48 hours before | Raise glycogen stores | Adding hard training at the same time, or eating large amounts of high-fibre food |
| 1–4 hours before | 1–4 g/kg of a familiar meal | Trying an untested food |
| During the race | Hourly carbohydrate, with multiple sources for long events | Skipping race fuel because "loading is done" |
| After the race | Return to normal eating with carbohydrate and protein | Continuing the loading protocol for days |

Loading does not replace race fueling. You still use glycogen, lose fluid and electrolytes, and face gut stress during the event. Treating loading as "the tank is full, so I can stop fueling" is a common misjudgement in long races.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Every race needs loading"** — only events longer than 90 minutes have a clear rationale; 5K, 10K and most half marathons do not.
- **"Loading means eating more pasta"** — total carbohydrate grams matter, not one specific food; protein and fat cannot substitute for carbohydrate.
- **"More is always better"** — absorption has a speed limit, and excess does not become extra glycogen.
- **"You must empty glycogen first"** — the early depletion protocol was simplified by later research; the depletion phase is unnecessary.
- **"You can train hard while loading"** — high carbohydrate plus high training load tends to leave you carrying fatigue into the race.
- **"Loading guarantees no wall"** — the wall also involves pacing, dehydration, race tactics and individual differences; glycogen is one part.
- **"Force food down if you cannot eat"** — adjust food form and timing instead of forcing volume; persistent vomiting or severe abdominal pain needs medical assessment.

## Who should be more cautious

- People with diabetes, insulin resistance or other conditions requiring medical nutrition management should adjust carbohydrate amount and timing with a doctor or registered dietitian.
- People with a history of eating disorders, or a strong sense of losing control around food and body weight, should not self-prescribe a high-carbohydrate protocol.
- People with irritable bowel syndrome, chronic diarrhoea, gastro-oesophageal reflux or previous severe race-day gut problems should rehearse the plan on a smaller scale first.
- Pregnant and postpartum runners, adolescents and people actively losing weight should not apply athlete loading doses.
- Persistent vomiting, severe abdominal pain, inability to eat, marked bloating with breathing difficulty, fainting or chest pain should be treated as an emergency; do not continue the race.

This page is nutrition education. It is not a personal dietary prescription and does not diagnose or treat any condition. Every dose needs individualisation for body mass, race duration, tolerance and medical history.

## Checklist

- [ ] Is my race actually longer than 90 minutes?
- [ ] Do I know the daily gram range for my body mass?
- [ ] Have I split the total across several meals rather than one or two?
- [ ] Have I reduced high-fibre, high-fat and spicy foods in the final 24 hours?
- [ ] Have I practised this food and these portions in training?
- [ ] Am I still planning hourly carbohydrate during the race?
- [ ] Have I avoided combining race-week hard training with loading?

## Split intake and review

The 36–48 hour window is not one huge meal; split the total into meals and snacks **every 3–4 hours**, favouring low-fibre, low-fat staples you already tolerate. Cramming it in raises bloating and diarrhoea risk without filling glycogen any faster.

Test the same foods and timing in one long run the week before; review gut symptoms after the race; and review in the same order every race so you can tell whether the problem is the food, the timing or the intensity.

## Sources

- ACSM, AND and DC, Nutrition and Athletic Performance (joint position statement, 2016): events longer than 90 minutes, 36–48 hours before, 10–12 g/kg per day; 30–60 g/h for 1–2.5 hours and up to 90 g/h beyond 2.5 hours; individualised advice.
- Beck et al., Role of nutrition in performance enhancement and postexercise recovery (review, 2015): loading for events longer than 90 minutes; fatigue delayed by about 20% and performance improved by 2%–3%; depletion phase unnecessary and 24 hours possibly sufficient; no clear advantage for 60–90 minutes; 7–12 g/kg in the 24 hours before shorter events; 1–4 g/kg in the 1–4 hours before exercise longer than 60 minutes; SGLT1 saturation near 1 g/min and about 1.3 g/min with fructose; 90 g/h requiring multiple carbohydrate sources.
- ISSN, Nutrient timing position stand (2017): about 30–60 g/h during prolonged high-intensity exercise, adjusted to the individual and the event.
