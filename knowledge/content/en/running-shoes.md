## Bottom line

Running shoes are a **trade-off system**, not a product review: fit comes first, and only then cushioning feel, drop, support, weight, surface match and training type. This page does not recommend brands, and it does not claim any shoe can treat an injury. Three judgments come first. First, complete the length, width and heel-lock fit checks in the shop before comparing any spec. Second, arch labels and strike pattern are only a starting filter: the controlled evidence does not support prescribing a shoe type by foot shape to reduce injury. Third, shoes are consumables: retire them when the midsole collapses, the outsole wears through or containment fades, not by a fixed mileage that supposedly fits everyone.

The evidence boundary matters here. Many footwear conclusions come from observational studies, single-brand cohorts and manufacturer guidance; true randomized trials are rare. Where this site cannot verify a primary number, the text says “evidence insufficient” or “no official value given” instead of inventing a threshold. The site does not diagnose foot conditions. Persistent pain, obvious structural problems, diabetes or reduced foot sensation should be assessed by a qualified medical or podiatry professional first.

## Definition and prerequisites

The running shoes discussed here are footwear designed for walking, jogging, road training and trail movement. The selection process includes fit judgment, spec trade-offs, surface matching, rotation in use and retirement assessment. This page is for runners without an acute foot injury, open wound or clinician-imposed load restriction. It assumes you use comfort, stability and fit while walking, jogging and a small side-step as your main criteria, rather than price, brand, midsole stack or someone else's recommendation.

If you already have persistent foot pain, numbness, clear structural abnormality, diabetes or peripheral circulation problems, seek a clinician or podiatrist before choosing footwear; changing shoes is not treatment. Details on everyday fit and cushioning feel are in [Shoe fit and cushioning: comfort before technology](/en/knowledge/shoe-fit-and-cushioning/).

## Fit: length, width and heel lock first

Fit is the prerequisite for every other parameter. The athletic-footwear advice from Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust lists several checks you can make in the shop: the foot stretches about 5 mm from standing to walking, so leave room; leave about one finger's width (about 1 cm) between the longest toe and the toe box; laces or straps fasten across the arch while leaving forefoot room; look for six or more eyelets to adjust the fit; the sole should be wider than the ball of the foot; heel lining pads help lock the foot in place while leaving toe space.

Timing matters too: feet swell later in the day and after exercise, so late-afternoon fitting is closer to running state. Wear your usual running socks; thick or thin socks change the judgment. Three motion checks: walk normally, jog to the ground reaction, and take a small side-step. If any of these produces toe impact, chafing, heel lift or instep pressure, change size or last instead of relying on “it will break in”. Sizing is not uniform across brands; do not go by the box number alone.

## Cushioning, drop and weight: linked trade-offs

These parameters are usually advertised separately, but they constrain one another inside the same shoe and should be read together.

- **Cushioning feel:** midsole material, thickness and deformation determine softness on landing. A secondary analysis of a trial in 527 recreational runners found that higher perceived heel cushioning and higher overall shoe preference were associated with lower injury risk; this is an association, not causation, and the study had a commercial funding background. It does not follow that “softer shoes prevent injury”.
- **Heel-to-toe drop:** the height difference between heel and forefoot shifts load among Achilles tendon, calf and knee. A low drop reduces heel stack and increases calf and Achilles demand; a high drop does the opposite. There is no single “most natural” drop; moving abruptly from high to low drop often brings calf and Achilles discomfort in the first two weeks.
- **Weight:** a lighter shoe costs less to lift each stride, but lightness usually trades against durability, protection and cushioning. The relationship between shoe mass and metabolic cost appears in the plated-shoe review as an estimate of about 1% per extra 100 g; that estimate comes from pooled experiments, not a personal pace guarantee.
- **Support and containment:** support structures are usually firmer and heavier; containment comes from last, lacing system and heel lock, not from a firm arch press. If the shoe pushes on the arch, alters your landing path or brings new knee/ankle discomfort, it is not suitable for you.

Do not rank by one parameter. A very soft but heavy, loosely contained shoe may cost more than an ordinary-spec but stable, well-fitting one.

## Foot type and shoe type: how strong is the label evidence?

“Low arch = stability shoe, high arch = cushioning shoe” is the most common retail script. As a medical fitting rule, the existing evidence does not support it. This site checked three studies.

| Evidence | Sample and design | What it shows | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | |
| Arch-based prescription vs uniform stable shoe (Knapik 2014) | 3 randomized prospective studies, 7,203 recruits | Injury rates differed little between groups; fitting by foot morphology did not reduce basic-training injuries | Military basic-training population; generalization to recreational runners needs care |
| Uniform neutral shoes followed for 1 year (Nielsen 2014) | 927 novices, 1,854 feet, 326,803 km | Pronators were not injured more often; injury incidence per 1,000 km was 0.37 lower than in neutral feet | Novice neutral-shoe cohort; whether high pronation raises risk still needs study |
| Foot posture and lower-limb overuse review (Neal 2014) | 21 studies, 6,228 participants | Pronated feet had a stronger but very small association with medial tibial stress syndrome (SMD 0.28); patellofemoral pain 0.33 in the same review; no association with foot/ankle injury, bone stress or non-specific overuse injury | Association does not prove shoe type works; static foot posture belongs only inside a multifactorial assessment |

A decision table follows instead of ordering shoes by “arch type”:

| Your situation | Consider a support/stability shoe | Do not change shoes for a label | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| History of MTSS with clearly pronating posture | Yes, as part of a multifactorial assessment; try stability models to compare | — | Small association; footwear is one factor (Neal 2014) |
| No injury, but told “low arch” or “high arch” | — | Yes; choose by fit, comfort and surface | Arch-based prescription did not show injury reduction (Knapik 2014; Nielsen 2014) |
| High arch, wanting more cushioning | A well-cushioned shoe is fine | No need to seek an “high-arch dedicated” model | This site found no controlled evidence for dedicated models (evidence insufficient) |
| The shoe presses the inside or feels awkward in gait | Change shoe or size | Do not force insole “correction” to an uncomfortable fit | Over-correction changes loading, but public research gives no quantifiable injury-rate difference (evidence insufficient) |

The same clarification applies to running form: **strike pattern does not decide shoe type**. Forefoot, heel or midfoot strike is mostly a running performance; the evidence does not support assigning a shoe category by strike pattern. The relationship between strike mode and injury is reviewed separately in [Forefoot and heel strike: distribution, injury evidence and the cost of changing form](/en/knowledge/foot-strike-pattern/). The correct use of posture labels is a first filter: if a specific recurring injury accompanies them, tell the clinician, physiotherapist or fitter, and judge it together with load, surface and symptoms.

## Surface and training type: road, trail and race day

Different surfaces call for different shoe properties; one shoe covering every scene usually pays a compromise price.

| Scene | Priorities | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| City road and park paths | Fit, stability, comfortable cushioning | No need to pay for “strongest cushioning”; price and fit are not the same thing (NHS: try at a running shop, expensive shoes are not essential) |
| Gravel and mud trail | Grip lugs, toe protection, drainage, slip resistance | Weight and road feel decrease; lug depth, drop and plate trade-offs are in [Trail gear](/en/knowledge/trail-gear/) |
| Wet technical trail | Sticky rubber, lug pattern, ankle stability | Shoes cannot replace route judgment and slip training |
| Long training runs | Durability, stable containment, slow midsole breakdown | Reassess once worn |
| Race day | Familiar, broken in, no blisters | Never try on a new shoe in the race |

Trail lug depth, drop, plate and waterproofing are product logic: firm dirt and gravel suit shallow lugs, soft mud suits deep lugs; drop ranges come from manufacturer and retail guidance. **No controlled trial proves a given lug depth or drop reduces injury (evidence insufficient).** The relationship between surface choice and adaptation is in [Surface choice: no absolute best, only an adaptation process](/en/knowledge/running-surface/).

## Plated and advanced shoes: benefits, costs and evidence limits

Advanced footwear usually pairs high-energy-return foam with a full-length carbon plate that raises longitudinal bending stiffness. A systematic review and meta-analysis of crossover trials reported that plated shoes were associated with lower metabolic demand; pooled reductions across running economy, metabolic cost, oxygen consumption and energetic cost of transport averaged about 2.75% (range −0.99% to −4.47%). Evidence certainty was moderate for running economy, metabolic cost and oxygen consumption, and low for energetic cost of transport. The same review states that the plate's contribution cannot be separated from high-return foam, rocker geometry and shoe mass; studies were mostly laboratory crossover trials, and injury outcomes were not assessed. Another biomechanics systematic review and meta-analysis reported a borderline-significant drop in ankle power (SMD −0.71; 95% CI −1.42 to 0.00; p = 0.05) and a small non-significant decrease in cadence (SMD −0.16; 95% CI −0.32 to 0.01; p = 0.06), with limited certainty; biomechanical differences do not translate directly into performance gains.

On “do elite and recreational runners agree”, state it side by side: review samples mixed recreational and high-level runners; existing data cannot give a personal saving ratio by body mass or level. Classic team-sport workload–injury data come from elite cohorts and cannot be moved directly onto recreational runners. On the benefit side, the same pace may feel easier; on the cost side, the shoes are usually more expensive, durability and everyday comfort are uncertain, the last is aggressive, and there is no evidence they prevent injury. Whether to use them in training or on race day should be decided after a broken-in fit, not as a substitute for everyday trainers. Mechanisms and limits are in [Plated and advanced shoes: evidence, use cases and boundaries](/en/knowledge/shoe-technology/).

## Decision-branch table: if → then

| Condition (if) | Action (then) | Evidence boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No toe room, instep pressure or heel lift on trial | Change size or last first; skip spec comparison | Fit first; GSTT athletic-footwear sizing checks |
| No injury, only labeled “low/high arch” | Choose by fit, comfort and surface; do not pick stability by label | Arch fitting did not show injury reduction (Knapik 2014; Nielsen 2014) |
| Recurrent MTSS or knee pain with clear pronation | Hand symptoms and posture to a professional for multifactorial assessment; compare stability models | Small association; footwear is one link (Neal 2014) |
| Mostly road training, occasional greenway | Road shoes suffice; no need for a heavy, stiff trail shoe | Switch by surface; trail product logic in On/Runners Need guides |
| Entering a race with technical terrain | Choose trail shoes against the event mandatory list and route; break in before the course | Mandatory-kit definition in [Trail gear](/en/knowledge/trail-gear/) |
| Wanting plated shoes | Build a stable base first; consider them for races or tempo efforts; do not rely on them for everyday long runs | Metabolic benefit is a group-level association; no injury-prevention evidence (systematic review) |
| Shoe visibly collapsed, worn through or lost containment | Retire and replace; do not use fixed mileage alone | NHS lists worn, unsupportive shoes as a possible heel-pain factor |

## Frequency, mileage life and retirement criteria

There is no single retirement mileage that fits everyone. NHS lists “unsupportive or worn shoes” as one possible heel-pain factor; public guidance gives no uniform kilometre retirement line (no official value given). Observable retirement criteria include: the midsole visibly collapses under pressure or walking and returns slowly; the outsole pattern is flat, worn through locally or skewed; the upper loses containment and the toe box or heel deforms; the familiar motion becomes uncomfortable, or new pressure or pain appears.

Rotation is a common way to extend usable life and spread load: alternate two well-fitting pairs so the midsole material recovers between uses. Recording purchase date, use and rough mileage is more reliable than impression; the [shoe mileage tool](/en/tools/shoe-mileage/) can keep that log. Body mass, surface, weather and shoe structure all affect life; heavier runners and those on hard roads or trails usually age shoes faster.

## Worked example: two rotated pairs and retirement check (teaching assumption)

Assume a runner trains 3 times weekly, 8 km each session, for a weekly total of 24 km; two fitting road shoes rotate, each pair about 12 km per week.

- Mileage per pair per week: 24 ÷ 2 = 12 km/pair.
- Over 13 weeks (about 3 months): 12 × 13 = 156 km/pair; first wear check at this point.
- Over 26 weeks (about 6 months): 12 × 26 = 312 km/pair; the two pairs together cover 24 × 26 = 624 km.
- Retirement judgment: at the 13-week check, if one midsole has clearly collapsed or the outsole is worn through, replace early; the structurally sound pair continues to the next check.

All numbers above are a teaching assumption showing the “record usage, retire by state” method, not a uniform replacement line. Real life depends on wear state and comfort.

## Failure modes and early signals

- **Specs before feet:** early signal is comparing cushioning and weight in the shop, then getting toe impact, heel chafing or instep pressure at home. Rule: complete length, width and lock checks first, then compare specs.
- **Ordering a support shoe by arch label:** early signal is an arch press, altered landing path or new calf/knee discomfort. Rule: labels are a first filter; no injury means no label-driven change.
- **Wearing new shoes on race day:** early signal is the first long-distance break-in right before the event, raising blister and black-toenail risk. Rule: race shoes go through several training sessions first; see [Black toenails: causes, prevention and no self-drainage](/en/knowledge/black-toenails/).
- **Keeping shoes past midsole collapse:** early signal is a dull landing, worsening local wear, and more calf or heel effort at an familiar pace. Rule: check against retirement criteria instead of “still wearable”.
- **Using plated shoes as everyday trainers:** early signal is depending on an aggressive last for easy runs, with weaker adaptation of calves and feet. Rule: plated shoes serve specific scenarios; base training stays in stable, well-fitting shoes.

## Common misconceptions

- **“More expensive is better.”** Price may buy durability or lightness, not a match for your foot and surface; NHS also notes you do not need to spend a lot on shoes.
- **“Softer cushioning means more protection.”** Subjective cushioning and injury risk are associated, not causal; too soft can also be unstable.
- **“One shoe for every scene.”** Road and trail shoes differ in grip, protection and weight; switching by surface is more reasonable.
- **“Fixed mileage replacement is enough.”** There is no universal mileage; wear state and comfort change matter more.
- **“Form decides the shoe.”** Strike pattern does not constitute a forced fitting basis; the cost of changing form is reviewed in the running-form page.

## Pain, risk and medical boundary

NHS advises not to keep running through pain; seek medical or physiotherapy advice if pain is severe, persistent or swollen. Persistent foot pain, structural problems, diabetes, abnormal sensation or recurring specific injuries should be assessed by a qualified medical or podiatry professional; changing shoes is not treatment, and insoles or orthoses should follow professional assessment. This site does not make a medical diagnosis or judge whether a shoe can correct alignment or cure injury.

## Scope and limits

This page does not recommend brands, models or purchase channels, and does not judge whether a given shoe suits your foot type, injury history or race goal. Evidence linking foot type, running form and injury is mostly small-effect observational work; manufacturer and retail guidance is product logic, not clinical evidence. Persistent pain, structural foot problems, diabetes or peripheral nerve/sensory issues should be referred to qualified medical or podiatry professionals; minimum trail kit is in [Trail gear](/en/knowledge/trail-gear/).

## What would change this advice

- **Surface and environment:** moving to trails, hard treadmill decks or winter wet snow changes grip, protection and waterproofing needs.
- **Training phase:** novice, preparation and taper periods differ in durability, break-in and race attributes; do not try new shoes on race day.
- **Body state:** after injury recovery, weight change or foot structural change, repeat the fit judgment.
- **Evidence updates:** research on arch fitting, plated-shoe injury outcomes and shoe mass–metabolic relationships is still moving; the page is revised when high-quality reviews or guidelines shift the position.
- **Equipment rules:** for races with mandatory kit, the event official rules prevail; shoes are only one item.
