## Definition and who this applies to

Running-shoe choice means selecting and managing shoes by foot shape, use, surface and comfort. It applies to people without an acute foot injury, open wound or medical instruction to limit weight-bearing; diabetes, marked structural problems or persistent pain need a clinician or podiatrist first. It holds when comfort, stability and fit during walking, easy jogging and direction changes decide the choice, rather than price or specifications. The NHS suggests a running-shop fitting; expensive shoes are not essential.

## Fit first, technology second

The first test is fit: room in front of the toes, no pressure across the top of the foot, and no heel sliding while walking or jogging. Feet swell later in the day and after activity, so try shoes with some margin. Width, arch support and lacing affect fit.

Expensive shoes may mainly be more durable or lighter, and beginners may not need them.

## Cushioning is a perception, not a leaderboard

A secondary analysis of a randomised trial involving 527 recreational runners found that moderate or high perceived heel cushioning, and higher overall shoe appreciation, were associated with lower injury risk. These are associations, not proof that cushioning prevents injury.

Choose by comfort, stability and fit during walking, easy jogging and direction changes, not by midsole thickness or material names.

## Arch and shoe type: labels are weaker than the evidence

“Low arch, buy stability; high arch, buy cushioning” is the most common shoe-shop rule, but as a medical rule the evidence is thin.

| Evidence | Sample and design | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Shoes assigned by arch height vs one stability shoe for all | 3 randomised prospective trials, 7,203 recruits | Injury rates differed little; assigning by plantar shape did not reduce injury (Knapik 2014) |
| One-year follow-up in neutral shoes | 927 novice runners, 1,854 feet, 326,803 km | Pronators were not at higher risk; their injury rate per 1,000 km was 0.37 lower (Nielsen 2014) |
| Foot posture and lower-limb overuse injury | 21 studies, 6,228 people | Strong but small association between pronation and medial tibial stress syndrome (pooled SMD 0.28); the same review reported 0.33 for patellofemoral pain; no association with foot/ankle injury, bone stress reactions or non-specific overuse injury (Neal 2014) |

Use a decision table rather than ordering by arch label:

| Your situation | Consider a stability shoe | Do not change shoes for the label alone | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| History of MTSS with visibly pronated feet | Yes, as one part of a multifactorial assessment; trial both | — | The association is small; shoes are one factor (Neal 2014) |
| No injury, merely told you have “low arches” | — | Yes; start with fit and comfort | Arch-based assignment did not reduce injury (Knapik 2014) |
| High arch and wanting more cushioning | A cushioned shoe is reasonable | No need for an “arch-specific” model | No controlled evidence for a dedicated shoe type (unknown) |
| Medial pressure or awkward gait in the shoe | Change model or size | Do not “correct” into discomfort with insoles | Over-correction changes load, but no study quantifies its injury rate (unknown) |

**How large the risk of “over-correction” is remains unknown.** The direction is plausible: stability shoes are often firmer and heavier, and if a shoe feels as if it is pushing under your arch, changes your landing path or creates new knee or ankle symptoms, it is the wrong shoe. Treat arch labels as a starting filter; fit, comfort, symptoms and load management decide.

## Road shoes, trail shoes and surface

Road shoes prioritise comfort and stability on hard surfaces. Trail running adds mud, rock, wet ground and hiking sections; grip, protection and drainage usually cost weight and road feel. Do not treat a road shoe as a universal trail shoe, or rely on the outsole alone on technical terrain.

## How to do it

1. Measure, then try on: assume a late-afternoon fitting in running socks, with about a thumb's width in front of the toes; walk and jog to confirm no heel slip and no pressure.
2. Test walk, easy jog and a small direction change; judge cushioning, stability and flex. Change size or model if one causes pressure, rubbing or instability.
3. Record use and wear: assume three 8-kilometre runs weekly, 24 kilometres total, rotated between two pairs at about 12 kilometres each; check regularly for a collapsed midsole, worn outsole or lost upper hold.

## Shoes by situation

| Situation | Priority | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| City roads and parks | Fit, stability, comfortable cushioning | Paying extra for “maximum cushioning” |
| Gravel and muddy trails | Grip, protection, drainage | Added weight, reduced road feel |
| Wet technical trails | Outsole grip, ankle protection | A shoe cannot replace route judgement |
| Long training runs | Durability, stable hold | Assess replacement when worn |

Models need fitting on the relevant surface.

## Frequency, cycle and replacement

No fixed mileage applies to everyone. The NHS lists unsupportive or worn-out shoes among possible contributors to heel pain. Consider replacing when the midsole feels collapsed, the outsole is worn through, the upper no longer holds the foot, or a familiar shoe makes familiar movements uncomfortable.

For usage tracking, assume two rotated pairs, about 12 kilometres each weekly, 600 kilometres over six months and about 300 kilometres per pair. That is an example, not a universal replacement line. A regular wear check is more useful than blind mileage.

## Worked example (assuming 24 kilometres weekly)

Assume a runner does three 8-kilometre runs weekly, 24 kilometres total, rotating two road shoes at about 12 kilometres each weekly. Assume six months gives about 600 kilometres, or about 300 kilometres per pair. At the third-month check, replace a pair early if its midsole has collapsed or the outsole is worn through; keep the other pair if its structure holds.

These numbers are assumptions showing use tracking and condition-based replacement.

## Common misconceptions

- “More expensive is better.” A higher price may buy durability or lightness, not a better match.
- “Softer cushioning protects more.” Cushioning and injury risk are associated, not causal; very soft shoes can also feel unstable.
- “A fixed mileage tells you when to replace.” No single mileage applies; wear and comfort changes matter more.

## Pain, risk and seeking care

The NHS advises not running while in pain and seeking medical or physiotherapy advice when pain is severe, persistent or accompanied by swelling. Persistent foot pain, structural problems, diabetes or altered sensation deserve a clinician or podiatrist; changing shoes is not treatment.

On trails, carry the NPS minimum kit: navigation, sun protection, clothing, illumination, first aid, fire, repair kit, food, water and water-treatment supplies, and shelter. Navigation means a map, compass and GPS, with a physical map backing up electronic tools. A headlamp with spare batteries is recommended, and someone should know the route and expected return time.

## Self-check list

- [ ] Toes have room, the top of the foot is not pressed, the heel does not slide
- [ ] Walking, easy jogging and direction changes feel comfortable and stable
- [ ] Purchase date, use and regular wear checks are recorded
- [ ] Road and trail runs use appropriate shoes rather than one pair for everything
- [ ] Trail runs carry map, compass, headlamp with spare batteries, first aid and layers

## Scope and limits

This page does not recommend brands or decide whether a shoe suits your foot shape, injury history or race goal. Persistent pain or diabetes deserves clinical or podiatry assessment.
