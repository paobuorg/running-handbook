## Definition and preconditions

Trail navigation is confirming position, direction and distance with map, track, waypoints and terrain reading where blazes are sparse and coverage may be missing. It applies to trail runners and hikers on unfamiliar routes. It holds only with trustworthy route information, a battery-independent backup, and a contact who knows the route and return time; otherwise shorten it.

## Plan, track, waypoint and GPX

A trip plan lists route, companions, times and a Plan B. A track is a time-ordered coordinate series; a route an ordered turning-point sequence; a waypoint one coordinate. GPX is an XML schema for exchanging GPS data; GPX 1.1 defines these structures with latitude, longitude and optional elevation.

## Navigation layers and battery budget

Phones fail from cold, falls, water or battery drain; GPS can be unreliable, so the NPS advises alternatives.

| Layer | Job | Failure | Backup |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Phone map | Position | Battery, water | Paper map |
| GPS watch | Heading | Weak signal | Not sole source |
| Paper map | No-power navigation | Rain | Waterproof bag |

Budget battery in hours. On a 3 hour 30 minute plan, airplane mode and intermittent fixes might take a full phone to about half; continuous recording might leave 20%. Conserving battery matters; consider a locator beacon.

## Off route: stop, locate, backtrack

Stop first; do not search on the move. Confirm position against the last certain point, judge the error from map and terrain, then walk back before choosing again. Do not cross scree or water. If daylight or energy fails, retreat. Past the return time, the question is risk, not navigation.

## Worked example: a 20 km loop and the half-water rule

Assume a 20 km loop with 600 m of climb, a 3 hour 30 minute plan and 1.5 litres of water, with decision points at 5 km, 10 km and 15 km.

The NPS rule is to turn back before half the water is gone. Assume 0.7 litres remain at the 10 km ridge. Returning 10 km takes at least 2 hours; continuing takes similar time with unknown weather, so retreat. With 0.9 litres and 10 minutes ahead, continue, treating 15 km as a checkpoint. These numbers are hypothetical.

## How to do it

1. Load the GPX offline, mark junctions and decision points, and leave route and times with a trusted contact.
2. Budget battery hourly; give phone, watch and headlamp separate power; carry whistle, first aid and layers.
3. At each decision point check position, water and time; if water is below half or time past return, retreat and call your contact.

## Paper, phone and watch: what each one is for

The three tools fail differently, which is why the minimum kit lists them together. Paper does not need power and can be read in rain if it is in a case, but it cannot tell you where you are. A phone gives a position and a map, but cold drains the battery and a cracked screen ends the session. A watch is convenient and waterproof, yet its small screen and limited battery make it a poor primary map. Use the paper map for the plan and the decision points, the phone for the position fix, and the watch for distance and elapsed time; when they disagree, trust the map and your own observation of the terrain.

A GPX file is a track: an ordered set of points with latitude, longitude and optional elevation. It shows where a route goes, not whether the path is open, whether a bridge is out or whether the descent is exposed. Before loading it, read the route description and the weather, and mark the points where you would turn around if you were late. That turns a line on a screen into a decision plan.

## Night, fog and poor visibility

Navigation difficulty rises sharply when you cannot see the next marker. In fog or darkness, shorten your decision cycle: check position more often, reduce the distance between confirmed points, and slow enough that you can stop within the distance you can see. If the route depends on cairns or painted markers, a missed marker can put you on a parallel path that looks correct for hundreds of metres. When that happens, stop, return to the last certain point, and re-enter the route there rather than improvising a shortcut.

## Common misconceptions

- A GPX is access permission. It has no closures, broken bridges, snow depth or rescue access, and may be wrongly recorded.
- A phone is enough. GPS can be unreliable and cold can kill the battery; without paper, the percentage is the only margin.

## When to stop, turn back or call for help

Stop when water is below half, return time has passed, lightning or flood risk arrives, someone is injured, or visibility fails. Move to safety, then backtrack or use the escape route. Call for help if you cannot move safely, awareness changes, fracture or head impact is suspected, or you cannot reach safety before dark. Give location, people, injuries and supplies; use the beacon or a coverage point. Never leave anyone alone in danger.

## Frequency, review and progression

Run the full routine on every long trail session; review GPX, closures and escape points every 3 months; scout new routes with a short out-and-back. Progress from marked trails to one decision point, then several with an escape route, then night running.

## Scope and limits

This page gives a general routine, not access, live conditions or rescue cover. Private land, protected areas and races have their own rules; check the land manager, organiser and weather service.

## When devices fail: the order

Navigation failure is not only “the phone died”. Protect the person, then the direction, then the track. The wrong order sends you off-route while hunting signal, drains the battery or walks into dangerous terrain.

| Order | Action | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Stop; check people, water, insulation, headlamp | Rule out hypothermia and dehydration |
| 2 | Confirm position with offline/paper map | Do not depend on one device |
| 3 | Return to the last certain point | Rebuild direction from known terrain |
| 4 | If still unsure, take the retreat route | Do not cut a new line |
| 5 | Send a help message and stay put | Save energy and battery |

When the phone is at 1%, do not spend it on photos; send location and plan, then use airplane mode. The backup headlamp and batteries should last until daylight, not “until the next aid station”.

## Retreat points and being lost

Mark 3–5 retreat points before every trail race: road access, signal, a lift or a return to the start. When lost, ask which retreat point is closest, not which finish is closest. Darkness, dropping temperature, injury or a deteriorating teammate makes retreat the highest-completion choice, not a failure.

| Situation | Decision |
| --- | --- |
| Off-route <200 m, terrain visible | Return to the last certain point |
| Off-route >200 m, fog/night/steep | Stop cutting; contour back to known terrain |
| Injury or hypothermia | Retreat/help nearby; abandon the plan |
| Storm, lightning, flood | Leave ridges and valleys; take the low-risk line |
| Dark and no direction | Shelter, send help, wait for daylight |

## Ten-minute pre-race navigation check

Load the track on two devices, plus paper map and spare batteries; confirm start, finish, aid stations, retreat points and cut-offs; send the route to family or crew. One GPX on one phone is not “navigation”.

## Navigation downgrades in snow and at night

Snow hides blazes, cairns and junctions, and other people’s tracks may lead the wrong way. Above the snow line, do not rely on a single track, do not follow unknown footprints, and do not treat “looks like a path” as a path. Carry a map and compass or GPS and know how to use them independently; the phone is a backup, not the only navigator.

Night is similar. A headlamp reaches only a few metres, and markers and junctions are harder to read. Write the next section’s distance, climb and junction numbers on paper and check them on arrival. With snow, fog and darkness combined, retreat on a known line or shelter; do not cut a new one.

## Route card: navigation on one sheet

Devices fail in cold, water and drops; paper does not. Make a route card with four lines: today’s start and finish, total distance and climb, three key junctions with turns, and two escape points with cut-off times. Put it in a waterproof bag in the top pocket or behind the bib; do not store it only on the phone.

The card is not a replacement for the course map; it is the last backup when every device fails. At each major aid station, compare actual arrival with the card and reassess the next section if you are more than 30 minutes off. Do not skip the check because it “feels early”.

| Field | What to write | Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start/finish | Name, CP number | Matches bib |
| Distance/climb | Total and splits | Matches official map |
| Key junctions | Three numbers and turns | Draw arrows |
| Escape points | Two locations and distance | Mark cut-off |

Keep it short and hard; copying the whole course map makes the one thing you need impossible to find.
