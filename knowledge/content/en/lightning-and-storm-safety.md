## When to use this protocol

This protocol supports decisions during trail running, mountain hiking and race days: what signals to watch for, where to move next, and when to stop activity. It applies both to backcountry more than 30 minutes from a modern building or metal-topped vehicle and to closer suburban routes where shelter can be reached quickly. It does not replace local weather warnings, race-director decisions or mountain rescue training; check the forecast, route and exit plan before going out, following the broader framework in [route safety](/en/knowledge/route-safety/) and [trail navigation](/en/knowledge/trail-navigation/). This page covers risk judgment and avoidance only; post-strike field care follows first-aid training and professional rescue.

## Precursors and recognition

Thunderstorms develop quickly, and risk often appears before rain. Per official guidance, watch for:

- **Cumulonimbus and darkening bases.** Tall, blackening thunderheads, churning cloud bases, shifting wind or an oppressive still, hot air are visual signs of an approaching storm.
- **Flash-to-bang interval.** Count seconds between flash and thunder; sound travels about one mile every five seconds, or one kilometre every three seconds. The shorter the gap, the closer the storm. NOAA states that hearing thunder means lightning is near and there is no safe place outside. The classic 30/30 rule puts the storm within danger range at 30 seconds or less between flash and bang (about 6 miles / 10 km); the NWS backcountry brochure uses the more conservative trigger that hearing thunder means lightning is within 10 miles (about 16 km) and descent should begin.
- **Imminent-strike signals.** Hair standing on end and skin tingling mean a strike may be about to hit nearby; some people also notice tinnitus or a static charge. The Met Office is explicit: if your hair stands on end, drop into the crouch position immediately.
- **Remote lightning.** Positive bolts launch from the upper storm and can strike several miles ahead of it; a strike can occur with no rain overhead, so dry ground is not a reason to stay.

## If/then decision table

| If you observe | Official-guidance judgment | Immediate action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thunderstorm warning issued, or clouds building fast in the morning | Risk window already open; postpone or turn the outing into Plan B | Cancel or delay; do not enter exposed terrain |
| Distant lightning, no thunder yet | Storm may be outside range but approaching | Shorten the stay, confirm the exit route and shelter direction; stop moving toward high ground or open terrain |
| Thunder heard, however distant | Already within range of the next ground flash; no safe place outside | Leave ridges, peaks, open ground and isolated trees immediately; move toward low ground or a substantial building / metal-topped vehicle |
| Flash-to-bang 30 seconds or less | Storm within roughly 6-10 km, inside the danger radius | Stop travel on exposed terrain; follow the exposed-terrain procedure; do not keep climbing to finish the loop |
| Hair standing on end, skin tingling, unusual static noise | Imminent-strike signal; a nearby discharge may occur | Take the lightning crouch at once: feet together, lower centre of mass; spread the group per interval guidance |
| Reached a substantial building or hard-topped vehicle | The officially recognized relatively safe place | Stay inside until at least 30 minutes after the last thunder; avoid plumbing, corded phones and electrical equipment |
| Storm seems to have passed, rain easing | Risk may persist; going out too soon is a common cause of casualties | Keep waiting; reassess only after the full 30 minutes, not because rain stopped |

## Exposed terrain: descent order and crouch

In the backcountry (30 minutes or more from modern buildings or vehicles) there is no truly safe spot. On the NOLS/NWS 0-10 scale, a modern building or vehicle rates 10, and nothing in the backcountry rates above 3. Descent and avoidance follow the four ranked actions in the NWS backcountry brochure; each later action is roughly half as important as the one before:

1. **Time visits with local weather.** Learn local patterns, set a turn-around time that gets you off exposed terrain before storms arrive, watch the weather and discuss its state with the group; change the plan rather than forcing a summit or open crossing.
2. **Find safer terrain when you hear thunder.** Avoid peaks, ridges and markedly higher ground; where you can choose, descend the side without cloud cover, then relax the judgment once on low rolling ground.
3. **Avoid trees and long conductors.** Stay clear of isolated trees and bushes rising above the rest, and of fences, cables, railings and power lines.
4. **Take the lightning position if you cannot move.** Squat or sit with feet together, reduce your contact area with the ground, stand on a pack or insulating pad if available; do not lie flat.

**Positions that are explicitly not safe:** beside an isolated or tall tree (side flash jumps from a struck object to the person beside it); cliff edges and rock overhangs (arcs can cross the gap); ponds, lakes and rivers (contact and conduction risk); the middle of open flat ground; cave mouths, shallow caves and wall-less shelters or pavilions. If the tent sits on a ridge, open ground or beside a tall tree, leave it for safer terrain before the storm arrives. On exposed ground, spread the group about 7 m (20 ft) apart to reduce multiple injuries, while staying visible or within calling distance.

| Position | Usable as shelter? | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Substantial building with electricity or plumbing | Yes, officially recognized relatively safe place | NWS / NOAA substantial-building shelter |
| Enclosed hard-topped vehicle with windows up | Yes, officially recognized relatively safe place | Metal-roof frame Faraday-cage effect |
| Beside an isolated or tall tree | No | Side flash jumps from the struck tree to the person beside it |
| Cliff edge, rock wall, rock overhang | No | Arcs cross the gap; no real shelter |
| Ponds, lakes, rivers and other water | No | Contact voltage and water conduction risk |
| Middle of open flat ground | No | Exposed and large ground-current contact area |
| Wall-less pavilion, shed or tent | No | Not a lightning shelter |
| Cave mouth, shallow cave | No | Arcs can cross the entrance gap |

## Race interruption and withdrawal rules

On race day, follow the current organizer regulations and on-course referee decisions, but the general principles are consistent: check thunderstorm forecasts beforehand; the organizer may suspend, close sections or order a sweep. At thunder, an approaching cumulonimbus, or a referee stop signal, stop climbing or entering exposed sections, move to the nearest safe point or follow the sweep direction; do not leave the marked course on your own shortcut. Accept withdrawal or sweep decisions made by medical and race personnel. Aid-station buildings and hard-topped vehicles are better shelter than a backcountry crouch; open pavilions, shade shelters and tents are not lightning shelters. The resume-activity basis is also at least 30 minutes after the last thunder, unless race command decides otherwise.

## Team roles

- **Lead / navigator:** watches ahead terrain and cloud state, chooses the descent direction and route; checks turn-around time and the backup route.
- **Weather observer:** tracks cloud, listens for thunder, counts flash-to-bang intervals and calls out the risk level; in a group this is not left to the person running all-out.
- **Tail:** confirms everyone clears key points and nobody drops behind; carries the first-aid kit, headlamp and communication device.
- **All members:** execute the decision signal immediately, do not leave the group alone, and report state and gear position.
- **Event staff / rescue:** hold the authority to interrupt, sweep and medically evacuate; runners accept those decisions.

## Red flags / stop rules

Stop activity and take avoidance action immediately on any of the following; do not keep moving to finish a run or race:

- Thunder heard, or flash-to-bang 30 seconds or less;
- Tall cumulonimbus overhead or ahead, dark churning base, sudden wind shift;
- Hair standing on end, skin tingling, unusual tinnitus or static sensation;
- Positioned on a ridge, peak, open ground, under an isolated tree, on a rock wall or beside water, with the storm approaching;
- A team member shows lightning-related signs: altered awareness, collapse, palpitations, breathing difficulty, burns, or abnormal state after a fall;
- Visibility drops or rain turns heavy enough that the route can no longer be read safely.

Lightning victims do not carry a residual charge and are safe to touch. Call for help and emergency services; follow first-aid training: triage reverses the usual order, attending first to those in cardiac or respiratory arrest without obvious lethal injury, with CPR or an AED as needed. Do not leave a victim alone in hazardous terrain.

## Checklist

- [ ] Forecast and warnings checked before departure; nearest building, vehicle and exit route known.
- [ ] Turn-around time set to leave exposed terrain before storms arrive.
- [ ] Roles assigned: navigation, weather observer, tail.
- [ ] Isolated trees, rock overhangs, water, open ground and wall-less shelters recognized as non-safe spots.
- [ ] Crouch remembered: feet together, low centre, minimal ground contact, no lying flat, on pack or pad if possible.
- [ ] Know to wait at least 30 minutes after the last thunder once sheltered.
- [ ] Phone/radio conserved; beacon or backup navigation available.

## Common misconceptions

- **No rain means no need to retreat.** Positive bolts can strike miles ahead before rain; hearing thunder means act.
- **Sheltering under a big tree or rock overhang.** Side flash and arcing across a gap reach the person beside it; these spots are more dangerous.
- **Lying flat to avoid a strike.** Lying flat enlarges exposure to ground current, which causes about half of lightning fatalities; squat with feet together instead.
- **Going out as soon as rain stops.** Leaving too soon after the storm passes is a common cause of death; wait the full 30 minutes after the last thunder.
- **Rubber soles or tires insulate.** Rubber offers zero lightning protection; vehicle safety comes from the metal-roof frame Faraday effect, and soft-top convertibles are most at risk.
- **Staying put alone to watch gear.** The group spreads at intervals but keeps contact; nobody leaves alone.

## Scope and limits

This protocol follows general guidance published by NWS, NOAA, NOLS and the UK Met Office. It does not replace local weather warnings, race regulations, mountain rescue or first-aid training. Lightning activity, terrain shelter, cell coverage and rescue response vary widely by region; there is no absolutely safe position in the backcountry, and the protocol reduces risk rather than eliminating it. This page does not diagnose or prescribe medication; a strike, altered awareness, chest pain, collapse or breathing abnormality is an emergency — call for help first.

## What changes this advice

- **Season and local climate.** Afternoon thermal thunderstorms, coastal squall lines and high-altitude storms differ in speed and warning lead; turn-around times follow local patterns.
- **Terrain and route.** Ridges, peaks, stream valleys, rock walls and woodland carry different risk; escape points and crouch positions are chosen from the actual ground.
- **Race rules and on-site command.** Suspension thresholds, sweep points and resume times follow the current regulations and race command, and may be stricter than the general 30-minute basis.
- **Evidence and guidance updates.** Official lightning safety guidance is revised over time; this protocol is synced at its six-month review.
