## Position and diagnostic boundary

Deciding whether to withdraw on race day should not be a willpower argument once the body is already in trouble. This page organizes the common red flags, withdrawal routes and communication flow into if/then rules to help runners and support crew make on-course triage judgments.

**Diagnostic boundary first: this site does not give medical diagnosis.** This page does not determine what condition you have, grade an injury, prescribe a training or recovery plan, or recommend any medication. On-course medical staff own examination, diagnosis, the decision to continue or transfer. When the emergency signals below appear, the action is to stop moving, call race medical or local emergency services, not to self-diagnose against this page and keep running. Recognition and call-out for cardiac arrest and unresponsiveness are covered separately in [cardiac emergency while running](/en/knowledge/cardiac-emergency-in-running/); the full heat-environment risk framework is in [heat and humidity](/en/knowledge/heat-and-humidity/).

## When withdrawal is mandatory: red-flag overview

The table splits signals into "stop immediately and call medical" and "stop and seek on-course medical assessment". The tiers follow the nature of the signal; on-site staff decide the actual treatment.

| Signal class | Red-flag presentation | Action tier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Altered mental state | Confusion, slurred speech, disorientation, ataxia (unsteady gait), fainting or loss of consciousness | Stop immediately, call emergency services; treat as emergency |
| Heat illness | Abnormally hot or cold skin, persistent vomiting, very rapid pulse, high heat sensation, collapse | Stop immediately, cool and call medical; suspected heat stroke as emergency |
| Hypothermia | Uncontrolled shivering, slowing responses, slurred speech, stiff limbs, severe fatigue | Stop, shelter from wind, warm up, call medical |
| Chest and breathing | Chest pain, radiating pain, breathlessness not relieved by rest, abnormal palpitations with dizziness and cold sweat | Stop immediately, call emergency services |
| Musculoskeletal | Progressively worsening pain, swelling, inability to bear weight normally, visible deformity, one-sided calf swelling and pain | Stop the race, on-course medical assessment before deciding to continue |
| Thrombosis signs | One-sided calf or thigh pain and swelling, red or darkened skin, swollen veins; with breathlessness or chest pain | Stop immediately, call emergency services (suspected pulmonary embolism) |
| Time cut-off | Missed a checkpoint, or predicted to miss the next one | Leave the course by the rules, follow official withdrawal flow |
| Low blood / low sugar | Dizziness, cold sweat, tremor, slowed responses that do not settle after carbohydrate | Stop and seek medical assessment |

The Comrades 2026 race doctor listed runner red flags as dizziness, confusion, nausea, muscle cramps, disorientation, chest pain and sudden chills, and noted temperatures often drop steeply after 4 p.m., so heat and cold alternate and stack risk. Western States medical notes list on-course risks including hypothermia, hyperthermia, dehydration, hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, disorientation and mental and physical exhaustion.

## Heat-illness signals and the response order

The NWS basis separates two tiers: heat exhaustion can cause heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea, headache and fainting; heat stroke is an emergency with confusion, slurred speech, very high body temperature, rapid pulse and possible loss of consciousness.

- **if** only dizziness, nausea, heavy sweating and weakness, with clear consciousness; **then** stop immediately, get to shade, loosen clothing, take small fluids, and seek on-course medical assessment.
- **if** confusion, slurred speech, fainting, convulsions, abnormally hot or cold skin, or persistent vomiting appears; **then** treat as emergency: call emergency services first, cool the person while waiting; do not leave them alone in the heat, and do not substitute "walk it off" for medical assessment.
- **if** pace cannot hold in heat and humidity; **then** change the target from performance to finishing or withdrawal; do not push through. Dehydration beyond 2% of body weight is a range requiring correction (ACSM hydration basis); fluid rhythm and sweat-rate estimation are in [hydration](/en/knowledge/hydration/).

Heat stroke is not solved by drinking water, and it cannot be pushed through with willpower.

## Hypothermia and cold signals

The NWS explains that extreme cold air combined with wind creates dangerous wind-chill values; exposed people may suffer frostbite and succumb to hypothermia; uncovered skin and extremities such as hands and feet are most prone; hypothermia occurs when the body loses heat faster than it can produce it. Mountain races can chill quickly at night stations, rain and descents even in summer.

- **if** shivering worsens, speech slows, coordination drops, urine output rises or unusual fatigue appears; **then** stop exposure, add insulation, shelter from wind, and seek medical assessment.
- **if** altered mental state, shivering that suddenly stops after severe trembling, pale or bluish skin appears; **then** call medical immediately and treat as emergency.
- **if** it is a night or rainy trail race; **then** insulation and rain shell are mandatory before starting, not judged after feeling cold on course.

## Progressive pain and musculoskeletal signals

Pain alone does not mean withdrawal, but "progressive" is the key: pain that worsens with mileage, stays fixed in one spot, alters gait, or comes with swelling, tenderness or inability to bear weight means local load has exceeded tolerance. The general relationship between pain and load is in [pain and load](/en/knowledge/pain-and-load/); pre-existing injury risk can be checked with the [injury risk self-check tool](/en/tools/injury-risk-check/).

- **if** there was clear pain or an unresolved old injury before the start; **then** decide on race entry by the injury recovery plan, not by adding load on race day.
- **if** pain stays stable during the race and does not affect gait; **then** reduce intensity and observe; stop if it does not settle.
- **if** pain progresses, swelling, limp or inability to land normally appears; **then** stop the race and seek on-course medical assessment; do not aim to "hold on to the next station".
- **if** one-sided calf or thigh pain and swelling, red or darkened skin, or swollen veins appears; **then** watch for deep-vein thrombosis; with breathlessness or chest pain, call emergency services as a possible pulmonary embolism (NHS DVT basis).

Painkillers or anti-inflammatories should not be self-used to push through: the Comrades race doctor advised against painkillers or anti-inflammatories during the race; medication decisions are made by medical staff, and this page gives no medication advice.

## Time cut-off and being swept

Missing a cut-off is a rule-level withdrawal, independent of whether the body could still run. How to read the table, convert segment pace and budget buffer is in [cut-off management](/en/knowledge/cutoff-management/).

- **if** buffer at the current point is below the reserve line and the next section is a long climb or night segment; **then** switch to finishing rhythm, prioritize fueling and station efficiency, and stop chasing the target.
- **if** a checkpoint cut-off has been missed; **then** leave at the nearest point by the rules and use official withdrawal transport; do not continue along the course.
- **if** body signals and a cut-off miss appear together; **then** medical care comes first: handle the body state, then complete the withdrawal record.

## How to withdraw: next reachable point versus waiting in place

Withdrawal routes split into two; the choice depends on position, body state and race support.

| Situation | Choice | Action points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mild symptoms, clear consciousness, close to next aid/medical point | Move to the next reachable point | Accompanied by staff or a partner; do not walk alone; report and assess on arrival |
| Severe symptoms, altered mental state, unable to move, or inaccessible terrain | Wait in place for rescue | Stay at a marked, locatable position; keep warm and hydrated; notify race medical and liaison |
| Road or open course | Move to a safe position off the course | Do not cross traffic; wait for official transport; do not leave on an unofficial vehicle and rejoin |
| Between trail mountain stations | Do not descend or enter woodland privately | Contact official timing/medical and wait at the designated point on instructions; mountain access may be on foot, horseback or helicopter only |

Western States medical notes state much of the course is inaccessible and many sections are reachable only on foot, horseback or helicopter; medical help may be difficult or impossible to reach in time, so signals call for earlier help than on an urban course. When ground or helicopter emergency evacuation is required, costs are borne by the runner (that event's basis); insurance and rescue arrangements should be understood before the start.

## Post-withdrawal communication, record and recovery

1. **On-site record**: report at the withdrawal point or medical tent; confirm how the DNF is recorded, the withdrawal station and transport arrangement; keep bib information and medical treatment records.
2. **Notify liaison**: the lead liaison notifies family or crew of the withdrawal location and destination; division and liaison protocol for long races is in [crew and pacer planning](/en/knowledge/crew-and-pacer-planning/).
3. **Medical follow-up**: cooperate if on-site staff recommend transfer or review; after the race arrange recovery by medical advice, not by the original race plan.
4. **Post-race check**: verify the result record and DNF notation; raise any objection through the regulations' appeal process.
5. **Recovery rhythm**: general post-race recovery principles are in [post-race recovery](/en/knowledge/post-race-recovery/); return-to-run timing follows body recovery and medical opinion, not the finishing goal.

## The line between persistence and risk-taking

Persistence continues within controllable, stable signals and the rules and safety boundary; risk-taking ignores red flags and uses willpower to override body feedback. Write the criteria into the plan before race day:

- **May continue**: signals stable or improving; fueling normal; gait and consciousness clear; buffer within budget; weather and surface controllable.
- **Should downgrade**: feel sliding, fuel absorption worsening, local pain appearing; actions are lower intensity, longer stops, shorter target — not faster pace.
- **Must stop**: altered mental state, chest pain or breathlessness, progressive pain or swelling, heat-illness or hypothermia signs, thrombosis signs, missed cut-off.

Three easily confused lines: first, "it usually aches in training" does not make this signal normal; second, others struggling is not a reason to continue; third, distance covered in training cannot offset today's race-day body state. World Athletics competition medical guidelines recommend organizers prepare a medical plan with medical points, emergency response and evacuation routes, and that the medical team handles pre-event planning, on-course response and hospital transfer; personal judgment works with this on-site system, not instead of it.

## Additional signals at altitude races

Above 2500 m, unacclimatized lowlanders carry risk of acute mountain sickness (AMS), high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE) and high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) (WMS 2024 basis).

- **if** headache, nausea, fatigue or poor sleep appear (AMS); **then** stop ascending, observe, and descend if it does not settle.
- **if** unsteady gait (ataxia) or altered mentation appears; **then** this suggests HACE: descend immediately and call medical.
- **if** cough, breathlessness or lung rales appear (HAPE); **then** give oxygen if available and descend at least about 1000 m or until symptoms resolve, while calling medical.

## Three failure modes and early signals

| Failure mode | Early signal | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Willpower overriding red flags | Signal already present but plan is "hold one more station"; refusing medical assessment | Mild case becomes emergency; evacuation harder and risk rises |
| Handling it alone, no liaison | No notification to crew or race medical; position hard to describe | Rescue localization slow after lost contact; best response window missed |
| Returning to training load right after withdrawal | Fatigue or pain unresolved, then running by the schedule | Minor injury becomes long-term; return takes longer overall |

Matching actions: tell yourself and support crew the red-flag list and withdrawal flow before the race; agree one liaison voice; progress gradually by recovery principles after the race. The overall long-distance rhythm framework is in [ultra-running basics](/en/knowledge/ultra-running-basics/).

## Scope and items the official source does not publish

This page is general triage education; it does not give diagnostic conclusions, injury grading, recovery timelines or medication plans. Medical point locations, AED placement, sweep transport and rescue cost terms follow that edition's official handbook. The following are marked unknown on this site: that edition's medical station spacing and response times; the rescue scope covered by personal insurance; the specific authority of on-site medical staff. Until that edition's materials are obtained, do not carry over last year's medical arrangements.
