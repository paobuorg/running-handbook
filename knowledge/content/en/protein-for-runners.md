## Conclusion first

For runners, protein's primary role is repairing muscle damage, supporting adaptation and maintaining body structure — not supplying the main fuel for running. The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand puts daily protein at 1.4–2.0 g/kg body weight for most exercising individuals; general recommendations are about 0.25 g/kg of high-quality protein per serving, or an absolute 20–40 g, distributed evenly every 3–4 hours across the day. Carbohydrate remains the performance fuel for endurance work; protein helps offset muscle damage and supports recovery.

Three actions come first. First, secure total carbohydrate and energy intake before arranging protein distribution. Second, spread protein over 3–5 meals rather than concentrating it in one large dinner. Third, plant-based eaters actively plan total protein, amino-acid profile and leucine sources, using legumes, soy products or plant protein powder where needed, rather than copying an omnivore plate.

This page is general nutrition education, not a medical diet prescription. People with kidney disease, an eating-disorder history, pregnancy or other special conditions should have protein intake assessed by a doctor or registered dietitian first.

## Definition and premises

- **Protein:** built from essential and non-essential amino acids; supports muscle protein synthesis, enzymes, hormones, immune substances and tissue repair. Runners balance muscle damage and repair after training.
- **Muscle protein synthesis (MPS):** the process of building or repairing muscle protein; both protein intake and the exercise stimulus promote it, together.
- **Leucine:** a branched-chain amino acid considered a primary trigger of MPS; plant proteins are often lower in leucine.
- **High-quality protein:** contains all essential amino acids with good digestibility, such as dairy, egg, meat and soy isolate.

This page targets road and trail endurance runners. Those with a heavy strength-training component may sit toward the higher end of the range; see [Strength training for runners](/en/knowledge/strength-training-for-runners/). For food around sessions, see [Pre- and post-run fueling](/en/knowledge/pre-and-post-run-fueling/); for the everyday diet structure, see [Daily fueling](/en/knowledge/daily-fueling/).

## Total intake: by body weight, training load and goal

ISSN: for building muscle or maintaining it through a positive muscle protein balance, 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day is sufficient for most exercising individuals. The plant-diet review cites common groupings of 1.2–1.4 g/kg for endurance athletes and 1.6–1.7 g/kg for strength or power athletes; vegetarian and vegan athletes may sit toward the higher end of the ISSN range because of plant-protein digestibility and amino-acid profile.

| Group / scenario | Reference range (g/kg/day) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Easy-running, low-volume recovery week | About 1.2–1.4 | Low end when energy is adequate; do not let protein crowd out carbohydrate |
| Regular endurance training day | 1.4–1.6 | Repair and adaptation; total adjusts to load and state |
| Hard session, race day or post-taper recovery | 1.6–2.0 | Toward mid-to-high end with large stimulus and repair demand |
| Substantial concurrent strength work | 1.6–1.7 and above | Demand rises when strength stimulus stacks on endurance |
| Vegan / plant-based diet | Toward the higher end of 1.4–2.0 | Watch digestibility, essential amino acids and leucine sources |

These are population ranges from research, not a gram-level personal prescription; body size, age, injury history, energy balance and goal all change where you land. ISSN also notes that when energy intake is inadequate, protein is burned for fuel; securing carbohydrate and total energy comes before protein can serve repair.

## Timing and distribution: spread across meals

ISSN recommends about 0.25 g/kg high-quality protein per serving, or an absolute 20–40 g, ideally distributed evenly every 3–4 hours across the day rather than concentrated at dinner.

- **Pre-session meal:** easy-to-digest carbohydrate plus moderate protein; avoid high fat, high fibre and a large solid protein load that causes gastric distress on the move. For pre-race arrangements, see [Race-day fueling](/en/knowledge/race-day-fueling/).
- **Post-session:** the synthetic response after exercise lasts beyond a few dozen minutes; there is no narrow "anabolic window" that expires. Arranging adequate protein and carbohydrate in the next meal supports glycogen recovery and repair.
- **Daily meals:** one protein serving at breakfast, lunch, dinner and a snack each is closer to the evidence pattern than loading the whole day's allowance into one meal.
- **Evening:** keep one protein serving at dinner; runners do not need a late-night high-protein snack unless the day's intake was genuinely short.

The ISSN nutrient-timing stand also cautions that timing and amount around exercise affect performance and recovery, but recommendations must cover a wide range of individuals and adjust to exercise duration.

## Plant protein and the leucine boundary

A plant diet can meet training needs, but it needs active planning rather than a default equivalence to an omnivore plate:

1. **Amino-acid profile:** plant proteins are often incomplete in essential amino acids and typically lower in branched-chain amino acids than animal sources. Grains are most often low in lysine; legumes are rich in lysine, and soy beans and lentils are the plant leucine sources cited in the review.
2. **Digestibility:** plant proteins generally digest less well than animal proteins; vegetarians may need more total protein to compensate, and vegan athletes are advised toward the higher end of 1.4–2.0 g/kg.
3. **Combining:** the older rule that different plant proteins must be combined within a single feeding to make a complete amino-acid profile is no longer considered necessary; a variety of grains, legumes, nuts and seeds across the day supplies the essential amino acids.
4. **Optional supplements:** soy, pea, rice, hemp or blended plant powders are a practical means when whole foods cannot reach the target. Supplements do not replace the overall diet; choice and evidence are reviewed in [Supplements and evidence](/en/knowledge/supplements-and-evidence/).

Common high-protein plant foods (protein per 100 g dry weight, USDA figures from the ISSN vegan review table): pumpkin seeds about 30.2 g, red split lentils about 24.6 g, black beans about 21.6 g, almonds about 21.2 g, tempeh about 20.3 g, calcium-set tofu about 17.3 g, rolled oats about 16.9 g, quinoa about 14.1 g.

## Decision table: where protein sits for each day

| Day's situation | Protein tendency | Pairing reminder |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Easy 30–60 min run, normal workday | Low end of range; normal meals plus one dairy/soy/egg serving | Secure carbohydrate and energy first |
| Long run or tempo over 90 min | Mid range; a post-session meal with adequate protein and carbohydrate | Protein does not replace in-race carbohydrate |
| Race day | Carbohydrate during the race; one adequate protein-and-carb meal after | Recovery arrangements in [Post-race recovery](/en/knowledge/post-race-recovery/) |
| Taper or rest week | Drop toward low end with training load; do not keep hard-session high intake | Avoid protein crowding out carbohydrate or a long-term energy surplus |
| Vegan with high training volume | High end of range; check legume/soy frequency | Use plant powder if needed to top up |
| Recent injury, surgery or recovery phase | Confirm targets with the rehabilitation/clinical team | Protein is one recovery condition, not a treatment |

## Worked example: daily split for a 70 kg runner (assumed)

Assumptions: a 70 kg runner on a regular endurance training day. At 1.4–1.6 g/kg, that is about 98–112 g protein per day; split into four to five servings at about 0.25 g/kg (≈17.5 g) or the 20–40 g absolute serving range.

| Meal | Example foods (assumed) | Protein target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Breakfast | Oats with milk or soy milk, one egg | About 20–25 g |
| Post-session | Yogurt or soy product with carbohydrate (fruit, bread) | About 20 g |
| Lunch | Legumes/chicken/fish with staple and vegetables | About 25–30 g |
| Dinner | Tofu, fish or lean meat with staple | About 25–30 g |
| Snack (if needed) | Nuts or a glass of milk/soy milk | About 10–15 g |

Reading the numbers: the daily total sits in range, with an independent protein serving at each meal. For a 2+ hour long run, post-session carbohydrate follows the long-run fueling line, and protein is only part of that meal. Plant-based eaters swap animal foods for legumes, soy and grain combinations, and nudge total intake toward the high end of the range.

## Common misconceptions

1. **Writing runner nutrition as a bodybuilding programme:** endurance runners' protein needs are lower than strength-focused muscle builders; this page does not recommend bodybuilding-style large doses or stacked high-protein powders.
2. **Replacing carbohydrate with protein:** ISSN states endurance athletes should focus on adequate carbohydrate for performance, with protein helping offset damage and recovery; in-race fuel stays carbohydrate-led; see [Long-run fueling](/en/knowledge/long-run-fueling/).
3. **"More is always better":** in otherwise healthy people, evidence does not show a high-protein diet harming kidneys or bones, but that does not mean no ceiling; protein well beyond the range does not add running performance and can displace carbohydrate.
4. **Solving intake with powder alone:** whole foods can meet protein needs; powder is a convenience. Long-term reliance on supplements misses micronutrients and fibre from food.
5. **Assuming a plant diet is enough without adjustment:** it can be adequate, but total amount, variety and leucine sources need deliberate planning, not the same plate proportions.

## Failure modes and stop rules

- **Large training load but chronically insufficient energy:** early signals are accumulated fatigue, stalled performance, menstrual disturbance or repeated injury; first assess energy availability rather than blindly adding protein, see the female runner and RED-S pages.
- **Protein concentrated in one meal:** early signals are little protein at breakfast and lunch, oversized dinner; move to split meals.
- **Plant diet long dominated by single grains:** signals are low legume, soy and nut frequency; adjust food variety or consider a blended plant powder.
- **Oedema, marked change in urine output or unexplained rapid weight change:** this is outside nutrition adjustment; stop self-prescribed supplements and seek care.

## Applicable boundaries

This page is general exercise nutrition education, not a diagnosis, medical diet prescription or supplement recommendation. g/kg ranges come from position stands and reviews of specific populations and cannot be converted into a precise personal requirement; observational and position-stand evidence is flagged separately. Kidney disease, liver disease, eating disorder, pregnancy and lactation, medication treatment or other special medical situations call for clinician or registered-dietitian assessment of protein and overall diet. This site does not interpret blood nutrition markers or replace clinical nutrition judgment.

## What would change this advice

- **Training phase and goal:** hard-preparation, race, taper and off-season days sit at different protein points; an endurance-focused cycle does not eat to a strength-muscle-gain plan.
- **Diet pattern:** omnivore, vegan, food allergy and digestive condition change available protein sources and the total range.
- **Injury and recovery:** post-surgery or post-injury rehabilitation protein targets are set by the rehabilitation team and may be above or below the usual range.
- **Energy balance:** protein may be higher in a weight-loss phase to preserve lean mass, and carbohydrate rises together with protein in a weight-gain phase; when energy is short, protein is used first for fuel.
- **Evidence update:** protein dosing, leucine thresholds and plant-protein digestibility remain active research; this page adjusts if later high-quality consensus or reviews differ.
