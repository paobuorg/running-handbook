## Conclusion first

Whether you can run during pregnancy and after childbirth is not answered by a generic race schedule. It is a decision that starts with obstetric assessment — involving pelvic-floor rehabilitation when needed — and then adjusts around your body's signals. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists Committee Opinion No. 804 states that, in the absence of obstetric or medical complications or contraindications, physical activity in pregnancy is safe and desirable; women who were habitually vigorous or physically active before pregnancy can continue those activities during pregnancy and the postpartum period, while sedentary women should follow a more gradual progression. NHS guidance adds a boundary that is easy to miss: running as a vigorous activity should not be started for the first time during pregnancy.

Three actions come first. First, before continuing or starting to run, tell your obstetrician or midwife about your exercise history, gestational age and any complications, and obtain explicit clearance. Second, use the talk test, hydration and thermal comfort as your main monitoring tools; after 20 weeks of gestation, avoid prolonged lying flat on your back. Third, after birth do not return to your pre-pregnancy mileage directly. Complete a pelvic-floor and abdominal-wall assessment first, and only then consider starting with one or two minutes of easy running.

This page does not diagnose, prescribe a training plan or recommend medication. Vaginal bleeding, regular painful contractions, fluid leakage, breathlessness before exertion, dizziness, headache, chest pain, weakness affecting balance, or calf pain or swelling mean stop exercising and contact obstetric care; loss of consciousness, severe chest pain or marked breathlessness are emergencies.

## Who this is for, and who it is not for

**This page is for** runners who trained regularly before pregnancy, have an uncomplicated pregnancy, and have been assessed as cleared; runners who want to switch to low-impact alternatives during pregnancy and later reassess whether running can return; postpartum women looking for the return-to-run window and pelvic-floor signals; and coaches who need to know which boundaries must be left to clinicians.

**This page does not apply directly to** people who were inactive before pregnancy and want to take up running now — NHS advises against starting a new vigorous activity such as running in pregnancy; begin with walking, swimming or stationary cycling instead. Anyone with pregnancy complications, cardiovascular or respiratory disease, ongoing vaginal bleeding, preterm-labour risk, a multiple pregnancy, a prior pelvic-floor disorder, or a caesarean or severe perineal injury needs an individualized plan set by obstetric and rehabilitation professionals; this page cannot replace that assessment.

Two separate questions are often conflated. The first — "is staying active safe in pregnancy?" — mainstream guidance answers positively for uncomplicated groups. The second — "is running, this high-impact activity, suitable for me right now?" — depends on prior habit, gestational age, body response and assessment; the first answer does not imply the second.

## Definition and premises

"Running" here means weight-bearing impact activity such as road or trail running, with brief phases of airborne flight. "Postpartum" means the recovery period from delivery until a regular running habit resumes, observed in weeks rather than from discharge day. The source positions are:

- ACOG CO 804: women with uncomplicated pregnancies should be encouraged in aerobic and strength-conditioning exercise before, during and after pregnancy; at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, spread through the week; previously active women may continue vigorous activity.
- NHS: aim for 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week, talk but not sing, in shorter sessions if needed; avoid activities with fall or abdominal-impact risk; do not start running as a new vigorous activity in pregnancy.
- CDC: physical activity is healthy and safe for most pregnant women; discuss any activity restriction with your care provider; the 150 minutes can be broken into small chunks; weight-gain goals are set with your provider.
- Goom, Donnelly and Brockwell 2019 postpartum return-to-run consensus: running is not advisable before 3 months postnatal; it is delayed beyond that if pelvic-floor dysfunction symptoms are present; every postpartum mother, regardless of delivery mode, should be offered a pelvic-health assessment from 6 weeks with a specialist physiotherapist.

These sources differ in strength: ACOG and NHS are official guidance, CDC is a public-health page, and the Goom 2019 document is a professional consensus built from the best available evidence plus clinical opinion; its authors state it assists clinical reasoning rather than providing a prescriptive approach.

## Key mechanisms: how pregnancy changes the running decision

Four sets of physiological change shape the running decision:

1. **Bodyweight and joint load.** Weight gain and the forward shift of the centre of gravity increase lumbar lordosis and the forces across joints and the spine during weight-bearing exercise; ACOG cites data that more than 60% of pregnant women experience low back pain, and strengthening abdominal and back muscles may reduce that risk.
2. **Circulation.** Blood volume, heart rate, stroke volume and cardiac output rise while systemic vascular resistance falls. Heart-rate responses can be blunted in pregnancy, so ACOG recommends rating of perceived exertion (Borg 13–14, "somewhat hard") and the talk test rather than heart rate alone.
3. **Position and blood pressure.** After 20 weeks, prolonged supine positioning can reduce venous return through aortocaval compression from the gravid uterus, causing hypotension and dizziness; avoid long flat-back holds.
4. **Temperature and blood glucose.** Prolonged exercise should be in a thermoneutral or controlled environment, with no prolonged heat exposure; high-intensity or prolonged exercise beyond 45 minutes can contribute to hypoglycaemia, so fuel before exercise or keep session intensity and duration modest.

These mechanisms explain why "I ran before" does not mean "I run the same way now": joint load, circulatory demand and thermal response at the same pace all change.

## Conditions to continue, and signals that mean stop

The table separates two kinds of information: left, the premises for continuing activity after clearance; right, signals that stop exercise and trigger contact with obstetric care. The right column is not a "try one more run" threshold — stop first, then contact a professional.

| Premises for continuing after obstetric clearance | Red flags: stop exercise and contact obstetric care (ACOG Box 3) |
| --- | --- |
| Assessed as uncomplicated with no contraindication; your clinician knows your activity plan | Vaginal bleeding |
| You already ran or did vigorous exercise regularly before pregnancy (starting running anew in pregnancy is not in this group) | Abdominal pain |
| Moderate intensity: full sentences, Borg about 13–14; no pace or PR pursuit | Regular painful contractions |
| Controlled environment, not overheated; fuel before exercise, no fasted sessions | Amniotic fluid leakage |
| Adequate hydration and suitable support; avoid prolonged supine after 20 weeks | Dyspnea before exertion |
| No discomfort, dizziness, pain or pelvic heaviness during activity; feed before a session if lactating | Dizziness, headache, chest pain |
|  | Muscle weakness affecting balance; calf pain or swelling |

NHS adds avoidance of sports with fall or abdominal-impact risk (hockey, rugby, mountain biking), scuba diving, and — in later pregnancy — lying flat on the back for more than a few minutes. Contact sports, high fall-risk activities, diving and unacclimatised altitude above 6,000 feet sit in the same "exclude first" category, outside the running discussion.

## The postpartum return: time window, assessment and pelvic-floor signals

The evidence base for postpartum return to running is limited; the existing professional consensus gives a time window and screening criteria rather than a fixed weekly plan:

- **0–3 months:** Goom 2019 recommends low-impact activity only — walking, stationary bike, elliptical, swimming once lochia has stopped and wounds are healing. Pelvic-floor muscle training can begin in the immediate postpartum period under guidance. From 6 weeks, every postpartum mother should be offered a pelvic-health assessment with a specialist physiotherapist, regardless of delivery mode.
- **3–6 months:** if assessment passes and no pelvic-floor or abdominal-wall dysfunction symptoms are present, a graded return can be considered after 3 months. Start small — often one to two minutes of easy running — add volume (distance/time) before intensity, and keep the weekly running distance or time increase around or below 10%.
- **When to delay:** if symptoms are found in assessment, or appear after an attempted return, return to low impact and refer for pelvic-floor rehabilitation rather than pushing through running.

Pelvic-floor and abdominal-wall dysfunction signs (Goom 2019 Table 1): urinary or faecal incontinence; urgency difficult to defer; pelvic heaviness, pressure, bulge or dragging; pain with intercourse; obstructed defecation; a pendular abdomen, separated abdominal muscles or reduced abdominal strength; lumbopelvic pain; ongoing or increased bleeding unrelated to menstruation beyond 8 weeks postpartum.

ACOG's postpartum position is that exercise routines may be resumed gradually as soon as medically safe, depending on delivery mode (vaginal or caesarean) and any medical or surgical complications. Some women can resume activities within days of delivery; regular aerobic exercise in lactating women does not appear to affect milk production, composition or infant growth. Running is a high-impact item, so its pace of return should be slower than walking and low-impact cross-training.

## Worked example: a previously active postpartum runner (assumed)

The following is an assumed scenario illustrating the decision sequence, not a personal plan. A woman who ran 30 km/week before pregnancy has an uncomplicated vaginal birth. At 6 weeks her pelvic assessment passes: pelvic-floor strength and endurance are adequate, no leakage, no heaviness, no obvious diastasis recti, no lumbopelvic pain.

| Stage | Assumed time | Arrangement | Gate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0–6 weeks | Birth to 6 weeks | Walking, pelvic-floor and basic core work; no running | Lochia, perineal or caesarean wound recovery per clinical advice |
| 6–12 weeks | 6–12 weeks | Low impact: power walking, stationary bike, elliptical; swimming depending on lochia and wounds | No pelvic symptoms, no pain, assessment passed |
| Around 3 months | From 12 weeks | If cleared: run-walk with running bouts of about 1–2 minutes easy; add time before intensity | No leakage, heaviness, pain or bleeding in the 24 h after running |
| After | Weekly | Weekly mileage/time increase within about 10%; quality sessions and long runs on well-recovered days | Any pelvic red flag means step back to low impact and refer |

Reading the numbers: even with smooth recovery and a passing assessment, running restarts from minute-level bouts and run-walk, not pre-pregnancy mileage. If assessment does not pass, the time window yields to symptom management.

## Common misconceptions

1. **"Pregnancy means no exercise; bed rest protects the baby."** ACOG states there is no credible evidence for prescribing activity restriction or bed rest to reduce preterm birth routinely; routine restriction can add venous thromboembolism, bone demineralisation and deconditioning risks. Whether restriction applies to you is decided by your obstetrician for a specific condition.
2. **"I ran before, so I keep the same race plan in pregnancy."** Previously active women can continue activity, but routines still adjust for anatomic, physiologic and fetal demands; race-level intensity, prolonged duration and heat exposure are not within general advice and need specialist supervision.
3. **"The six-week check means I can go back to long runs."** A routine six-week postnatal check is not the same as a dedicated pelvic-floor and abdominal-wall assessment; leakage, heaviness, diastasis and lumbopelvic pain are not all covered by a standard visit.
4. **"Leaking after birth is normal, just push through."** Goom 2019 calls such symptoms "common but not normal"; they need assessment and rehabilitation, not a harder running habit.
5. **"Running while breastfeeding ruins the milk."** Evidence does not show regular aerobic exercise harming lactation; empty the breasts before exercise and hydrate, and adjust if discomfort appears.

## Failure modes and stop rules

- **Treating "I finished one run" as "I can keep adding load."** Early signals: leakage during or after running, pelvic heaviness, a midline abdominal separation sensation, lumbopelvic pain. Rule: step back to walking and low impact, arrange pelvic assessment, and do not add running bouts next time.
- **Pushing through heat or fasted in pregnancy.** Early signals: dizziness, black vision, nausea, a hypoglycaemic feeling. Rule: move to a controlled environment, fuel and hydrate; if this recurs, stop and seek care.
- **Returning on the life schedule.** Sleep deprivation and caregiving load reduce fitness and tissue tolerance. Rule: check sleep, energy and mood before deciding whether to add volume this week; see [Sleep and recovery](/en/knowledge/sleep-recovery/) and [Post-race recovery](/en/knowledge/post-race-recovery/).
- **Using watch pace or a past PR as the target.** Heart-rate response can be blunted in pregnancy; pace reference drops in value. Rule: follow the talk test and RPE.

## Red flags and care-seeking boundary

This page is triage education, not diagnosis. Stop exercise and contact obstetric care for the signals below; loss of consciousness, severe chest pain, marked breathlessness, seizure, continuously worsening abdominal pain or heavy bleeding are emergencies:

- Vaginal bleeding; abdominal pain; regular painful contractions; amniotic fluid leakage;
- Breathlessness before exertion; dizziness; headache; chest pain; weakness affecting balance; calf pain or swelling;
- Markedly abnormal fetal movement (contact obstetric care; do not judge from online information);
- Ongoing or increased bleeding unrelated to menstruation after birth, pelvic bulge sensation, pain with intercourse;
- Anyone with pre-existing cardiovascular, respiratory, hypertensive or diabetic conditions obtains clinical clearance before activity.

## Applicable boundaries

This page is general health and activity education, not a diagnosis, treatment prescription, training plan or medication advice. The 150-minute figure is a population recommendation, not your personal training target. Goom 2019 is an expert-consensus document with a limited evidence base, used to support clinical reasoning. People with pregnancy complications, multiples, threatened symptoms, prior injuries or surgical history must be assessed by obstetric, maternal-fetal or pelvic rehabilitation professionals before activity choices. This site does not interpret imaging or replace a sports physician, physiotherapist or coach.

## What would change this advice

- **Complications and history:** gestational diabetes, hypertension, bleeding history, cervical issues, multiples, prior pelvic injury or caesarean complications move the conclusion from "activity can progress" to "clinical assessment first".
- **Delivery mode and recovery:** vaginal versus caesarean birth, perineal tears or surgical complications shape early activity; wound healing and lochia are hard preconditions.
- **Evidence update:** high-quality postpartum return-to-running research is still accumulating; if later high-quality reviews or official guidance adjust the window and screening standards, this page updates at review.
- **Environment:** heat, humidity, altitude or travel change thermal and circulatory load, and activity must be downgraded accordingly; see [Heat and humidity](/en/knowledge/heat-and-humidity/).
- **Lactation and life events:** sleep loss, caregiving pressure and energy deficit reduce tolerance and take priority over mileage.
