## Definition and who this applies to

The long run is the clearly longer easy or endurance run of the week. It practises sustained movement, fuelling rhythm, route judgement and control under fatigue. It is not “the farther the more serious”, and not a weekly maximum test; its value comes from repeatable, recoverable training.

This applies to runners who already complete shorter runs consistently and are preparing for a marathon, trail race or general endurance. The condition is that length may grow only when current volume does not affect normal activity the next day; with frequent pain, poor sleep or accumulating fatigue, maintain or reduce.

## What the long run is for

It develops aerobic endurance, practises eating and drinking while moving, builds route and kit familiarity, and trains judgement under fatigue. It matters for marathon or trail preparation; for 5K, 10K or health-focused running, a longer controlled easy run may be enough.

## Length is relative to your base

Length depends on weekly volume, goal distance, surface and recovery capacity. A common approach is the longer easy run of the week, not a sudden doubling. Stabilise frequency and easy-run time before adding length, and let someone who knows your history adjust the distance.

## What share of weekly volume: no fixed recipe

How much of the week the long run should take depends on your base, goal event and recovery; there is no universal percentage. In the worked example below, a 16 km long run in a 40 km week is 16÷40 = 40% of weekly volume. For a less experienced runner on a smaller week, the same 40% may already be too much; for a well-built runner in a race block, one long run can make up a lower share because the week contains several sessions. A systematic review pooling 36 studies and 23,047 runners found conflicting evidence linking weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity or recent changes to injury, and cautioned against one optimal progression for everyone. A share is therefore a reference for reviewing your own plan, not a safety ceiling or floor.

Most endurance training stays low intensity, with a small number of sessions at higher intensity; the long run is aerobic accumulation and should not be stacked on top of a quality session on the same day. See the [marathon training plan](/en/knowledge/marathon-training-plan/), [trail and ultra training plan](/en/knowledge/trail-ultra-training-plan/) and [polarized 80/20 training](/en/knowledge/polarized-training-80-20/), with progression rules in [training volume and progression](/en/knowledge/training-volume-and-progression/).

## Practise fuelling and route judgement together

The ISSN nutrient-timing position stand says carbohydrate timing and amount around exercise can affect performance and recovery, but guidance must fit exercise demand, duration and individual tolerance. The NHS notes that long activity or heat can increase fluid needs. Do not debut a new gel, drink or breakfast on race day.

Choose routes with water, escape points, low traffic risk and familiarity. A GPX shows geometry only, not closures, water or phone coverage; check the weather and leave a trip plan.

## How to design a long run in three steps

1. **Set one purpose.** Sustained movement, fuelling practice, climbing or simulating the late race: choose one.
2. **Set route and fuelling.** Pick water, escape points and low traffic risk; carry tested food and fluid, and plan the way home.
3. **Set recovery and review.** Schedule an easy day or rest, record sleep, next-day pain and movement quality, then decide whether the next run stays, shortens or grows.

## Sample week layout

| Position | Example arrangement | Main purpose | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Day before | Easy run or rest | Preserve glycogen and focus | No stacked hard session |
| Long-run day | Longer easy run with fuelling practice | Sustained movement and route judgement | No sprint finish |
| Day after | Rest or very short easy run | Absorption and recovery | Extend rest if pain worsens |
| Other days | Mostly easy, at most one quality session | Maintain frequency | Keep quality away from the long run |

## Worked example: distance within volume and pace (assumed)

Assume a weekly volume of 40 kilometres and a long run of 16 kilometres, which is 16÷40 = 40% of the week. At 6 minutes 30 seconds per kilometre, 16 kilometres takes about 16×6.5 = 104 minutes; at the same pace, 2 hours (120 minutes) covers about 120÷6.5 ≈ 18.5 kilometres. These figures are hypothetical; 40% is not a recommended share.

## Common pitfalls

- **Treating the long run as a weekly test.** Running to the limit squeezes recovery.
- **Doubling distance suddenly.** A single jump adds a large load.
- **Longer is always better.** Distance must serve the purpose and recovery; one ultra-long run does not automatically build endurance.
- **Debuting new fuelling on race day.** Gut and kit need training verification.
- **Using a fixed percentage as proof of safety.** The evidence is conflicting.

## Frequency, cycle and progression

The long run is usually weekly, but how often and how far should follow the phase. An illustrative rhythm: in a base phase, review every 2–3 weeks and extend only when recovery is good; before a race, stabilise the longest run near the goal, then taper. Progression is a cycle of increase, stabilise and review.

A systematic review pooled 36 studies and 23,047 runners, of whom 26.2% reported a running-related injury. Evidence linking weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity or recent changes to injury was conflicting, and the authors caution against one optimal progression. Judge by fatigue, sleep, pain and movement quality.

## When to stop, shorten or seek care

Do not force a long run with fever, significant pain, unusual palpitations or chest pain, dizziness, or persistent vomiting or diarrhoea; seek medical help when needed. In severe weather, on unfamiliar routes, without reliable fuelling or when you cannot return before dark, shortening or postponing is more sensible. If fatigue, pain or poor sleep persists, keep or reduce the next run.

## Before and after checklist

- [ ] The long run has one main purpose, written down.
- [ ] The route has water, escape points and low traffic risk.
- [ ] Fuelling and kit are already tested in training.
- [ ] Weather is checked, someone knows the plan, and an offline route is loaded.
- [ ] An easy day or rest is scheduled for the next day.

## Scope and limits

This page does not prescribe an individual distance or promise that a distance improves performance or prevents injury. A plan should account for health, injury history, goal and recovery; consult a qualified clinician with a medical condition. The long run does not replace speed work, strength work or sleep.

## Three kinds of long run: base, specific, back-to-back

“Long run” is not one distance. It is three different jobs. A base long run builds time tolerance and aerobic foundations; a specific long run rehearses race terrain, fuelling and pace; a back-to-back pair practises running again on tired legs. They carry different recovery costs and should not all be maximal every week.

| Type | Purpose | Typical session | Recovery cost | When |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Base | Time tolerance, fat oxidation, form | Easy pace, 25–35% of weekly volume | Moderate | Base phase, every 1–2 weeks |
| Specific | Rehearse course, fuel, kit, pace | Includes target terrain/pace block | High | 8–12 weeks out, every 2–3 weeks |
| Back-to-back | Run again tired, night and load | Two consecutive days, shorter second | Very high | 100K/100 mi, sparingly |

A base long run is not “too easy to matter”. It should leave you able to do an easy run the next day. If one long run needs two or three days to recover, it has become a specific or excessive session.

## Distance ceilings by race

| Target race | Longest single | Longest back-to-back | Key limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5K/10K | 10–14 km | Not needed | Quality first |
| Half | 16–18 km | Not needed | Taper last two weeks |
| Marathon | 28–32 km or 2.5–3 h | Usually not needed | Recovery and fuelling |
| 50K | 30–35 km | Optional two days | Climb and time |
| 100K | 35–45 km | Two days, 15–25 km second | Night, load, feet |
| 100 mi | 40–50 km | Two days, 20–30 km second | Sleep, kit, crew |

These are training ceilings, not substitutes for race distance. Beyond three hours the recovery cost rises sharply; if you are slow, cap by time rather than kilometres.

## Rehearse fuel, kit and navigation together

The long run is the only session that tests eating, wearing, navigating and carrying at once. Change one variable at a time: a new gel this week, a headlamp next, load after that. Do not change shoes, pack and fuel in the same run.

| Rehearsal point | What to verify | Failure signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hour 1 | Breakfast and first carbohydrate | Reflux, side stitch |
| Hours 2–3 | 30–60 g/h tolerance | Nausea, bloating |
| Hour 4+ | Solid/liquid, sodium, caffeine | Dizziness, vomiting, gait change |
| Night | Primary/backup light, warm layer | Cannot see, hands stiff |
| Final hour | Last fuel and finishing | No appetite, cold |

## Two worked examples

**First marathoner, 50 km weekly, 4:30 goal.** Start the long run at 24 km, add 2–4 km every two weeks, peak at 32 km; peak weekly volume is about 55–60 km, with the long run around 55%. Taper to 24 km and 16 km in the last two weeks. Run 30–60 s/km slower than goal marathon pace; every third week include 8–10 km at goal pace.

**100K runner, 1,500 m weekly climb.** Peak weekend: 35 km plus 20 km the next day. Day one includes 1,200 m climb and two hours at night; day two is walk-run, testing “eat while tired”. Do only two or three back-to-backs, then at least ten days without another. Every night kit item must be used in training, not opened on race day.

## Failure modes and stop rules

- **Adding distance every week:** long runs are not a weekly volume contest; keep a down week every 2–3 weeks.
- **Using the long run to replace missed easy runs:** piling volume into one day raises recovery cost.
- **Longest run three weeks out:** peak 3–4 weeks before the race, then taper.
- **Race pace for the whole long run:** specific blocks are a portion, not the entire session.
- **Persistent pain, dizziness, vomiting, cold:** stop and manage it; “the long run must be completed” is not a rule.

## The 48 hours after

After the long run, replace fluid, carbohydrate and protein, then sleep. The next day is walking, an easy run or rest. Use morning heart rate, perceived fatigue and pain rather than pace to judge recovery. If you are still heavy-legged, sleeping poorly or more painful 48 hours later, cut next week by 20–30% instead of forcing another long run.
