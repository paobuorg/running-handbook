## Definition and scope

Heat-and-humidity running means training or racing in high temperature, high humidity, strong sun or poor airflow. It is for summer runners and anyone racing in heat. It applies when you know your medical conditions and medication, the route has water and exits, and you will adjust the goal by feel. One case where it holds: a 30 to 60 minute easy run in the second week of heat adaptation.

## Heat illness is a spectrum

The National Weather Service lists heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea, headache and fainting as heat-exhaustion signs. Heat stroke is an emergency and can involve confusion, slurred speech, a very high body temperature, a rapid pulse and possible loss of consciousness. Heat exhaustion usually still allows standing and heavy sweating; heat stroke involves altered consciousness and abnormal temperature, so call emergency services first and cool while waiting.

## Why humidity is harder than temperature

When humidity is high, sweat evaporates slowly and heat loss becomes less efficient, so the same temperature costs more. Wind, sun, clothing and radiant heat also change the load. "Normal pace" is not a valid reference: a dry morning and a humid afternoon make the same route two different sessions.

## Adjust time, route and intensity

Avoid the hottest, sunniest part of the day; choose early morning, evening, shade, airflow or indoors. On first entering heat, use lower intensity and shorter duration. Judge intensity with the CDC talk test: an easy run should allow full sentences; if the old pace leaves you able to say only a few words before a breath, you are in vigorous territory.

## Fluid and electrolytes

No single fluid volume suits everyone. The NHS suggests using urine colour, aiming for clear pale yellow, as a daily guide, and notes that long activity or a hot environment may increase needs. Combine thirst, sweat rate, aid stations and tolerance; large amounts at once are not safer than small, frequent drinks. Electrolyte drinks can replace some salt, but do not debut a concentrated drink or salt tablets on race day.

## How to: three steps with cooling first

1. Check before leaving: temperature, humidity, sun, wind and air quality; choose time and route before distance.
2. Reduce load while running: start clearly easier for 10 minutes, control by the talk test, sip every 20 minutes, and stop for dizziness, chills or nausea.
3. Cool down first: slow before finishing, move to shade, drink, loosen clothing and use cool water; skip the stuffy room and hot shower.

## Table: adjust to the heat load

| Condition | Adjustment |
| --- | --- |
| High humidity, little wind | Shorter distance, early morning or indoors |
| Direct strong sun | Shaded route, hat |
| Few aid stations | Carry water or use a loop |
| Hot race day | Change the goal to steady completion |
| Dizziness or chills | Stop and cool immediately |

## Worked example: 10 km in humid heat (hypothetical)

All figures are hypothetical: 32 °C and 80% relative humidity, which puts the site's heat-adjustment tool at a load score of about 35.2, its top band. A planned 10 km is normally run in 60 minutes. Hold an intensity that allows full sentences; at 35 to 40 seconds per kilometre slower (roughly 10%–11%), the run takes about 66 to 67 minutes. Sip 150 ml every 20 minutes, about 450 ml over three checks. If dizziness lasts more than 2 minutes, abandon the remaining distance.

## Common misconceptions

- Judging a hot-day run by normal pace; humidity and sun raise the cost of the same speed.
- Believing heavy sweating proves training quality; sweating is cooling, not a session measure.

## When to stop and seek care

Stop and call emergency services for confusion, slurred speech, fainting, seizures, unusually hot or cold skin, persistent vomiting, chest pain or severe breathlessness. The NWS sequence is to call first and cool while waiting. Do not leave someone alone in the heat, and do not substitute "walk it off" for assessment.

## Frequency and heat adaptation

Build adaptation by the week: first week at 60% to 70% of normal duration and lower intensity; second week approaching normal duration; third week considering quality work. Three to four exposures a week work better than one heroic session. Adaptation fades, so keep 1 to 2 weeks of exposure before a hot race.

## Check yourself

- Temperature, humidity and sun were checked, timing adjusted.
- The route has shade, water and an exit.
- The first 10 minutes were clearly easier, with full sentences possible.

## Scope and limits

This page does not provide heat-stroke treatment or medical clearance. Heat illness is a medical emergency; those with a medical condition, medication, pregnancy or prior heat illness should ask a clinician.
