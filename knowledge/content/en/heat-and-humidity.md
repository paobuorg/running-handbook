## Conclusion first

The core message of running in heat and humidity is: **bring heat-illness risk down before chasing pace; the same route on a dry morning and a humid afternoon is two different sessions, and "normal pace" is not a valid reference.** The ACSM 2021 expert consensus on exertional heat illness identifies exertional heat stroke (EHS) as a true medical emergency with potential for organ injury and death; risk rises with environmental heat stress and exercise intensity, and activity should be modified or canceled in high-risk environments. The standing position on this site: lower intensity, change the timing, drink to conditions, and recognize heat-illness signals before sticking to the original plan.

Three actions come first. First, check temperature, humidity, sun, wind and air quality before leaving; decide time and route before distance. Second, start clearly easy in heat, hold intensity by the talk test, and sip fluid about every 20 min. Third, dizziness, chills, nausea, altered consciousness or unusually hot skin mean stop, cool down, and treat it as an emergency.

This page is safety education and coaching advice, not heat-stroke treatment or medical clearance. Those with underlying disease, medication, pregnancy or prior heat illness should be assessed by a clinician first.

## Definition and premises

- **Heat stress:** the total heat the body must shed, combining exercise heat production with environmental cooling conditions; temperature, humidity, wind, sun and clothing all enter it.
- **Heat exhaustion:** heavy sweating with weakness, dizziness, nausea, headache or faintness; usually still able to stand and sweating heavily. It is heat-related distress that requires stopping.
- **Heat stroke (EHS):** a true emergency that may involve confusion, slurred speech, a very high body temperature, rapid pulse and possible loss of consciousness. The ACSM consensus lists central nervous system changes together with rectal temperature above 40°C as the primary identifying signs.
- **Heat acclimatization:** the process of improving cooling capacity and tolerance through repeated heat exposure; adaptation fades after interruption.
- **WBGT (wet bulb globe temperature):** a heat-stress index combining temperature, humidity, wind speed and solar radiation. The **heat index** combines air temperature and relative humidity only, excluding wind and radiation.

This page covers summer training, racing in heat, and road, trail and ultra running in humid conditions. Cold-environment issues are in [Cold-weather running](/en/knowledge/cold-weather-running/); altitude cooling challenges are in [Running at altitude](/en/knowledge/altitude-and-running/); combined air-quality risk is in [Air quality and running](/en/knowledge/air-quality-and-running/).

## Why humidity is harder than temperature

When humidity is high, sweat evaporates slowly and cooling becomes less efficient, so the same temperature costs more. Wind, sun, clothing and radiant heat also change the load. The NWS heat-index explanation is explicit: the heat index combines air temperature and relative humidity but does not account for wind speed, cloud cover or radiant heat; WBGT combines temperature, humidity, wind speed and solar radiation from wet bulb, globe and dry bulb temperatures. In other words, under the same air temperature, a cloudy breezy day and a still sunny day are two different loads.

Wind is not uniformly helpful: a 2025 Journal of Applied Physiology study found metabolic rate and oxygen demand showed a strong linear relation with the square of relative air speed, with differences becoming significant at about 4 m/s and above; headwind and tailwind relationships differ, and heart rate shows a moderate linear relation with relative wind speed. Wind usually aids cooling, but a strong headwind also raises metabolic cost; the two are weighed together.

## Environmental measures: WBGT, heat index and the site proxy

| Measure | Factors combined | Excluded / boundary | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Air temperature | Dry bulb temperature | No humidity, wind or sun | Basic reference; cannot set heat risk alone |
| Heat index | Air temperature + relative humidity | No wind speed, cloud cover or radiant heat | NWS high-risk grading; apparent temperature in humid conditions |
| WBGT | Temperature, humidity, wind speed, solar radiation (wet bulb, globe, dry bulb components) | Needs a station or on-site measurement; runners usually cannot self-measure | Occupational and event-medical risk grading and rest–work cycles |
| Site heat-load score (simplified proxy) | Temperature + 0.04 × relative humidity | Not an official WBGT; does not replace forecasts or medical judgment | Quick pre-training grading; tool convention in the [heat-adjustment tool](/en/tools/heat-adjust/) |

OSHA heat-hazard guidance splits heat-stress limits into WBGT bands for light, moderate, heavy and very heavy workload; it treats workers on the job for less than one to two weeks as unacclimatized, with acclimatization built up through repeated heat exposure. Running is not occupational labor, but the idea of grading by workload and confirming acclimatization first transfers as a risk reference.

## Heat-load tiers and downgrade decision table

| Heat-load condition (if) | Adjustment (then) | Evidence boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Site score below 24, comfortable, low humidity, breezy | Train as planned; drink by rhythm | Simplified proxy; official forecast prevails |
| Score 24–29, muggy or strong sun | Shorten distance, move to early morning or evening, choose shaded, breezy route; start easy, hold intensity by talk test | NWS/OSHA: actively modify activity as environmental heat stress rises |
| Score at or above 30, hot and humid or still sun | Cut pace, reduce duration or move indoors; carry water if aid gaps are long; target a steady finish | ACSM consensus: modify or cancel in high-risk environments; Beal 2022: start more conservatively in heat |
| Route lacks shade, water and exits | No long or high-intensity session; route-safety judgment in [Route safety](/en/knowledge/route-safety/) | With risk factors stacking, water and exit options take priority over pace |
| Dizziness, chills, nausea, involuntary muscle twitching | Stop immediately, move to shade and cool; watch for red-flag progression | NWS: heat-exhaustion signs need to stop; do not push through |

## Heat illness is a spectrum: exhaustion versus heat stroke

The U.S. National Weather Service lists heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea, headache and fainting as heat-exhaustion signs. Heat stroke is an emergency and can involve confusion, slurred speech, a very high body temperature, a rapid pulse and possible loss of consciousness. The key dividing lines are consciousness state and temperature: heat exhaustion usually still allows standing and heavy sweating; heat stroke involves altered consciousness and abnormal high temperature.

The ACSM 2021 consensus adds the recognition boundary: subtle to profound central nervous system changes together with a rectal temperature above 40°C are the primary identifying signs of EHS; rectal temperature helps differentiate EHS from other causes of collapse during or after exercise. In the field without a thermometer, altered consciousness itself is the signal to stop and call emergency services; absence of a measured 40°C does not rule out heat stroke.

## Recognition and on-site handling: from stopping activity to transport

The ACSM consensus chain of survival is rapid recognition, management and communication between care teams: with suspected EHS, stop activity immediately to stop body heat generation, provide rapid total-body cooling and call medical support; delayed recognition and insufficient or delayed cooling can cause complications including death. Cold water immersion has the best recorded cooling rates and clinical outcomes and is the preferred cooling strategy; when it is not possible, use the fastest cooling technique available on site.

The IOC Tokyo 2020 adverse-weather working group adds the competition prehospital sequence: events under high environmental heat stress or with high metabolic demand should adopt an EHS management policy; the basic principles are early recognition, early diagnosis, rapid on-site cooling and advanced clinical care. After diagnosis, cool on site until rectal temperature falls below 39°C; blood analysis to rule out hyponatremia or hypoglycemia may run while cooling if safe, but must not interrupt cooling. Only after on-site treatment is the athlete transported to advanced care for full evaluation; coordination across venue medical staff, transport and hospital is required.

What runners can do personally: stop running, move to shade, loosen clothing, pour cool water or apply wet towels, fan; if conscious, drink small amounts; with altered consciousness, seizure or collapse, do not give fluids or medication by mouth, wait for emergency services. Do not leave someone alone in the heat.

## Worked example: 10 km in humid heat (teaching assumption)

Assumptions: 32°C air temperature, 80% relative humidity. The site simplified heat-load score = 32 + 0.04 × 80 = 35.2, the top band (at or above 30 is extreme risk). The runner normally finishes 10 km in about 60 min.

- Intensity: hold a pace allowing full sentences; by the tool convention this load means about 35–40 s/km slower (roughly 10%–11%), giving about 66–67 min rather than a 60-min target.
- Fluid: about 150 mL every 20 min, roughly 450 mL over three checks; the actual amount follows sweat rate, per [Hydration](/en/knowledge/hydration/) and the [sweat-rate tool](/en/tools/sweat-rate/).
- Stop rule: if dizziness lasts more than 2 min, abandon the remaining distance; altered consciousness, vomiting, unusually hot or cold skin mean immediate emergency handling.

The score in this example is a site proxy, not an official WBGT. Before a formal race or long session, check the official heat forecast, WBGT or heat-index forecast.

## Heat acclimatization: duration, rhythm and fade

Acclimatization reduces heat-illness risk and improves performance in the heat (ACSM consensus, prevention). The training convention builds by week: week one at 60%–70% of normal duration and lower intensity; week two approaching normal duration; week three considering quality work again. Three to four exposures per week work better than one heroic session. Adaptation fades, so keep one to two weeks of repeated exposure before a hot race.

Duration positions differ and are presented side by side: OSHA occupational guidance treats anyone on the job for less than one to two weeks as unacclimatized; sports-science literature generally places full acclimatization at one to two or more weeks of repeated heat exposure, but the exact number of days depends on exposure intensity, duration, humidity, individual baseline and active heat bathing. The ACSM consensus confirms acclimatization reduces risk and improves performance, but the claims verified here do not give a single day count. Rapid acclimatization protocols (such as pre-race hot baths) are specialized arrangements tied to personal health status; this page does not copy them as a prescription. Scheduling detail is in the [heat-acclimatization planner](/en/tools/heat-acclimation-planner/).

## Pace, wind and race evidence: the Doha case

Beal 2022's analysis of the 2019 Doha World Championships women's marathon gives one hot-race sample: the race ran at 32°C and 74% relative humidity with a 41% failure rate; average pace was 14.82 ± 0.96 km/h, slower than London's 15.74 ± 0.96 km/h (p < .001). The top 10 started at 93.7% ± 2.1% of personal-best pace on average, and ranks 21–40 at 96.6% ± 2.8%; groups showed no difference before 15 km, after which the slower groups slowed immediately and the faster group after 20 km. Finishers and non-finishers showed no pace difference before 10 km, so pace cannot explain all withdrawals; the authors recommend a more conservative start in heat to improve completion and performance.

Read this evidence with care: it is an observational analysis of a single event, and the abstract reports no WBGT. It supports a conservative start in heat but cannot set a precise personal pace reduction. The site tool's 10%–11% slowdown is a training reference band, not a race guarantee.

## Combining fluid, electrolytes and cooling

Fluid needs rise in heat, but the position matches [Hydration](/en/knowledge/hydration/): small divided doses, checked by thirst and weighing; do not force fluids for performance. Consider electrolyte drinks in long or heavy-sweating sessions; the boundary between sodium, cramps and hyponatremia is in [Electrolytes and sodium](/en/knowledge/electrolytes-and-sodium/). Cooling measures include shaded, breezy routes, a hat, loose clothing, cool water on the neck and skin, and avoiding stuffy rooms and hot showers after the session.

## Failure modes and early signals

- **Holding a cool-day pace:** early signals are abnormal heart-rate drift, a sudden jump in sweating, heavy legs, dizziness. Rule: start 10 min easy, hold intensity by the talk test.
- **Checking only temperature, not humidity and wind:** early signals are stuffier breathing and early fatigue at the same temperature. Rule: read temperature, humidity, sun, wind and air quality together; set timing and route first.
- **No acclimatization on first hot-weather trip or seasonal change:** early signals are nausea, fatigue and poor heat tolerance within a short time. Rule: build duration and intensity by week, keep one to two weeks of exposure before the race; see the [heat-acclimatization planner](/en/tools/heat-acclimation-planner/).
- **Continuing to run with early heat-illness signs:** early signals are chills, shivering, worsening headache, visual disturbance, poor coordination. Rule: stop immediately, cool and observe; do not "hold on another kilometre".
- **First new drink or cooling aid on race day:** early signals are GI distress layered on heat stress. Rule: drinks, hat, ice towels and other aids are all validated in training first.

## Red flags: call emergency services

With any of the following, stop exercise and call emergency services: confusion, slurred speech, seizure, fainting, unusually hot or cold skin, persistent vomiting, chest pain, severe breathlessness, collapse. While waiting, move to shade and cool as fast as possible; do not leave the person alone, do not substitute "walk it off" for assessment, and do not give fluids or medication by mouth while consciousness is impaired. After the race, tea-colored urine, severe muscle pain or marked swelling calls for rhabdomyolysis assessment; see [Blood in urine after running](/en/knowledge/blood-in-urine-after-running/).

## Applicable boundaries

This page is safety education and coaching advice, not a heat-stroke treatment plan, medical diagnosis or race clearance. WBGT, heat index and the site score are risk-grading tools; they cannot measure an individual's core temperature. Observational race analyses, consensus stands and official guidance are flagged separately by evidence type. Cardiovascular disease, metabolic disease, pregnancy, prior heat illness, or medication affecting cooling or fluid/sodium balance call for clinical assessment before hot-weather training and racing. This site does not measure core temperature in the field or replace emergency judgment.

## What would change this advice

- **Season and location:** seasonal change, first hot-weather trip, altitude, or sea-breeze/dry-humidity shifts all change cooling conditions; reassess acclimatization on entering a new environment.
- **Event arrangements:** hot and humid race editions may shift start time, aid-station placement or cancel; follow the event's official medical rules; see [Cutoff and withdrawal management](/en/knowledge/cutoff-management/).
- **Personal state:** poor sleep, low energy intake, dehydration, post-illness recovery and prior heat history all lower heat tolerance.
- **Evidence update:** acclimatization protocols, cooling-method effectiveness and event heat-risk thresholds remain under study; the page adjusts if later high-quality consensus or guidelines differ.
- **Disease and medication:** underlying conditions or drugs change the safe range for cooling and fluid/sodium; clinical assessment takes priority over the general grading here.
