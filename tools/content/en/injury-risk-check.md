## What the tool is for

Running pain is often mishandled: runners either push through it or stop completely out of fear. This checklist gathers common signals to help you decide between watching symptoms and seeking assessment. It suits runners who already train regularly and want a direction when mild pain appears; it can also serve as an outline for describing symptoms to a professional at a later appointment.

It gives no diagnosis and cannot rule out injury. A review of 36 studies and 23,047 runners found that 26.2% reported a running-related injury, but evidence linking training parameters to injury is conflicting. The checklist can remind you to watch signals, but it cannot prove a body part is "fine" or "affected". NHS advice is more direct: do not run while in pain, and seek medical or physiotherapy advice when pain is severe, persistent or accompanied by swelling.

## Inputs and outputs

There are seven inputs: pain during running, pain at rest or while walking, swelling, night or morning pain, recent weekly volume change percent, recent sleep hours per night, and prior injury in the same area. Outputs are the self-check score, the band (low, moderate, high), whether a red flag is present, and an advice message. The score sums a few experience-based weights, and the band only maps that score onto three prompts. The red flag is judged separately and outranks the total. The tool outputs no imaging conclusion, injury name, recovery timeline or individualized exercise prescription.

## Formula and rules

| Item | Condition | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain during running | yes | 1 |
| Pain at rest or while walking | yes | 2 |
| Swelling | yes | 2 |
| Night or morning pain | yes | 1 |
| Volume change | above +10% | 1 |
| Sleep | below 7 hours | 1 |
| Prior injury in the same area | yes | 1 |

The total runs from 0 to 9: 0–2 low, 3–4 moderate, 5 or above high. Rest pain, walking pain or swelling sets a red flag and the message starts with a seek-assessment prompt. The weights, the 10% volume threshold and the 7-hour sleep line are experience-based heuristics, not a clinical prediction rule. Volume change is a percentage: +12 means a 12% increase, −10 a decrease; a reduction never adds points.

## Worked example 1: low band

Example one: pain during running and a prior injury in the same area, otherwise no; volume +6%, sleep 7.5 hours. Score is 1 + 1 = 2, a low band with no red flag.

| Item | Value | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain during running | yes | 1 |
| Rest/walking pain, swelling, night pain | no | 0 |
| Volume change | +6% | 0 |
| Sleep | 7.5 h | 0 |
| Prior injury same area | yes | 1 |
| Total |  | 2 (low) |

A low band means no red flag was reported, so observation can continue and symptoms can be logged; it does not mean symptoms can be ignored. If pain worsens over the next days or new signals appear, reassess.

## Worked example 2: moderate band

Example two: pain during running, night or morning pain, volume +12%, sleep 6 hours, otherwise no. Score is 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4, a moderate band, still without a red flag.

| Item | Value | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain during running | yes | 1 |
| Night or morning pain | yes | 1 |
| Volume change | +12% | 1 |
| Sleep | 6 h | 1 |
| Rest/walking pain, swelling, prior injury | no | 0 |
| Total |  | 4 (moderate) |

A moderate band suggests reducing load, cutting volume or intensity and observing. If pain persists, worsens or comes with swelling, seek medical or physiotherapy assessment.

## Worked example 3: high band with red flag

Example three: pain during running, pain at rest or while walking, swelling, volume +15%, sleep 5.5 hours; night pain no, prior injury no. Item scores: run pain 1 + rest/walk pain 2 + swelling 2 + volume above +10% 1 + sleep below 7 h 1 = 7, a high band. Rest/walk pain and swelling are both true, so the red flag is true.

| Item | Value | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain during running | yes | 1 |
| Rest or walking pain | yes | 2 |
| Swelling | yes | 2 |
| Night or morning pain | no | 0 |
| Volume change | +15% | 1 |
| Sleep | 5.5 h | 1 |
| Prior injury same area | no | 0 |
| Total |  | 7 (high), red flag true |

This case shows the red flag and the total are two separate lines: even at a modest score, rest/walking pain or swelling means stop running and seek assessment. The checklist gives no individualized exercise prescription; rehabilitation and return-to-running decisions follow professional assessment.

## Red flags and the boundary for seeking care

The following situations skip the checklist score: stop running and seek medical or physiotherapy assessment directly. These include pain at rest or while walking, visible or palpable swelling, persistent night or morning pain, a changed gait, inability to bear weight, visible deformity, rapidly worsening redness heat or swelling, fever, and severe pain after acute trauma. Joint deformity, inability to move, numbness or coldness, major swelling or any altered-consciousness symptom should be treated as an emergency and emergency services called. A red flag means "further examination is needed", not "a specific injury has been diagnosed"; diagnosis, imaging and recovery plans are completed clinically.

## Rounding and precision

Points are whole numbers, so tolerance is 0. Volume change and sleep are used only for threshold comparisons: exactly +10% adds nothing, exactly 7 hours adds nothing. The bands are inclusive: 0–2 low, 3–4 moderate, 5 and above high. The inputs rely on your own recollection; the main source of error is judging whether something counts as pain, not the arithmetic.

## How to read the result

A low band means observation can continue, not that symptoms can be ignored. A moderate band suggests reducing load and monitoring; with severe, persistent pain or swelling, seek advice. A high band means several factors are present: reduce load and seek assessment if symptoms persist. Rest pain, walking pain or swelling outranks the total score, because NHS advises not to run in pain and to seek care when pain is severe, persistent or accompanied by swelling.

Persistent fatigue and a performance decline should also prompt consideration of overtraining syndrome and exclusion of underlying illness; that assessment is clinical and includes rest plus correction of the imbalance between training and recovery.

## Common pitfalls

The first pitfall is reading a low score as permission to keep running: the score is an experience-based checklist, and red flags and how you feel outrank the number. The second is using the score to name a specific injury: the checklist neither diagnoses nor rules out damage. The third is looking only at volume change; sleep, prior injury and pain pattern also enter the judgement. The fourth is treating the checklist as a return-to-run plan: when and how to resume running, and at what intensity, follow professional assessment rather than the score.

## Limits

This tool is for self-observation by general runners. It does not suit acute trauma, inability to bear weight, visible deformity, fever, rapidly worsening redness or swelling, or post-operative rehabilitation; those go directly to care. It does not diagnose a specific injury, cannot rule one out, and does not decide when to return to running; resumed training needs professional assessment. The checklist cannot replace examination, imaging or medical advice, and cannot be used as proof that it is safe to keep running. Children, pregnant runners and runners with prior serious injury should consult a professional first. The tool gives no medical diagnosis and no exercise prescription.

## How to use it

1. Answer the seven questions for the past week; express volume change as a percentage and take sleep as the recent nightly average.
2. Read the score and band; check rest pain, walking pain or swelling first, then the total.
3. Keep logging in a low band, reduce load at moderate or high, and with a red flag stop running and seek assessment.
