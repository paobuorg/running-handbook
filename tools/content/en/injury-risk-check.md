## What the tool is for

Running pain is often mishandled: runners either push through it or stop completely out of fear. This checklist gathers common signals to help you decide between watching symptoms and seeking assessment.

It gives no diagnosis and cannot rule out injury. A review of 36 studies and 23,047 runners found that 26.2% reported a running-related injury, but evidence linking training parameters to injury is conflicting. NHS advice is more direct: do not run while in pain, and seek medical or physiotherapy advice when pain is severe, persistent or accompanied by swelling.

## Formula and rules

| Item | Condition | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain during running | yes | 1 |
| Pain at rest or while walking | yes | 2 |
| Swelling | yes | 2 |
| Night or morning pain | yes | 1 |
| Volume change | above +10% | 1 |
| Sleep | below 7 hours | 1 |
| Prior injury in the same area | yes | 1 |

The total runs from 0 to 9: 0–2 low, 3–4 moderate, 5 or above high. Rest pain, walking pain or swelling sets a red flag and the message starts with a seek-assessment prompt. The weights, the 10% volume threshold and the 7-hour sleep line are experience-based heuristics, not a clinical prediction rule. Volume change is a percentage: +12 means a 12% increase, −10 a decrease; a reduction never adds points.

## Worked example

With pain during running, night pain, volume +12% and sleep 6 hours, but no rest pain or swelling: points are 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4, a moderate band without a red flag.

## Rounding and precision

Points are whole numbers, so tolerance is 0. Volume change and sleep are used only for threshold comparisons: exactly +10% adds nothing, exactly 7 hours adds nothing.

## How to read the result

A low band means observation can continue, not that symptoms can be ignored. A moderate band suggests reducing load and monitoring; with severe, persistent pain or swelling, seek advice. A high band means several factors are present: reduce load and seek assessment.

Persistent fatigue and a performance decline should also prompt consideration of overtraining syndrome and exclusion of underlying illness; that assessment is clinical and includes rest plus correction of the imbalance. NHS advises proper fitted shoes while noting expensive ones are not essential, but a shoe change does not replace assessment.

## How to use it

1. Answer the seven questions for the past week.
2. Read the score and band; check rest pain, walking pain or swelling first.
3. Keep logging in a low band, reduce load at moderate or high, and with a red flag stop running and seek assessment.

## Limits

It is for self-observation. It does not suit acute trauma, inability to bear weight, visible deformity, fever, rapidly worsening redness or swelling, or post-operative rehabilitation.

## When this number misleads you

The score is a sum of experience-based weights and cut-offs, not a validated prediction model: the same situation answered by two people can land in different bands. Recording last week's volume 20% wrong, or treating a race week as a normal week, moves the load term directly. The dangerous mistake is reading a low score as permission to keep running: pain at rest, night pain, swelling or a changed gait outrank any score.

Do not use it to diagnose or rule out an injury, to decide when to return to running, or in place of examination, imaging or a clinician's judgement. It only points at load factors worth reviewing and is not medical advice.
