## Definition and preconditions

Night traffic risk is defined as collision risk when a driver detects a runner too late after dark. This page applies to city streets, commuter routes and poorly lit rural roads. It holds when vehicles are moving, drivers rely on vision, and the runner controls visibility and route.

## Visibility matters more than fitness

NHTSA data cited by FHWA note that almost fifty percent of pedestrian fatalities occur between 6:00 p.m. and midnight; eight out of ten drivers who struck people at night reported not seeing them; dark clothing may first be seen about 55 feet away. At ordinary road speeds that leaves little reaction time. What matters is how much light returns to the driver's eyes.

## Reflective placement and active light

Reflectors send light back toward its source and are easier to detect under headlights than ordinary light fabric. Put a vest on the torso and small reflectors on ankles, wrists and swinging arms. A headlamp helps you see the surface and helps oncoming traffic notice you; a red rear light helps traffic behind. Check batteries and carry a spare.

## How to do it: three actions before leaving

1. Choose a lit, familiar route with pavements or wide shoulders and lower speeds; count crossings in advance.
2. Put reflectors on moving parts, switch on active light and confirm the rear light.
3. Use one earbud or transparency mode, wait for vehicles to slow or stop before crossing, and tell someone your route and expected return time.

## Route and crossings

Run on the pavement where possible and keep a wider gap where there is none. Cross at marked crossings and signals and never step out between parked cars. At an unmarked crossing, wait until a vehicle has genuinely slowed or stopped. Running against traffic usually improves the view. Slow down near junctions, bridges and slip roads.

| Situation | Preferred | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lit pavement | Stay on the inner side | Stepping into the lane |
| Unmarked crossing | Wait until a vehicle stops | Crossing while on a phone |
| Junction or slip road | Slow and confirm the view | Assuming you were seen |

## Worked example: a 5 km commute

The numbers are hypothetical. Assume a 5 km route with 3 crossings, 2 signalled and 1 not, on a road signed at 40 km/h. A vehicle covers about 11 metres per second; 55 feet is roughly 17 metres, under 2 seconds. If the driver first sees you at 17 metres, there is little room to react and brake. Slow at all 3 crossings, wait for a stop at the unmarked one, and push detection distance earlier with reflectors and light.

## Headphones, company and telling someone

Keep volume low enough to hear traffic, ideally one earbud or transparency mode. Run with someone when possible; alone, share your route and expected return time. If followed or in a collision, move to a lit, populated place and call police.

## Choosing the hour, the surface and the company

The visibility problem is worst in the hours after sunset, when drivers' eyes are still adapting and windscreens are often smeared. If your schedule allows, run before dusk or after the late-evening traffic peak rather than in the busiest twilight window. Prefer lit sidewalks and dedicated paths over shoulders, and cross at junctions with street lighting rather than mid-block. On unlit paths, a chest or waist light that illuminates the surface in front of you matters more than being seen, because uneven pavement and roots are the immediate hazard.

Running with someone else roughly doubles the visible area a driver perceives and gives you a second pair of eyes at crossings. If you run alone, tell a household member the route and expected return time, and carry a phone in a pocket rather than a hand that will be swinging. In cold weather, remember that hats and hoods narrow your own field of view, so turn your head further before crossing.

## Common misconceptions

- Light clothing is enough. Without headlights on it, pale fabric is little safer than dark fabric.
- Drivers should see me. In the cited data, eight out of ten night drivers who struck someone did not.
- Noise-cancelling headphones hide tyre noise, an early warning.

## When to stop, reroute or seek care

Stop or reroute if lights are out, roadworks leave no safe passage, rain or fog cut visibility, vehicles pass too close, you feel followed, or you cannot concentrate. After a collision, a head impact, altered consciousness, inability to bear weight or persistent pain, seek medical care. NPS advice applies: carry enough water and turn back before half is gone; carry a map, compass, headlamp with spare batteries, whistle, first aid and extra layers; GPS may fail, so carry backup navigation.

## Frequency and review

Check gear and route in one minute before each run and review fully every 4 weeks: battery runtime, ageing reflectors, changed lighting, earlier darkness.

## Before-you-go checklist

- Reflectors on ankles, wrists or other moving parts.
- Active light working, spare batteries carried.
- Route and return time shared.
- Phone charged, one earbud or noise cancelling off.

## Scope and limits

No route is guaranteed safe at night, and this page does not replace local law, race rules or police guidance. Vision, hearing, balance or cardiovascular conditions warrant clinical advice.
