## Definition and preconditions

Urban park and greenway running means running on paths inside parks, riverside greenways and connected community networks. It applies to runners using a park as their main training ground. It holds when the site is public, the path continuous, and runners share space with walkers, cyclists and dogs. The task is to complete the session steadily in mixed traffic, not chase a fastest kilometre.

## Surface is a mix, not a single choice

Parks mix asphalt or concrete, rubber track, grass edges and boardwalk. A small study of 30 male habitual rear-foot strikers compared artificial grass, rubber and concrete and found no significant differences in several joint-motion and loading variables; concrete showed lower braking and mediolateral forces but higher ankle moments and frontal-plane hip and knee moments. The authors suggested harder surfaces might raise overuse risk for rear-foot strikers; this was a 30-person short-term mechanics comparison and cannot show that one surface causes injury. Changing surface redistributes load, so do it gradually.

## A greenway is shared space: courtesy before pace

The rule is predictable movement, not runner priority. Warn walkers before overtaking and pass through the space they leave. For a leashed dog, slow down and confirm the owner has control. Look back before changing line near cyclists and e-scooters, signal turns and avoid cutting across narrow paths. One earbud lets you hear warnings; when busy, watch the metres ahead rather than your watch.

## Designing a loop: decide the turn point and water first

A loop avoids repeated road crossings. Mark an early-exit turn point, water locations and each lap's exit, then confirm them on the ground. Keep the turn point inside the first half so you can shorten honestly when form, weather or crowds turn bad, as hikers turn back before half the water is gone. Events and hoardings change routes; follow signs and staff.

## How to do it: three steps

1. Choose the loop and confirm the turn point, water and exits exist on the ground.
2. Run one full lap easy and log surface changes, places to slow, conflict points and slippery sections.
3. Change one variable at a time — laps, pace or share of new surface — and check the next day's response.

## Surface and use

| Surface | Good use | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asphalt or concrete | Main line for easy and long runs | Camber, seams, cyclists |
| Rubber track | Warm-up, intervals, wet substitute | Monotonous laps, inner-bend load |
| Grass and soil edges | Short doses of new stimulus | Holes, slipperiness, roots |
| Boardwalk and stone | Easy running in scenic sections | Wet surfaces, gaps, visitors |

## Worked example: a 6 km park loop

The numbers are hypothetical. Assume a 6 km loop of three 2 km laps, a turn point at 3 km, and water at 1.5 km and 4.5 km. At a hypothetical 6:00 per km, the loop takes about 36 minutes, with 3 km and roughly 18 minutes between water points. If the day is warm or sleep was poor, stop at the turn point: 3 km, about 18 minutes. If water points are more than a lap apart, carry water.

## Common misconceptions

- Softer grass must be safer. It reduces impact but adds instability, wet patches and holes.
- The greenway is a race course. Sudden stops and cuts cause most conflicts.

## When to stop, reroute or seek care

Reroute or finish if water covers the path, construction leaves no safe passage, a thunderstorm is near, visibility drops, or cyclists pass too close repeatedly. Seek medical help for chest pain, marked dizziness, fainting, inability to bear weight or worsening pain.

## Frequency and progression

Adults need at least 150 minutes of moderate activity weekly or an equivalent combination, plus muscle-strengthening on at least two days; activity can be split, and some is better than none. The injury review covered 36 studies and 23,047 runners, with 26.2% reporting a running-related injury, but evidence linking weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity or recent changes to injury was conflicting, so no single progression should be prescribed. Use four weeks as a hypothetical review cycle, holding one variable steady if laps, pace and new surface all rose.

## Before-you-go checklist

- Water for the route; turn back before half is gone.
- Map, phone and backup navigation; park signal may be patchy.
- Route and return time shared with someone.
- Light for dawn or dusk, with charge confirmed.

## Scope and limits

This page does not judge whether a park or greenway is safe, and does not replace site, race or traffic rules. The research sample is small and does not generalise. People with cardiovascular, respiratory or musculoskeletal conditions should seek clinical advice before changing terrain or volume.
