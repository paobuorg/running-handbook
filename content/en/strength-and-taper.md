## Definition and who this page applies to

Strength, cross-training and tapering mean placing support work and pre-race load changes inside the training structure. Strength work is resistance exercise for major muscle groups; cross-training is low-impact aerobic work such as cycling; a taper is a planned volume reduction before a race. The page applies to runners training at least three times a week and to those cross-training through bad weather or a mild niggle. It holds with no acute injury, no clinician restriction, and stable mileage.

## Where strength work fits

The CDC recommends 150 minutes of moderate activity weekly and strength work on two days covering major muscle groups; ODPHP gives 150–300 minutes or an equivalent combination, also with two days of strength. Strength supports muscle and tendon capacity, but evidence that it prevents every injury is not uniform. Start with one or two sessions of a few stable exercises, keep lower-body work away from key quality sessions, and avoid unfamiliar heavy lifting in race week.

## Strength dose: tiers, not a universal programme

Runners often ask for sets and reps, but the evidence gives a **direction and a range**, not a **universal programme**. A systematic review included multiple strength interventions; 20 studies measured running economy, most showing **2%–8%** improvement versus control, though not all did. The author concluded that adding **2–3 sessions per week**, including a variety of strength modes, is likely to benefit middle- and long-distance performance, with no negative effect on body composition.

| Parameter | Range seen in research | Is there a single standard? |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 2–3 sessions per week | No prescription; 2–3 is the common range in the review (Blagrove 2018) |
| Exercise type | Multi-joint lower limb, core, plyometric/jumping, traditional resistance | No single list; the review included several modes |
| Load | Body weight through moderate-to-heavy loads | No single %1RM; studies differ widely (unknown) |
| Sets × reps | Inconsistent across studies | **unknown** |
| Programme length | Inconsistent across studies | **unknown** |
| Spacing from quality sessions | No universal number of hours | **unknown**; the principle is not to compromise key sessions |

A three-tier frame is more honest than a copied table:

| Tier | Who | Frequency | Focus | Progress when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Entry | Never trained strength, mileage just stable | Once weekly | Squat/hinge/calf raise/core, body weight or light load, stable technique | Four weeks without pain and with consistent form |
| Maintenance | Already competent, in season | 1–2 times weekly | Keep multi-joint lower limb and core work, no maximal loads | It does not affect quality sessions or the long run |
| Advanced | Trained, injury-free, not tapering | 2–3 times weekly | Add plyometrics or heavier loads, technique first | Good 24-hour recovery and no drop in running performance |

**The dose boundary:** research supports the direction of “2–3 sessions per week for several weeks”, but exercises, sets, reps and loads are not standardised enough to prescribe (unknown). Hence the tiers: stabilise technique and frequency first, then load; any persistent pain, unusual fatigue or performance drop means stepping back a tier and reassessing.

## Cross-training is not a substitute

Cycling, swimming and elliptical work add low-impact volume and help when injured. Running-specific load, landing mechanics and coordination cannot be replaced. Treating it as extra volume rather than recovery can let total load spiral. If pain is the reason, clarify the cause with medical or sports input.

## Taper evidence and cycle

A review and meta-analysis of 14 studies found tapering improved time-trial and time-to-exhaustion performance. A common effective pattern cut volume by 41–60% while maintaining intensity and frequency, lasting up to 21 days; pre-taper overload showed a larger effect in some studies. The authors also note that the optimal taper duration varies between individuals. Amateur runners can reduce 20–30% in week one and to 50–60% of normal in week two, keeping short easy running and brief accelerations.

## How to taper in three steps

The numbers below are hypothetical examples.

1. Record normal weekly volume, quality sessions and long-run length, and decide which intensity and frequency to keep.
2. Assume a 50 km week: 35–40 km in week one and 25–30 km in week two, with shorter long runs and short tempo efforts.
3. Assume race week holds 20–30 minutes of easy running and a few 20–30 second accelerations; stop for pain or unusual fatigue.

## Worked example: a three-week taper

Assume 50 km a week, a 15 km long run and an 8 km tempo, with a race on Sunday of week three. Week one drops to 35 km and the tempo to 6 km; week two to 28 km with a 10 km long run and four 1-minute accelerations; race week to 20 km with 8 km easy before the race. Against 50 km, week three is a 60% reduction, inside the 41–60% range. Hypothetical example.

## Common misconceptions

- “A taper means complete rest.” Effective tapers kept intensity and frequency and cut only volume.
- “The taper is a chance to catch up.” Added sessions increase fatigue and cancel recovery.
- “More strength work is always better.” Heavy lifting in race week can leave soreness.

## When to stop and seek help

Pause added work and seek assessment if pain worsens, resting heart rate rises, insomnia persists, mood drops, performance declines for weeks, or tapering leaves you more tired. Chest pain or fainting is an emergency. Previous injury, cardiovascular disease or medication warrants advice first.

## Checklist

- [ ] No more than two strength sessions this week.
- [ ] The taper keeps intensity and frequency while cutting volume.
- [ ] No new shoes, fuel or route in race week.
- [ ] Sleep, fatigue and pain are logged weekly.
- [ ] Stop and seek help for persistent pain or unusual fatigue.

## Scope and limits

This page does not prescribe an individual strength programme, treat injury or give an exact taper percentage. Persistent pain, fatigue, injury history or conflicting race goals deserve assessment by a qualified coach, clinician or sports professional. Pregnancy, cardiovascular disease or rehabilitation do not fit this arrangement.
