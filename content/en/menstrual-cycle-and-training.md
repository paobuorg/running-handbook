## Definitions and scope

This page covers the relationship between the **menstrual cycle, hormonal contraception and running training/racing**: whether cycle phase affects performance, how to manage symptoms, what to do when a race coincides with a period, and when medical care is required.

Assumptions: you are a runner who menstruates, or you coach someone who does, and you want to know whether training should change across the cycle. This page does not diagnose, does not give medication advice, and does not provide a hormonal plan. **Whether to use medication, change contraception or delay a period must be assessed by a doctor or qualified pharmacist.**

## Phases of the cycle

| Phase | Approximate timing | Hormonal background | Common symptoms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early follicular (including menstruation) | Around days 1–5 | Oestrogen and progesterone both low | Dysmenorrhoea, fatigue, heavy flow |
| Late follicular | End of bleeding to before ovulation | Oestrogen rising | Many people feel better |
| Ovulation | Around mid-cycle | Luteinising hormone surge | Brief pelvic pain in some |
| Luteal (early/mid/late) | After ovulation to next period | Progesterone rising | Premenstrual symptoms, mood and sleep changes |

These are general patterns with large individual variation. People with irregular cycles, hormonal contraceptive use or perimenopause cannot map their training onto this table.

## Performance effects: two columns, no single conclusion

The findings disagree, so both sides belong on the page.

| Evidence reporting a difference | Evidence reporting little difference or insufficient certainty |
|---|---|
| In McNulty 2020's meta-analysis of 78 studies, early follicular performance was slightly lower: early versus late follicular effect size **−0.14** (95% CrI −0.26 to −0.03) | The overall median effect size was only **−0.06** (95% CrI −0.16 to 0.04), which is **trivial** |
| The early follicular phase ranked lowest, with a **30%** probability of being the worst or among the worst phases | Other phases had probabilities of **53%–55%**, so the separation is small |
| In 1,086 athletes, dysmenorrhoea and premenstrual symptoms affected self-rated aerobic fitness, strength, concentration, balance and sleep | That study used **self-report**, not laboratory performance testing; perceived impact is not the same as measured performance loss |
| Some individuals clearly perform worse in specific phases | **42%** of the evidence was graded **low**; the authors explicitly state that general guidelines across the cycle cannot be formed |

McNulty 2020 concludes that performance **may** be slightly reduced in the early follicular phase, but the effect is small, between-study variation is large, and many studies are low quality. The recommendation is **individualisation**, not one cycle-based plan for everyone.

In other words: **at the population level there is not enough evidence to support uniform reductions or increases by phase; at the individual level some people are clearly affected, and tracking is how you find out.**

## Symptom burden is more certain than performance effects

Ekenros 2022 surveyed **1,086** female athletes from **57 sports**:

- Among the **407** not using hormonal contraception, **74%** reported dysmenorrhoea and **78%** reported premenstrual symptoms;
- Symptoms affected self-rated aerobic fitness, muscle strength, mental sharpness, balance and sleep quality;
- **63%** used some form of hormonal contraception, and **40%** of users reported side-effects;
- Only **18%** considered menstrual cycle or contraceptive issues when planning training or competition.

Two things follow: symptoms are common, and most athletes do not plan around them. **Common does not mean it must be endured**, and it does not mean everyone needs cycle-based training; it means the issue deserves tracking and individual management.

## Differences among hormonal contraceptive users

- **Combined oral contraceptive (OCP) users do not have a natural hormonal cycle**; they have a pill cycle and a withdrawal bleed.
- Elliott-Sale 2020's meta-analysis included **42 studies and 590 participants**. OCP users may perform slightly worse on average than naturally menstruating women, but the group-level effect is **most likely trivial**; **83%** of studies were graded moderate, low or very low quality.
- The same analysis found performance relatively **consistent across the OCP cycle**; the authors do not support general performance guidance based on OCP use versus non-use, and did not compare pill-taking with non-pill-taking days.
- Ekenros 2022 found that the **inactive/placebo pill period** was associated with a more negative subjective impact. If you feel clearly worse then, record it and discuss it with a clinician, but do not change medication on your own.
- Different methods (combined oral, progestogen-only, intrauterine device, implant) cannot be assumed to behave the same; this page does not have enough evidence to compare them one by one.

## Practical approach: follow symptoms, not the calendar

**1. Track two to three cycles.**
Record dates, flow, pain score, sleep, mood, training content and RPE. Without records, you cannot tell whether a bad session came from the cycle, training load or life stress.

Example entry: `D1 | medium flow | pain 4/10 | sleep 6 h | easy run 30 min | RPE 4`. After two cycles, review how sessions went on days with pain ≥6; that is more reliable than memory.

**2. Adjust training by symptoms, not by phase.**
- Feeling normal: follow the planned session; do not reduce training simply because you are in the luteal phase.
- Marked dysmenorrhoea: switch to an easy run, shorten the session or rest. NHS self-care suggestions include warmth, a heat pad, gentle activity such as yoga, swimming, walking or cycling, and massage.
- Marked premenstrual symptoms: prioritise sleep and recovery, and move quality sessions to days when you feel better rather than forcing them.

**3. When a race coincides with a period.**
- Do not assume you will automatically perform badly; the population evidence does not support that.
- Prepare sanitary products, clothing changes and fuelling logistics; for long races, plan changing frequency and anti-chafing.
- If flow is heavy or pain is marked on the day, adjust expectations and pacing rather than using the race to prove you can endure it.
- **Do not start, stop or stack hormonal medication before a race to shift the date.** The NHS Periods page states that delaying a period requires assessment by a doctor, pharmacist or sexual health clinic; the approach with the combined pill depends on the pill type, and where the combined pill is not used a doctor may prescribe norethisterone, which is not suitable for everyone.

**4. Hormonal contraceptive users.**
There is no need to change your pill schedule for a race. If side-effects are troublesome, take your records to a clinician or pharmacist rather than stopping on your own.

## Red flags: seek medical care

The following are not normal period discomfort. Stop the relevant training and seek care:

- **Amenorrhoea**: **3–11 consecutive cycles** without a period, especially with weight loss, low energy intake, stress fracture or a sudden jump in training. The 2023 IOC REDs consensus lists secondary amenorrhoea caused by functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea as a **primary indicator** of REDs. It is not a normal cost of training hard.
- **Abnormally heavy bleeding**: changing a pad or tampon every **1–2 hours**, bleeding for more than **7 days**, clots larger than about **2.5 cm**, or bleeding through clothes or bedding. The NHS advises seeing a GP.
- **Severe pain**: pain that is worse than usual, does not respond to pain relief, or stops you doing daily activities. The NHS advises an urgent GP appointment; severe pain with fainting, fever or persistent vomiting should be treated as an emergency (call 120 in mainland China or go to an emergency department).
- **Non-menstrual bleeding**: bleeding between periods or after sex needs medical assessment.
- **Associated symptoms**: dizziness, palpitations, weakness or fainting with heavy bleeding; fever, foul-smelling discharge or severe lower abdominal pain. Seek care promptly rather than waiting to see whether it settles.

## Explicit statement

This page **does not diagnose, does not give medication advice and does not provide a hormonal plan**. Pain relief, bleeding control, hormonal contraception and period delay must be assessed by a doctor or pharmacist according to your history, clot risk, migraine type, smoking status and other factors. Do not start, stop or combine any medication based on this page.

## Common misconceptions

- **"I cannot run during my period."** The population effect is trivial; individual responses vary, and some runners are unaffected.
- **"Training must follow the cycle."** Current evidence does not support a universal plan; tracking and individualisation are more reliable.
- **"Period pain is normal, just push through."** Pain that stops daily activities, or does not respond to pain relief, is a reason to seek care.
- **"Missing periods means training is working."** Three to eleven consecutive missed cycles is a primary REDs indicator and needs assessment.
- **"The pill removes all cycle problems."** OCP users still have withdrawal bleeds and side-effects; methods cannot be generalised to each other.
- **"Delaying a period before a race is safe."** It is a medical decision with side-effects and contraindications; do not self-manage.
- **"Heavy flow is only an inconvenience."** Bleeding that meets the thresholds above can be associated with anaemia and needs investigation.

## Checklist

- [ ] Have I tracked two to three cycles, including dates, flow, pain and training response?
- [ ] Am I using symptoms rather than the calendar to decide whether and how hard to train?
- [ ] Do I know my thresholds for amenorrhoea, heavy bleeding and severe pain?
- [ ] If a race coincides with my period, do I have a plan for products, clothing changes and pacing?
- [ ] Have I changed my hormonal contraception before a race on my own? If so, stop and seek advice.
- [ ] Am I mistaking a missing period for successful training?

## Limits and emergencies

This is health education, not gynaecological or sports-medicine care. Heavy bleeding with dizziness, fainting or persistent palpitations, or sudden severe lower abdominal pain with fever and foul discharge, should be treated as an emergency. Pregnancy, the postpartum period, perimenopause, polycystic ovary syndrome, endometriosis, fibroids and a history of thrombosis all change how cycles and bleeding should be interpreted; a clinician should assess them. **Training adjustments cannot replace diagnosis; seek care if symptoms persist or worsen.**

## Sources and verification date

- McNulty et al., The Effects of Menstrual Cycle Phase on Exercise Performance in Eumenorrheic Women: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis, Sports Medicine 2020: 78 studies; overall median effect size −0.06 (95% CrI −0.16 to 0.04); early versus late follicular −0.14 (−0.26 to −0.03); early follicular ranked lowest (30%); 42% of evidence low quality; no general guidelines, individualisation recommended.
- Elliott-Sale et al., The Effects of Oral Contraceptives on Exercise Performance in Women: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis, Sports Medicine 2020: 42 studies, 590 participants; 83% moderate/low/very low quality; OCP users may perform slightly worse but the effect is trivial; performance consistent across the OCP cycle; no general guidance supported.
- Ekenros et al., Perceived impact of the menstrual cycle and hormonal contraceptives on physical exercise and performance in 1,086 athletes from 57 sports, Frontiers in Physiology 2022: 1,086 athletes; dysmenorrhoea 74% and premenstrual symptoms 78% among non-users; 63% used hormonal contraception, 40% reported side-effects; only 18% considered the cycle when planning; inactive-pill period associated with more negative impact.
- Mountjoy et al., 2023 International Olympic Committee's (IOC) consensus statement on Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs), British Journal of Sports Medicine 2023: REDs is caused by problematic low energy availability; secondary amenorrhoea (3–11 consecutive cycles) due to functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea is a primary indicator; primary amenorrhoea is a severe primary indicator.

- NHS, Missed or late periods: three missed periods in a row, irregular periods, or missed periods with weight change, tiredness, facial hair or skin changes should be assessed.
- NHS, Period pain: pain usually lasts up to 3 days; warmth, heat, gentle exercise and massage may help; severe pain unrelieved by painkillers needs urgent assessment; pain affecting daily activities needs a GP.
- NHS, Periods (Delaying your period): a period can be delayed with the combined pill, depending on the pill type; where the combined pill is not used a doctor may prescribe norethisterone, which is not suitable for everyone; assessment by a doctor, pharmacist or sexual health clinic is required and it should not be self-managed.
