## Definition and who this applies to

The long run is the clearly longer easy or endurance run of the week. It practises sustained movement, fuelling rhythm, route judgement and control under fatigue. It is not “the farther the more serious”, and not a weekly maximum test; its value comes from repeatable, recoverable training.

This applies to runners who already complete shorter runs consistently and are preparing for a marathon, trail race or general endurance. The condition is that length may grow only when current volume does not affect normal activity the next day; with frequent pain, poor sleep or accumulating fatigue, maintain or reduce.

## What the long run is for

It develops aerobic endurance, practises eating and drinking while moving, builds route and kit familiarity, and trains judgement under fatigue. It matters for marathon or trail preparation; for 5K, 10K or health-focused running, a longer controlled easy run may be enough.

## Length is relative to your base

Length depends on weekly volume, goal distance, surface and recovery capacity. A common approach is the longer easy run of the week, not a sudden doubling. Stabilise frequency and easy-run time before adding length, and let someone who knows your history adjust the distance.

## Practise fuelling and route judgement together

The ISSN nutrient-timing position stand says carbohydrate timing and amount around exercise can affect performance and recovery, but guidance must fit exercise demand, diet and individual tolerance. The NHS notes that long activity or heat can increase fluid needs. Do not debut a new gel, drink or breakfast on race day.

Choose routes with water, escape points, low traffic risk and familiarity. A GPX shows geometry only, not closures, water or phone coverage; check the weather and leave a trip plan.

## How to design a long run in three steps

1. **Set one purpose.** Sustained movement, fuelling practice, climbing or simulating the late race: choose one.
2. **Set route and fuelling.** Pick water, escape points and low traffic risk; carry tested food and fluid, and plan the way home.
3. **Set recovery and review.** Schedule an easy day or rest, record sleep, next-day pain and movement quality, then decide whether the next run stays, shortens or grows.

## Sample week layout

| Position | Example arrangement | Main purpose | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Day before | Easy run or rest | Preserve glycogen and focus | No stacked hard session |
| Long-run day | Longer easy run with fuelling practice | Sustained movement and route judgement | No sprint finish |
| Day after | Rest or very short easy run | Absorption and recovery | Extend rest if pain worsens |
| Other days | Mostly easy, at most one quality session | Maintain frequency | Keep quality away from the long run |

## Worked example: distance within volume and pace (assumed)

Assume a weekly volume of 40 kilometres and a long run of 16 kilometres, which is 40% of the week. At 6 minutes 30 seconds per kilometre, 16 kilometres takes about 104 minutes; at the same pace, 2 hours covers about 18.5 kilometres. These figures are hypothetical; 40% is not a recommended share.

## Common pitfalls

- **Treating the long run as a weekly test.** Running to the limit squeezes recovery.
- **Doubling distance suddenly.** A single jump adds a large load.
- **Debuting new fuelling on race day.** Gut and kit need training verification.
- **Using a fixed percentage as proof of safety.** The evidence is conflicting.

## Frequency, cycle and progression

The long run is usually weekly, but how often and how far should follow the phase. An illustrative rhythm: in a base phase, review every 2–3 weeks and extend only when recovery is good; before a race, stabilise the longest run near the goal, then taper. Progression is a cycle of increase, stabilise and review.

A systematic review pooled 36 studies and 23,047 runners, of whom 26.2% reported a running-related injury. Evidence linking weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity or recent changes to injury was conflicting, and the authors caution against one optimal progression. Judge by fatigue, sleep, pain and movement quality.

## When to stop, shorten or seek care

Do not force a long run with fever, significant pain, unusual palpitations or chest pain, dizziness, or persistent vomiting or diarrhoea; seek medical help when needed. In severe weather, on unfamiliar routes, without reliable fuelling or when you cannot return before dark, shortening or postponing is more sensible. If fatigue, pain or poor sleep persists, keep or reduce the next run.

## Before and after checklist

- [ ] The long run has one main purpose, written down.
- [ ] The route has water, escape points and low traffic risk.
- [ ] Fuelling and kit are already tested in training.
- [ ] Weather is checked, someone knows the plan, and an offline route is loaded.
- [ ] An easy day or rest is scheduled for the next day.

## Scope and limits

This page does not prescribe an individual distance or promise that a distance improves performance or prevents injury. A plan should account for health, injury history, goal and recovery; consult a qualified clinician with a medical condition. The long run does not replace speed work, strength work or sleep.
