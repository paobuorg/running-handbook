## Definition and who this page applies to

A supplement is a product that adds vitamins, minerals, protein, caffeine or similar ingredients to the diet, not a medicine. It applies to runners considering protein or caffeine. It holds when you have 3–4 weeks of diet and training records and no medical concern.

## Regulation is different: on sale is not proof

The U.S. FDA regulates dietary supplements differently from conventional foods and drugs. Companies handle safety and labelling before marketing, and the FDA can act after products reach the market. Availability is not proof.

## Ask what is missing: food first

For most runners a balanced diet supplies most training needs. Whether protein, carbohydrate, iron or vitamin D needs supplementing depends on diet records, tests, health and training load. Without a clear deficiency, stacking products adds cost and interaction risk.

The ISSN protein stand notes that active people can meet needs with whole foods.

## Protein: total, distribution and food

The ISSN protein stand gives 1.4–2.0 g per kilogram of body weight per day as sufficient for most exercising individuals. Per serving it suggests about 0.25 g/kg, or 20–40 g, every 3–4 hours.

These figures describe total intake, not proof that everyone needs powder. Endurance runners should still prioritise carbohydrate.

## Caffeine: dose, timing and response

The ISSN caffeine stand reports acute benefits in many but not all studies, with aerobic endurance showing the most consistent moderate-to-large effects. Doses of 3–6 mg/kg have improved performance; very high doses bring more side effects.

A common timing is about 60 minutes before exercise, depending on source. Responses vary, and caffeine can affect sleep or anxiety.

## How evidence is ranked

Randomised trials, systematic reviews and meta-analyses are generally more reliable than one small study, but supplement research often has small samples, funding issues and dose or population differences. When a percentage gain is claimed, ask about population, comparison and replication.

Cell and animal studies, or an “expert recommendation”, do not directly prove benefit for runners.

## How to review a supplement in three steps

These steps are a review routine, not a buying recommendation.

1. Write the gap: record 7 days of diet, estimate daily protein and meal spacing, and mark whether intake falls below 1.4 g/kg/day.
2. Check evidence and dose. Protein: 20–40 g per serving every 3–4 hours; caffeine: 3–6 mg/kg.
3. Run a 2–4 week single-product trial, recording sleep, anxiety, gut and training. Do not start within 8 weeks of a race.

## Worked example: protein and caffeine for a 70 kg runner

Assume a 70-kilogram runner. At 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day, total protein is about 98–140 g/day. At 0.25 g/kg, one serving is about 17.5 g; the stand also gives 20–40 g, so 20–40 g every 3–4 hours is easier to apply. Four meals give 25–35 g each.

At 3–6 mg/kg, caffeine is about 210–420 mg, about 60 minutes before exercise. These figures are hypothetical.

## How to read a supplement label

A dietary supplement label is not a drug label. Under US rules, supplements are not evaluated by the FDA for effectiveness before marketing, and a product that claims to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent a disease is making a claim it is not allowed to make. Labels commonly carry a disclaimer stating that the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease, and that it has not been evaluated by the FDA. Reading that line tells you what the manufacturer is not asserting.

The label also tells you the serving size, the amount per serving and the form of each ingredient; those are the numbers you need for comparison. A product that hides amounts behind a "proprietary blend" cannot be compared with a study dose. Before adding anything, check whether food already covers the need, whether the dose matches the evidence you have read, and whether a clinician or dietitian should review it given medication, pregnancy or a medical condition.

## Common misconceptions

- “If it is on sale, it works.” Companies handle safety before marketing; availability is not proof.
- “Every runner needs protein powder.” Whole foods can meet the need.
- “A testing mark proves effect.” Testing reduces contamination risk, not benefit.
- “Race day is fine for a first try.” Avoid starting within 8 weeks of a race.

## When to stop and seek help

Stop and seek medical assessment for unusual symptoms such as palpitations, marked anxiety, insomnia, gut upset, rash, breathing difficulty or abnormal urine colour. Supplements can interact with medication and burden the liver, kidneys, cardiovascular system or gut. With pregnancy, breastfeeding, a medical condition, medication or a history of disordered eating, consult a doctor or registered dietitian first.

## Frequency and cycle: trial, review, reassess

Distribute protein every 3–4 hours; observe each new product for 2–4 weeks; review diet, sleep and training every 4 weeks; reassess whether a real gap remains every 8–12 weeks. Do not start a new supplement within 8 weeks of a race.

## Checklist

- [ ] Keep 3–4 weeks of diet and training records.
- [ ] Calculate total protein and meal spacing.
- [ ] Check population and dose.
- [ ] Trial one product; record sleep, gut and training.
- [ ] Avoid a first trial within 8 weeks of a race.
- [ ] Keep batch records and stop for unusual symptoms.

## Scope and limits

This page does not recommend a brand, dose combination or shop, or decide whether a product suits you. Supplements do not replace medical assessment, treatment or a balanced diet. With a medical condition, pregnancy, breastfeeding, medication or a history of disordered eating, consult a professional first.
