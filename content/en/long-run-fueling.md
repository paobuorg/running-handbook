## Definition and who this applies to

Long-run fuelling is the planned intake of carbohydrate, fluid and electrolytes during sustained training or racing. The question is not “what is best” in general, but what your body can tolerate and absorb while running.

This applies to runners who already run continuously for about an hour or more and are preparing for a marathon or trail race. The condition is that every new approach must be verified in training first; with kidney or heart conditions, salt restriction or nutrition therapy, fluid and electrolyte plans should follow medical advice.

## The two direct goals of fuelling

The aims are to maintain available energy and to avoid ending early because of dehydration or gut discomfort; performance is indirect. The nutrient-timing position stand notes that carbohydrate timing and amount around exercise can affect performance and recovery, but guidance must fit exercise demand, duration and individual variation. Carbohydrate availability matters for prolonged endurance exercise.

## Carbohydrate: calculate actual intake per hour

For extended high-intensity exercise longer than 60 minutes, the ISSN position stand presents about 30–60 grams of carbohydrate per hour, delivered as a 6–8% carbohydrate-electrolyte solution every 10–15 minutes. That is a common range, not a target everyone must hit; practise and adjust it. Forms include sports drinks, gels, chews, bars or easy real food, provided you can take them while moving. Compare carbohydrate per serving or per hour, not packet count; products vary widely, so copying “packets per hour” is meaningless.

## Fluid and electrolytes

Fluid needs vary with temperature, humidity, altitude, sweat rate and the individual, so no single millilitre target covers everyone. The NHS advises most people to drink enough for clear pale yellow urine and notes that long activity or heat may require more fluid; stomach comfort and toilet access also matter. A sodium drink or tablet may help when sweat losses are large, but cramps are not simply “low sodium”. With kidney or heart conditions or salt restriction, a clinician should set the plan. If carbohydrate is inadequate, added protein may help recovery but does not replace carbohydrate.

## How to test fuelling in three steps

1. **Choose one or two variables.** For example, one gel providing about 25 grams of carbohydrate every 40 minutes, or a 6% carbohydrate-electrolyte drink.
2. **Record intake and response.** Note time, food type, carbohydrate per serving, weather, pace, stomach, mood and late-run performance.
3. **Repeat and keep an exit.** Use the plan in at least 2–3 similar long runs; if nausea, bloating or diarrhoea appears, reduce the amount or return to a familiar option.

## Fuelling test quick reference

| Variable | How to test | What to record | Adjustment signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carbohydrate per hour | Start lower, take at fixed intervals | Grams per serving, total, timing | Low energy, nausea or bloating |
| Solution concentration | Use a 6–8% carbohydrate-electrolyte solution | Concentration, cup volume, interval | Stomach fullness or thirst |
| Total fluid | Match weather and sweat | Millilitres per hour, urine colour | Dizziness, palpitations or thirst |
| Food form | Gels, chews, drink or real food | Whether you can swallow while moving | Chewing difficulty or queasiness |

## Worked example: an assumed 70 kg runner over 2.5 hours

Assume a runner covers 2.5 hours in mild weather and plans 45 grams of carbohydrate per hour. The total is 45 × 2.5 = 112.5 grams; at 25 grams per gel that is about 4.5 gels, spread as 5. A 500 ml drink at 6% holds about 30 grams; at 500 ml per hour, 2.5 hours needs about 1250 ml. These figures are hypothetical, not a prescription, and no brand is implied.

## Common pitfalls

- **Counting packets instead of grams.** Concentration varies, so “two packets per hour” may be far off the need.
- **Waiting for a crash and eating a large amount.** Concentrated intake is more likely to upset the gut.
- **Treating every cramp as low sodium.** Causes vary, and adding salt blindly can create problems.
- **Debuting a new plan on race day.** Gels, drinks and breakfasts need training verification.

## Frequency, cycle and progression

An illustrative rhythm: with one long run a week, spend the first 4–6 weeks stabilising a base approach, change only one variable every 2–3 weeks, then stop major changes 2–3 weeks before the race and lock in the tested combination. Progression means improving repeatability, not endlessly raising intake.

## When to stop and seek help

Stop the plan and the run, move to safety and seek help for persistent nausea, repeated vomiting, diarrhoea, dizziness, confusion, unusual palpitations or chest pain. After a long run, recurring gut problems, inability to eat normally or markedly reduced urine output also deserve advice. Do not treat “pushing through” as adaptation.

## Test record checklist

- [ ] Weather, duration, pace and fluid conditions are recorded.
- [ ] Carbohydrate per serving, timing and total grams are recorded.
- [ ] Fluid type, concentration, volume and urine colour are recorded.
- [ ] Stomach, mood and late-run performance are recorded.
- [ ] The plan was repeated in 2–3 similar runs, with one next variable named.

## Scope and limits

This page does not provide an individual nutrition prescription, disease management advice or brand recommendations, and does not promise improved performance. Needs depend on duration, intensity, weather, sweat rate and tolerance; with a medical condition, eating disorder or special diet, a clinician or dietitian should assess the plan.
