## Definition and scope

**Polarized training** is a training intensity distribution (TID): most training is low intensity, a small amount is high intensity, and the middle "threshold" zone is deliberately small. It is often shortened to "80/20", but that shorthand mixes at least three different units — **the share of sessions, the share of time, and the definition of "low intensity"**. Mixing them is where most misuse begins.

This page is for: runners asking whether 80/20 is a rule; runners already following a polarized idea who want to check whether they are doing it as intended; anyone building a week around "mostly easy, a little genuinely hard".

It is **not** for: people returning from injury, illness or a long layoff; people with cardiovascular, metabolic or respiratory disease whose exercise intensity is set by a clinician; or anyone hoping a ratio can replace individual coaching. In those situations, follow the intensity advice you have been given.

## "80/20" is an observation, not a prescription

Seiler's 2010 review summarised descriptive studies: nationally or internationally competitive endurance athletes training 10–13 times per week **seem to converge** on a distribution in which about **80% of sessions** are low intensity (around 2 mmol/L blood lactate) and about **20%** are dominated by high-intensity work such as intervals near 90% VO2max. The same review concluded that in already well-trained athletes, adding more high-intensity interval training did **not** provide convincing long-term performance gains.

Seiler and Tønnessen (2009) analysed **more than 15,000 training sessions** across the careers of three world and/or Olympic champions in distance running, cross-country skiing and orienteering. About **85% of sessions** were continuous low-to-moderate work (blood lactate ≤ 2 mmol/L). They also wrote the boundary explicitly: elite endurance athletes place **80% or more** of training clearly below lactate threshold and use high-intensity work "surprisingly sparingly".

These numbers show what excellent endurance athletes **often do**. They do not show what you **must do**. The original wording is descriptive ("seem to converge", "appear to self-organize"), not a causal conclusion from a randomised trial. Rewriting an observation as a command raises the evidence level beyond what the data support.

## How much low intensity? The range is wider than "80%"

Putting several studies side by side shows that 80% is a midpoint, not a boundary.

| Study | Population and sport | Low-intensity share | Metric |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Robinson et al. (1991) | New Zealand national runners, 1500 m to marathon | **96%** | Below 4 mmol/L lactate |
| Billat et al. (2001) | Top-class marathoners (men < 2:12) | **78%** | Below marathon race pace; 4% at marathon pace; 18% above |
| Billat et al. (2003) | Kenyan 10 km runners | **83.8%–84.2%** | Below lactate-threshold velocity; 6.9%–14.4% at threshold; 1.4%–4.3% above |
| Seiler & Kjerland (2006) | Norwegian junior cross-country skiers | **about 75%** | Zone 1; Zone 2 5%–10%, Zone 3 15%–20% |
| Plews et al. (2014) | Nine heavyweight elite rowers | **77.3%** | Below first lactate threshold; Zone 2 16.9%; Zone 3 5.8% |
| Lucia et al. (2000) | 13 professional cyclists | **77%–88%** | Below VT1; 78% pre-competition, 77% competition, 88% rest |
| Mujika et al. (1995) | 18 national/international swimmers | **about 77%** | 2 mmol/L as the low-intensity ceiling |
| Steinacker et al. (1998) | German, Danish, Dutch and Norwegian elite junior rowers | **75%** | Below 1.5 mmol/L |
| Hartmann et al. (1990) | German elite rowers | **70%–94%** | 86%–94% in preparation, 70%–77% in competition |

These figures are not directly comparable: some count **sessions**, some count **time**; some use blood lactate, some ventilatory thresholds or heart rate; the ceiling for "low" differs. What they do show is that **the low-intensity share among elite endurance athletes runs roughly from 70% to 96%, mostly 75%–85%, with both ends real — and it moves within the same athlete across the season.**

## Individual variation: the same ratio does not produce the same execution

A ratio is a group statistic, not a personal prescription. Festa et al. (2019) randomised 38 recreational runners to two groups for 8 weeks, matched for total training impulse (TRIMP):

- the polarized group (PET) targeted **77/3/20** (Zone 1/2/3 by heart rate);
- the focused group (FOC) targeted **40/50/10**.

Actual execution in the polarized group was **78% ± 9.2%** in Zone 1, **3.1% ± 1.3%** in Zone 2 and **18.9% ± 4.6%** in Zone 3. In other words, **one prescription landed with a standard deviation of about 9 percentage points in the low-intensity share**. That is before any difference in physiological response: both groups improved velocity at VO2max, velocity at ventilatory threshold, 2 km performance and running economy, with **no significant difference between groups**. But the polarized group spent **29.9 ± 3.1 hours** versus **24.8 ± 2.0 hours** in the focused group — the focused group achieved similar improvements with about 17% less training time.

The same paper cites Muñoz et al. (2014): after 10 weeks, polarized training was slightly better for 10 km performance (−5% versus −3.5%), but the authors concluded that the overall evidence was not enough to support one model over the other.

## Sport and distance: marathoners lean pyramidal, 1500 m runners lean polarized

The most common misreading of 80/20 is "all runners should train this way". Casado et al. (2022), a systematic review of 10 studies, is more specific:

- **Highly trained and elite distance runners overall more commonly follow a pyramidal distribution**: training volume decreases from Zone 1 to Zone 2 to Zone 3;
- **marathoners lean more pyramidal**, while **1500 m runners lean more polarized**;
- within a season, a common pattern is **pyramidal in preparation and pre-competition, shifting toward polarized in the competition phase**;
- training is organised around **hard day–easy day** alternation, not daily moderate running.

Rowing records show how wide the sport-to-sport range is: German junior rowers at **95-3-2**, New Zealand Olympic rowers at **77-17-6**, one successful French rower at **45% Zone 1 / 55% Zone 2**, and a Belgian elite sculler at **93-2-5**. Under the single label "endurance sport", the low-intensity share runs from 45% to 95%.

Running is not one line either: Billat et al. (2001) reported top-class marathoners at 78/4/18 (classified by marathon pace), while Billat et al. (2003) reported Kenyan 10 km runners at about 84% below lactate-threshold velocity and about 7%–14% at threshold velocity. Distance, race pace and training tradition all shift the distribution.

## How to define low intensity: talk test and %HRR

In polarized training, "low intensity" is not "whatever feels easy"; it has a physiological anchor: **below the first ventilatory threshold or first lactate threshold, commonly approximated as blood lactate ≤ 2 mmol/L** (Seiler 2010; Seiler & Tønnessen 2009). Without a lab, two practical checks work.

**Check one: the talk test.** The CDC definition is: at moderate intensity you **can talk, but not sing**; at vigorous intensity you **cannot say more than a few words without pausing for a breath**. On an easy run:

- you can speak a full sentence without breaking it → probably low intensity;
- speech becomes broken and you can only use short phrases → you are near or above the low-intensity ceiling;
- you can only say a few words before gasping → high intensity.

The talk test is cheap and immediate, but it separates "moderate" from "vigorous"; it does not equal your personal first lactate threshold. Use it to **calibrate**, not to replace a threshold test.

**Check two: percentage of heart-rate reserve (%HRR).** ACSM's 8th edition defines moderate intensity as **40%–59% HRR** (about 64%–76% HRmax) and vigorous as **60%–84% HRR**. The low intensity of polarized training usually sits in the **lower half** of that moderate band: speech is comfortable, breathing is steady, and the next day's training is unaffected. Do not read this as "59% HRR is always low intensity": thresholds differ between people, and the formula is only a starting point.

This site's article on heart-rate zones gives the full calculation: `HRmax ≈ 208 − 0.7 × age`, `HRR = HRmax − HRrest`, `target = %HRR × HRR + HRrest`, plus the error warning (in the HERITAGE sample of 762 people, the standard error of estimate for the Tanaka formula was about 11.4 bpm). If you want to control low intensity by heart rate, replace both inputs with your own measurements before arguing about percentages.

## Polarized, pyramidal, threshold: how the three differ

| Distribution | Zone 1 low | Zone 2 threshold | Zone 3 high | Typical description |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| **Polarized** | about 75%–80% | about 0%–5% | about 15%–20% | Both ends high, middle low; usually 1–2 hard sessions per week |
| **Pyramidal** | about 80% | about 12% | about 8% | Decreasing from low to high; threshold work has a regular place |
| **Threshold-focused** | about 40%–45% | about 35%–50% | about 10%–20% | Middle zone dominates; where unmonitored recreational training often drifts |

The percentages are **illustrative**. Different studies use different zone boundaries and counting methods, so these are not a precise recipe. The real difference is not whether low intensity is exactly 80%; it is **how much sits in the middle**:

- polarized: deliberately compress Zone 2 and split the stimulus into "very easy" and "very hard";
- pyramidal: keep some Zone 2, using threshold work to support race pace;
- threshold-focused: much of the training sits in the "somewhat hard but not hard" band.

Marathoners commonly use pyramidal distribution because the race itself is a long effort near threshold; 1500 m runners commonly use polarized distribution because the race depends more on repeated high-intensity capacity. **The target race distance and pace change the value of the middle zone.**

## Traps for recreational runners who copy the ratio

1. **Treating "80% of sessions" as "80% of time".** Seiler 2010 counted sessions; easy sessions are usually longer, so by time the low-intensity share is often closer to 90%. Both numbers can be correct, but they cannot be mixed. If you force 80/20 by time, your session mix may become "half easy, half hard".
2. **Easy runs that are not easy.** If low intensity drifts into "can talk but slightly breathless", you neither get the volume of easy work nor arrive fresh for the hard session. Use the talk test to push easy days back to full sentences.
3. **High intensity that is not high.** If the 20% is only "a bit faster than easy", it is neither polarized nor threshold training; it is moderate work with a new name.
4. **Adding intensity without adding recovery.** The intervention studies cited by Seiler and Tønnessen (2009) show that raising high-intensity work to 3–4 sessions per week for several weeks can effectively **suppress performance temporarily and induce overreaching**; more high intensity is not automatically better.
5. **Ignoring the time cost.** In Festa et al. (2019), the polarized group spent about 17% more total time for similar gains. For time-limited recreational runners, treating 80/20 as a mandatory target may cost sleep and recovery first.
6. **Mistaking an elite retrospective distribution for causal proof.** How elites train is an observation. Why they are fast may reflect selection, total volume, years of accumulation and recovery. Distribution is only one variable.

## A weekly example counted by sessions and by time

Below is an example for **five runs per week**, used to show the difference between "80/20 by sessions" and "about 85/15 by time". It is not a prescription and does not suit everyone.

| Day | Session | Intensity | Example duration |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Monday | Easy run | Can speak full sentences | 45–60 min |
| Tuesday | Hard session: 6 × 3 min with 2 min easy jog | About 90% VO2max; speech in short phrases | about 35–40 min |
| Wednesday | Easy run | Can speak full sentences | 40–50 min |
| Thursday | Easy run | Can speak full sentences | 40–50 min |
| Saturday | Long easy run | Can speak full sentences | 80–100 min |
| Sunday | Rest | — | — |

- **By sessions**: 4 of 5 easy = 80%; 1 of 5 hard = 20%.
- **By time**: roughly 205–260 min easy versus 35–40 min hard; low intensity is about **84%–87%**.
- **If you run only 3 times per week**: 2 easy + 1 hard = 67% of sessions, but the time share may still be close to 80%. At this frequency, "80/20 by sessions" is arithmetically awkward; use time, or a 10-day block, instead.
- **If you run 6 times per week**: 5 easy + 1 hard = 83% of sessions; 4 easy + 2 hard = 67%. Two hard sessions need at least 48 hours between them, and total intensity should not be maximal in both.

The ordering principle matters more than the ratio: **leave easy days between hard days; do not stack intensity around the long run; in race and taper weeks, cut the hard session rather than deleting easy runs.** This site's article on the training week discusses those principles in more detail.

## Evidence boundary: no evidence for one exact ratio

Treating 80/20 as a precise physiological law is not supported by the evidence:

- **Rosenblat et al. (2019)**, a systematic review and meta-analysis, screened 329 records but included only **4 randomised controlled trials**, of which **3** entered the meta-analysis. Two studies scored only **4/10** on the PEDro scale and two scored **5/10**. The pooled result showed a moderate effect for time-trial performance (ES = −0.66, 95% CI −1.17 to −0.15) favouring polarized training, but the evidence base is thin.
- **Festa et al. (2019)** found no between-group difference in recreational runners, and the focused group used about 17% less time.
- **Treff et al. (2017)** compared 11 weeks of polarized versus pyramidal distribution in 14 national elite rowers: polarized Zone 3 was 6% ± 3% versus 2% ± 1%, with Zone 1 clamped at about 93%–94% in both groups; there were **no significant differences** in 2000 m ergometer power, power at 4 mmol/L, or VO2max.
- **Casado et al. (2022)** found that highly trained distance runners **more commonly follow a pyramidal distribution**.

These studies do not contradict each other. They show that the evidence is fairly consistent at the coarse level — **most training should be low intensity** — and inconsistent at the fine level: **whether low intensity is 75%, 80% or 90%, and how much belongs in the middle, has no single answer.** The ratio is not a variable where "calculating it more precisely" buys extra adaptation; total load, the quality of hard sessions, recovery and consistency matter more.

So the conclusion of this page is: use 80/20 as a **starting point and a checking tool** — first make most sessions genuinely easy and a few genuinely hard — not as a KPI. You do not need to change a sensible week in order to hit 80.0%.

## Common misconceptions

- **"80/20 means two hard sessions every week."** The 20% is a share of sessions, not a fixed count; at five runs per week it is one session, at ten runs it may be two. The total volume of hard work and the spacing matter too.
- **"Polarized means no threshold work at all."** Polarized compresses the Zone 2 share; it does not ban threshold sessions. Race-specific and marathon phases often need threshold work, and the distribution shifts toward pyramidal.
- **"All elites train 80/20."** The systematic review of elite runners found pyramidal distribution more common; rowing records range from 45% to 95% low intensity. Copying one sport's observation into another is a common error.
- **"Low intensity means jogging as slowly as possible."** Low intensity needs volume, but it is not junk mileage; it should have a defined duration, be executed consistently, and pair with the hard sessions.
- **"If the ratio is right, progress is guaranteed."** The ratio is one part of load structure; sleep, total volume, strength work, injury and continuity all change the outcome.
- **"The zones on my watch are my zones."** Watches often generate zones from "220 − age"; replace maximum and resting heart rate using the calculation on this site first, then check with the talk test.

## Scope and limits

This page is education about exercise physiology and training organisation. It gives no individual coaching prescription, diagnosis or treatment. All percentages are research observations or illustrations, not predictions for you; every example week must be adjusted for your training age, total volume, injury history and recovery capacity. If you have cardiovascular, respiratory, metabolic or endocrine disease, or take medication that affects heart rate, exercise intensity should follow your clinician's advice. If you develop persistent fatigue, worsening sleep, an unusually elevated resting heart rate or a sustained drop in performance, reduce load and seek professional assessment rather than chasing a ratio.
