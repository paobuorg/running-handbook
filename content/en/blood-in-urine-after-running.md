## Definition and scope

This page is about **blood in urine after running**: visibly red, pink or tea-coloured urine, and microscopic haematuria found only on urinalysis. Microscopic haematuria is commonly defined as three or more red blood cells per high-power field; gross haematuria is blood visible to the naked eye.

It is written for people who notice a change in urine colour after a long run, marathon or ultramarathon, and for anyone trying to understand what "exercise-induced haematuria" actually means. It does **not** apply to people taking anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication, people with known urinary-tract disease, or anyone with fever or flank pain — those situations call for medical assessment first, not a training article.

This page is education and triage only. It gives no drugs, no treatment plan and no substitute for a clinician's judgement.

## Typical presentation and incidence

The classic picture is red or tea-coloured urine on the first void after a long run, usually without fever, sometimes with mild suprapubic discomfort, fading over one to two days. Gross haematuria is much less common than microscopic haematuria; most people only find out from a urine test.

Reported incidence varies widely, not because one number is "right", but because **populations, distances, sampling timing and definitions differ**.

| Study | Population and sampling | Definition | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reid et al., 1987 (Br J Urol) | 98 runners, urine before and immediately after a half or full marathon | Red cells (including microscopic) | Haematuria in **21%** of half-marathon and **22%** of marathon runners; proteinuria in **35%** and **69%** respectively |
| Urakami et al., 2018 (IJU Case Reports) | 12 patients presenting with gross haematuria after running, plus a literature review | Gross haematuria | Reported prevalence of running-related haematuria about **20–25%**; gross haematuria about **2%** in marathon runners |
| Chen et al., 2025 (Front Physiol) | 20 male finishers of a 100 km trail ultramarathon | Red cells ≥3/HPF | **0** before the race; **12/20 (60%)** after the race |

The 2025 study also found significant post-race increases in urine myoglobin, protein, microalbumin, specific gravity and creatinine, while **running speed was not associated with the degree of haematuria** (p = 0.368). "Running fast causes blood in urine" is not a reliable explanation; distance, impact and individual factors matter more.

## Three kinds of "red urine"

Dark urine is not automatically haematuria, and a positive dipstick is not automatically red cells — the dipstick detects haem, so myoglobin and free haemoglobin also turn it positive.

| Type | Dipstick haem | Microscopic red cells | Typical colour | Key clue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Haematuria | Positive | **Present** | Red, pink | Red-cell shape may suggest the source; dysmorphic cells favour a glomerular origin |
| Haemoglobinuria | Positive | **Absent or few** | Tea, cola | Intravascular haemolysis; "march haemoglobinuria" after long runs is linked to repeated footstrike |
| Myoglobinuria | Positive | **Absent or few** | Brown, cola | Markedly raised creatine kinase (CK); exertional rhabdomyolysis |

This is why dipstick and microscopy are read together. Dipstick alone can mistake myoglobinuria for haematuria; colour alone can mistake beetroot, some medicines or menstrual blood for haematuria. StatPearls notes that a strongly positive dipstick with no red cells on microscopy should raise myoglobinuria, haemoglobinuria or other pseudo-haematuria.

## Why long-distance running causes it

The literature describes at least four mechanisms, often combined:

1. **Bladder-wall impact.** During running the posterior bladder wall repeatedly strikes the fixed bladder neck, especially when the bladder is empty. In the 12 patients reported by Urakami et al., **9 (75%)** had mucosal contusions at the centre of the posterior wall on cystoscopy; the authors attributed this to repeated contact from vertical motion in an empty bladder. All were male, which the authors explain by the prostate fixing the bladder neck more firmly to the pelvic floor.
2. **Increased glomerular permeability.** During hard exercise, renal blood flow is redistributed and glomerular permeability rises transiently, producing proteinuria and red cells. Reid et al. found dysmorphic red cells in **81%** of the full-marathon group versus **30%** of the half-marathon group, suggesting more glomerular bleeding over longer distances and more non-glomerular (bladder) bleeding over shorter ones.
3. **Renal ischaemia and mechanical vibration.** Splanchnic vasoconstriction, raised renal venous pressure and step-by-step impact can all contribute to transient haematuria and proteinuria.
4. **Footstrike haemolysis.** Repeated impact destroys red cells mechanically, causing haemoglobinuria — "march haemoglobinuria". It differs from myoglobinuria but is also dipstick-positive.

A 1979 BMJ review called these transient changes "athletic pseudonephritis" and cited Blacklock's cystoscopic description: ecchymoses or contusions near the interureteric bar, the posterior rim of the internal meatus and the posterior bladder wall, with "10,000 metres haematuria" from repeated impaction.

## When medical assessment is required

The key boundary: **every episode of blood in urine deserves assessment; do not assume it is exercise-related first.** The NHS page is direct — if you think there is blood in your pee, even a small amount or if you are unsure, seek an urgent GP appointment or contact NHS 111, because blood in urine can be a sign of cancer and earlier assessment is easier to treat.

Seek assessment, or emergency care where relevant, if any of the following apply:

- **Painless visible haematuria**, especially new in people over 40. NICE NG12 recommends a two-week suspected-cancer pathway for people **aged 45 and over** with unexplained visible haematuria without a urinary tract infection.
- **Visible haematuria that persists or recurs after successful treatment of a urinary tract infection** in people aged 45 and over.
- **Aged 60 and over with non-visible haematuria plus dysuria or a raised white-cell count**, which NICE also refers for two-week assessment.
- **Haematuria lasting beyond 48–72 hours**, or recurring after running stops.
- **Fever, flank pain, dysuria, urinary frequency or urgency**, suggesting infection or stones.
- **Anticoagulant or antiplatelet use**, **smoking history**, occupational chemical exposure or a family history of urinary-tract cancer.
- **Clots, difficulty passing urine, marked suprapubic pain**, or dizziness, fainting or severe flank pain.
- **Severe muscle pain, swelling, weakness or reduced urine output** after exercise — consider rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury and treat as an emergency.

Of note, **3 of the 12** patients in Urakami's series (**25%**) turned out to have another urological disease: non-muscle-invasive bladder tumour, lower ureteral tumour and an impacted lower ureteral stone. That is a referral series and not a population incidence, but it shows that "blood after running" cannot always be attributed to exercise.

## What tests a clinician may arrange

This page describes the assessment pathway, not a personal plan:

- **Urinalysis with microscopy**: confirm red cells and examine shape, casts and protein.
- **Urine culture**: exclude urinary tract infection.
- **Urine cytology**: considered when carcinoma in situ is suspected or there is a history of gross haematuria, per StatPearls and AUA pathways.
- **Renal function and full blood count**: assess kidney involvement, infection or anaemia.
- **Urinary imaging**: CT urography is often used in higher-risk patients to examine the upper tract, stones and tumours; ultrasound can be an initial study.
- **Cystoscopy**: used for gross haematuria or high-risk microscopic haematuria; the bladder contusions in Urakami's series were found this way.
- **Repeat testing**: exercise-related changes usually improve within 24–72 hours, and Reid's cohort was normal at 4–12 weeks. Timing and tests should follow clinical risk.

The AUA/SUFU microhaematuria guideline stratifies patients as low, intermediate or high risk using age, sex, smoking, degree and persistence of haematuria and prior gross haematuria, then recommends cystoscopy and upper-tract imaging accordingly. Whether and how deeply to investigate depends on risk, not on the phrase "exercise-induced haematuria".

## An example: dark urine after a long run

Suppose a 38-year-old runner passes tea-coloured urine after a 30 km run, with no fever, flank pain or dysuria; the colour lightens after six hours, but next-day urinalysis still shows 5–10 red cells per high-power field.

A sensible sequence is: stop extra training, drink normally, observe for 24–48 hours; arrange repeat urinalysis with microscopy as advised, to see whether red cells persist and what shape they take; if colour resolves fully, repeat testing is normal and there are no risk factors, a clinician may judge it a transient exercise-related change; if visible haematuria persists, recurs, or the person smokes or takes anticoagulants, further urological assessment is appropriate. This example illustrates triage logic, not a prescription.

## What a runner can and cannot do

- **Can**: note timing, colour, distance, fluid intake and any pain; stop training and observe for 24–72 hours; attend repeat testing as advised.
- **Can**: drink normally after heat or long runs and avoid prolonged low urine output. One case series suggested not deliberately keeping the bladder completely empty before running, but the evidence is weak and this is not a firm rule.
- **Should not**: self-diagnose because "runners get this"; use "drink more water" instead of assessment; take haemostatic drugs, antibiotics or painkillers on their own; continue hard training or racing before visible haematuria is assessed.
- **Should not**: treat one normal repeat test as permanent immunity. Recurrent episodes need re-assessment.

## Common myths

- **"Exercise haematuria is common, so no tests are needed."** What is common is transient, microscopic, self-limiting change. Visible haematuria, persistence beyond 48–72 hours, or risk factors require assessment. The 1979 BMJ conclusion was that haematuria should be treated as urinary-tract disease until proven otherwise.
- **"Dark urine means haematuria."** Beetroot, some medicines, dehydration, menstrual blood, haemoglobinuria and myoglobinuria all change colour. Dipstick and microscopy are both needed.
- **"A positive dipstick means haematuria."** The dipstick detects haem; myoglobinuria is positive with no red cells under the microscope.
- **"Only fast runners get it."** The 2025 ultramarathon study found no association between speed and haematuria severity.
- **"Bladder contusion only happens in men."** The published contusion series happened to be all male, but women can also develop exercise-related haematuria through other mechanisms; do not infer that women cannot.
- **"Two days of rest means it can be ignored."** Symptom resolution supports a transient change but does not replace risk assessment, especially new visible haematuria at 45 or older.

## Checklist

- [ ] Did I notice abnormal urine colour on the first void after a long run?
- [ ] Do I have fever, flank pain, dysuria, clots or markedly reduced urine output?
- [ ] Has visible haematuria lasted beyond 48–72 hours, or recurred after running stopped?
- [ ] Am I 45 or older with new visible haematuria, or do I smoke or take anticoagulants?
- [ ] Have I had urinalysis with microscopy, rather than a dipstick alone?
- [ ] Am I following clinical advice for repeat assessment rather than deciding "it is nothing"?

## Scope and limits

This page is health education, not a diagnostic tool, and gives no drugs, surgery or treatment plan. Exercise-related haematuria is a diagnosis of exclusion: age, risk factors, urine sediment, imaging and cystoscopy all inform a clinician's decision. Pregnant or postpartum people, people who have had surgery, people with a history of urinary-tract disease, people taking anticoagulants or antiplatelets, and immunocompromised people should seek care directly if they see blood in urine. Chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness, inability to pass urine or high fever with rigors are emergencies.
