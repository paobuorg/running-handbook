## What this page covers

**Cardiovascular drift** (CV drift) means that after a period of continuous exercise, cardiovascular variables begin to change in one direction: stroke volume falls progressively, heart rate rises to match, and cardiac output stays nearly constant. The review puts the onset at about 10 minutes of moderate exercise (roughly 50–75% of maximal oxygen uptake) in a neutral or warm environment.

This page is for runners whose heart rate climbs during a long run even though the weather, gradient and pace have not changed, and for anyone wondering why heart-rate zones stop behaving late in a run. It does **not** apply when heart rate jumps within the first few minutes, or when it comes with chest pain or fainting — that is not drift, and it needs urgent or clinical assessment.

## How large is the drift

| Measure | Observation in the review | Context |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | About 10 minutes into moderate exercise | Neutral or warm environment, 50–75% of maximal oxygen uptake |
| Stroke volume | Down 13% between 15 and 55 minutes | Control condition, final core temperature 37.8 °C |
| Heart rate | Up 11% over the same period | Moves in parallel with the stroke-volume fall |
| Cardiac output | Nearly unchanged | The heart-rate rise offsets the stroke-volume fall |
| Skin blood flow | Stable after 20–30 minutes | Not temporally linked to the stroke-volume fall |
| Core temperature and heart rate | r-squared = 0.95 | Higher body temperature, higher heart rate |

A frequently cited comparison: stroke volume was the same at 35 °C and 8 °C despite an approximately four-fold difference in skin blood flow. That undercuts the idea that skin vasodilation is the whole explanation.

## Mechanism — a faster heart reduces stroke volume

The older explanation was that a rising body temperature dilates skin vessels, reduces venous return and lowers ventricular filling, so stroke volume falls. The review argues that direct evidence is weak, because skin blood flow has already stabilised at 20–30 minutes while stroke volume keeps falling. The timing does not match.

The authors' alternative is that the **rise in heart rate itself** shortens ventricular filling time, lowering end-diastolic volume and stroke volume. The supporting experiment "clamped" heart rate by giving a small dose of a beta-1 adrenoceptor blocker before exercise. The rise in heart rate after about 15 minutes was prevented, and the fall in stroke volume was eliminated, while skin blood flow was unchanged.

The causal chain to remember is: exercise continues → body temperature and sympathetic activity rise → heart rate rises → filling time shortens → stroke volume falls → heart rate rises further to defend cardiac output. It is a self-reinforcing loop, not simple vasodilation.

## How dehydration amplifies drift

Dehydration is not required for drift, but it greatly amplifies it. In the heat experiment cited by the review, trained cyclists exercised at 62–65% of maximal oxygen uptake for 120 minutes at 35 °C and lost 4.9% of body mass:

| Measure | Change |
|---|---|
| Stroke volume | Down 28% |
| Cardiac output | Down 18% |
| Core temperature | From 38.0 °C to 39.3 °C |
| Systemic vascular resistance | Up 17% |
| Skin blood flow | Reduced, limiting heat loss |

In the same experiment, preventing dehydration with fluid intake kept cardiovascular responses remarkably stable between 20 and 120 minutes. In the heat, a large part of drift therefore comes from dehydration, not merely from elapsed exercise time.

Mechanistically, dehydration lowers blood volume by about 3–5%. In the heat, expanding blood volume offsets only about half of the stroke-volume fall. Hypovolaemia alone, produced by dehydration in a cold environment, lowered stroke volume by about 7%; combined with hyperthermia, the fall reached 20–28%.

## Four training implications

1. **Heart-rate zones drift late in a run.** The same heart rate may correspond to a lower actual intensity. If you set a ceiling as a percentage of maximum heart rate, the late stages of a long run can look "below the ceiling" when the effort is already high.
2. **Do not chase pace with heart rate in the heat.** Drift plus dehydration pushes heart rate upward; speeding up shortens filling time further. Use perceived effort, breathing and planned time instead.
3. **Fluids are the main lever.** You cannot abolish normal drift, but you can shrink the dehydration component. Use thirst, urine colour and pre-to-post-run body mass, not just "how many sips I took".
4. **Drift itself is not a disease.** It is a normal response to prolonged exercise. What matters is a rise that starts unusually early, is unusually large, or comes with symptoms.

## What to do — recognise and respond

1. **Rule out external change first.** Check whether pace, gradient, weather or direct sun changed. If they did, the heart-rate rise may simply be a higher load.
2. **Look at the timescale.** Drift is a slow, progressive rise after 10 minutes. A jump in the first few minutes is more likely to be insufficient warm-up, nerves, caffeine or an arrhythmia.
3. **Compare with how you feel.** If heart rate is up but breathing, speech and legs are stable, drift is likely. If there is dizziness, nausea or cold clammy skin, stop.
4. **Respond in order.** Cooling (shade, wind, cold water) → fluids → slower pace → end the session if needed. Do not speed up to "push the heart rate down".
5. **Record it.** On a similar route and in similar weather, compare the average heart rate over the first 20 minutes with the last 20 minutes. That trend is more useful than a single absolute number.

## Normal drift versus a warning sign

| Observation | More like normal drift | More like a reason to stop and assess |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Slow rise after 10 minutes | Jump within the first few minutes |
| Speed of change | Gradual over time | Large rise in a short period that does not settle |
| Sensations | Breathing and legs feel stable | Dizziness, nausea, cold clammy skin, chest pain, fainting |
| After cooling and fluids | Partly settles | Does not settle or worsens |
| Environment | Neutral or warm, long duration | Extreme heat, marked dehydration, or fever and infection |

## A worked example (hypothetical)

Runner: 30 °C and 60% relative humidity, running 5:30 per kilometre for the first 20 minutes at an average heart rate of 150; at 60 minutes, still at 5:30 per kilometre, the average heart rate is 165.

Read it this way: pace and gradient are unchanged, and a 15-beat rise fits the timescale of drift; in the heat, dehydration and rising body temperature amplify it. The response is cooling, fluids and, if needed, slowing to 5:45–6:00 per kilometre — not holding the pace. The numbers here are hypothetical and illustrate the reading, not a prediction.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A higher heart rate means I am getting stronger."** Drift is a higher heart rate at the same load; it is not the same as a fitness gain.
- **"Heart-rate zones control intensity precisely."** Zones are estimates based on maximum heart rate or thresholds and carry error; drift distorts them late in a run.
- **"Drinking enough eliminates drift."** Fluids reduce the dehydration component, but normal drift still occurs.
- **"Sweating a lot proves the session worked."** Sweat rate depends on body mass, temperature, humidity, wind and acclimation; it is not a direct intensity measure.
- **"A high heart rate always means a heart problem."** Most cases involve drift, heat, dehydration, caffeine or poor sleep. Chest pain, fainting or unusual palpitations do need assessment.

## When to seek medical help

- Chest pain or tightness, fainting or near-fainting;
- A sudden very fast or irregular heart rate, or a persistently and clearly elevated resting heart rate;
- Dizziness, nausea, cold clammy skin or confusion during exercise (treat as heat illness and call emergency services);
- A long-term rise in heart rate at an easy pace alongside fever, infection or unexplained weight change;
- Anyone with known cardiovascular disease or taking rate-affecting medication such as beta-blockers should interpret heart rate with medical guidance.

## Checklist

- [ ] Have pace, gradient and weather changed since the last run?
- [ ] Did the rise begin gradually after 10 minutes?
- [ ] Are breathing and speech still stable?
- [ ] Have I cooled down and taken fluids instead of speeding up?
- [ ] Did I record the first-20-minute and last-20-minute average heart rates?
- [ ] Is there any chest pain, fainting or unusual palpitation?

## Limits

This page is exercise physiology education, not diagnosis or treatment. The classic drift studies used moderate intensity in neutral or warm conditions over 60–120 minutes; they do not transfer directly to short intervals, extreme cold or high-intensity racing. The beta-blocker experiment was a research protocol — readers should not use medication to "control" heart rate. People taking blood-pressure, anti-arrhythmic or beta-blocker medication have altered heart-rate responses and should ask their clinician.

## Review rhythm: every 15–20 minutes

On a long run, log heart rate, perceived effort and fluid intake at a checkpoint **every 15–20 minutes**; on the same route and in similar weather, review the drift every 3–4 weeks. That gives you a trend instead of a single number.

If drift at the same pace grows for two consecutive reviews, check dehydration, sleep and heat acclimation before assuming fitness has dropped; do not rewrite the plan from one outlier, and do not add intensity because one drift value looked small.
