## Definition and preconditions

Hill and mountain-path basics means first sessions on sustained uneven ground, on foot, not in a race. It applies to runners who mostly run flat and want to try hills. It holds when the route matches experience, a plan is shared, and the runner turns back when conditions change.

## Choosing a first route

Start short: a hypothetical 5 to 8 km outing with 200 to 400 m of gain. Pick a path others use, with clear entry and exit. Check weather and sunset, and leave a trip plan with route, contacts, return time and companions. Coverage may be absent; consider a locator beacon and a Plan B.

## Effort and the talk test

In the talk test, a full sentence means effort is controlled; a few words mean walk. Alternating running and walking is normal. Return with something left.

## Uphill and downhill are different

Uphill running shows higher step frequency, shorter swing and aerial phase and a greater duty factor; downhill shows longer aerial time, lower step frequency and more rear-foot striking. Downhill emphasises eccentric work; uphill needs greater muscular work at the hip. Use short steps uphill, walk the steep parts, and avoid charging downhill.

## Poles are optional

Poles are optional, not mandatory kit. They share load on long climbs, steep descents or with a pack. Practise length and timing on a short slope.

## Weather, light and margin

Check the forecast, prepare for lightning and flash flooding and keep a Plan B. Carry illumination, spare batteries and extra clothing. Allow at least 1 hour of daylight margin; routes are slower than the map.

## Water and food

Carry water for the conditions, not thirst, and turn back before half is gone. Trail water is not guaranteed; carry treatment if you plan to refill.

## How to do it: three steps

1. Pick 5 to 8 km with no more than 400 m of gain, check weather and sunset, and send a trip plan with route, contacts and return time to a trusted person.
2. Jog where a full sentence is possible, walk where it is not; drink every 20 to 30 minutes.
3. Set a turnaround time; at that time, or with half the water gone, turn back whether or not the top is reached.

## Worked example: 12 km with 600 m of gain

All numbers are hypothetical. Assume 12 km out and back with 600 m of gain, 6 min/km on flat, 9 min/km uphill and 7 min/km downhill. Assume 50 minutes out and 42 minutes back, 92 minutes moving, plus 30 minutes of breaks: 2 hours. Start at 09:00 and expect to return at 11:00. Allow a 2-hour margin, with latest return at 13:00 and turnaround at 12:15. Sunset at 18:30 leaves ample light.

## Terrain and action

| Terrain | Action | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gentle climb | Short-step jog | Sprinting early |
| Steep climb | Walk, hands on thighs | Overstriding |
| Rocky descent | Shorten steps, look ahead | Ankle rolls |

## Reading the terrain before you commit

A profile on a screen compresses distance and hides what the ground actually does. Before committing to a hill route, identify three things: the steepest sustained section, the point where the path becomes exposed or unclear, and the last place where turning back is simple. On a mountain path the descent often takes as long as the climb when the surface is loose, so budget the return leg separately rather than halving the uphill estimate.

Daylight is a resource you can calculate. Take the local sunset time, subtract the time you need for the descent and a margin for a wrong turn, and that gives your latest summit time. In winter the margin should be larger because snow and ice slow every step and can hide markers. A headlamp belongs in the pack even on a short planned day, because a minor delay compounds quickly.

## Common misconceptions

- Treating a mountain path as a flat run at flat pace.
- Relying on phone navigation alone; GPS can be unreliable, so carry a map and compass.
- Waiting until half the water is gone before planning the return.

## When to turn back, stop or seek care

Turn back at the planned time, when half the water is gone, when weather turns to lightning, wind, fog or flash-flood signs, or when light fails. Seek help for confusion, dehydration, abnormal body temperature, chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness or inability to walk.

## Frequency and progression

Start with one hill session weekly, the rest on flat ground. After 4 stable weeks, add 100 to 200 m of gain or 1 to 2 km, one change at a time, with a lighter week every 4 weeks.

## Checklist

- Trip plan sent with route, contacts and return time.
- Water sized to weather and duration; half-left rule understood.
- Map, compass or offline navigation, headlamp and spare batteries.
- Extra clothing, whistle, first aid, food and sun protection.

## Scope and limits

This page is introductory and does not cover race tactics, technical descents or night navigation. Route, weather and health vary, so general advice cannot replace local information, a guide or medical assessment. People with cardiovascular, respiratory or joint history, or who are pregnant, should consult a professional.
