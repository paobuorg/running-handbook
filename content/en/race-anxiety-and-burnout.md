## What race anxiety and training burnout mean, and who this is for

This page covers two states often mixed together: race anxiety (nerves, worry and physical arousal) and training burnout (loss of interest with persistent exhaustion and declining performance). It is for runners preparing for a race and for their coaches. Premise: emotion and training load affect each other. First condition: separate ordinary nerves from worry that persistently affects daily life before deciding whether to adjust the plan or seek help.

## Pre-race nerves are not automatically an anxiety disorder

The NHS notes that anxiety is a common response to stress and that occasional worry does not mean generalised anxiety disorder (GAD). GAD is more likely when anxiety is present most of the time, difficult to control and affecting daily life. Symptoms include sleep difficulty, restlessness, irritability, fatigue, muscle tension, stomach problems and palpitations.

Fast heartbeat, repeated kit checks and poor sleep before a race are familiar. If worry lasts weeks, affects work or sleep, or makes training difficult, consider a clinician or mental-health professional; adults can also refer themselves to talking therapies.

| Experience | Common signs | Signal to seek help |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-race nerves | Fast heartbeat, kit checks, slower sleep | Lasting weeks and affecting work or sleep |
| Generalised anxiety | Worry most of the time, hard to control | Affects daily life with body symptoms |
| Training burnout | Exhaustion, lost interest, falling performance | Still present after reducing load |

## Training burnout is not laziness

WHO lists "burn-out" as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition, and states it should not be applied to other areas of life. Runners' "training burnout" is not a formal diagnosis; it may combine insufficient sleep, low energy intake, life stress, repetitive training and illness.

If exhaustion, lost interest, a clear performance drop, worsening sleep or low mood lasts more than a week or two, reduce volume and intensity, review sleep, food and stress, and consider medical or psychological assessment. Do not use "push through" to mask worsening signals. Burnout is not the same as depression; if low mood and lost interest affect daily life, let a professional judge.

## Small actions that help

Rather than telling yourself to "just stop worrying", put attention on actions: prepare kit and route in advance, use a familiar warm-up, and choose a few specific cues ("relax the first 5 km", "shorten the stride uphill").

Avoid new caffeine, supplements or rituals on race day; they can amplify palpitations and anxiety. Talking to a friend or a familiar breathing practice beats refreshing race predictions.

## Life load and goals

Work, family, caregiving and financial stress all affect training. Goals can change: treating a race as an experience, shortening the distance, deferring it, or maintaining health are all legitimate choices. Rest is not failure.

## How to name the pressure in three steps

1. Name it: write one sentence about what you feel, turning vague tension into a concrete problem.
2. Classify it: decide whether it is ordinary pre-race nerves, persistent worry, or load-related burnout, and check the table for help signals.
3. Act: for ordinary nerves, fix the routine and remove last-minute variables; for weeks of worry affecting daily life, reduce load and book professional help.

## A worked example: three weeks before a race (hypothetical)

Assume 3 weeks of logs before a race. Week 1: 5 sessions, 50 km, sleep 7.5 hours, anxiety 4 out of 10. Week 2: 6 sessions, 60 km, sleep 6.5 hours, anxiety 6. Week 3: 4 sessions, 35 km, sleep 6 hours, anxiety 7. Volume rose then fell, sleep kept falling and anxiety kept rising, which suggests restoring sleep and simplifying the race routine. Numbers are hypothetical.

## Review rhythm before and during a season

Review sleep and training feel weekly, start an anxiety self-rating 3 weeks before a race, and debrief 1 week after it. Check every 4 weeks whether the season's goals still make sense; if the same goal brings persistent worry at two consecutive checks, adjust the goal rather than adding training.

## Common misconceptions

- "Nerves mean I am unprepared": moderate arousal is part of racing.
- "Burnout is laziness": exhaustion and lost interest usually point to load or recovery.
- "Rest lets others pass me": a short reduction protects long-term training better than pushing through.

## When to stop and when to seek help

Pause training and reduce load for exhaustion lasting more than a week or two, several nights of poor sleep, lost interest, a clear performance drop, or pre-race worry that already affects work and daily life. Discuss it promptly with a clinician or mental-health professional.

Seek medical help first for persistent palpitations, chest pain, fainting or unusual breathlessness. If you or someone else is at risk of self-harm or in a mental-health crisis, contact local emergency or crisis services.

## Checklist

- The main current worry is written in one sentence.
- Pre-race nerves, persistent worry and training burnout have been separated.
- Sleep duration and training feel are recorded for this week.
- The race routine is fixed, with no new caffeine or rituals.
- Help has been booked when problems lasted over 2 weeks and affect daily life.

## Scope and limits

This page does not diagnose anxiety, depression, eating disorders or overtraining, and gives no treatment advice. Mood and sleep problems need professional assessment.
