## What this page covers, and who it is for

This page turns general activity guidance into runnable plans when age, pregnancy or postpartum status, or a chronic condition changes the terms. It is for adults who want to keep running. Premise: population guidance is a starting point and individual limits need a clinician. First condition: identify your group and any medical issue before setting weekly volume.

## The baseline for most adults

The CDC recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity weekly, or equivalent vigorous activity, plus strength work on at least two days. Activity can be spread across the week, and some activity beats none. These are population minimums, not race plans.

| Group | Weekly aerobic target | Strength and other |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Most adults | 150 minutes moderate or 75 vigorous | Strength on 2 days |
| 65 and older | 150 minutes moderate or 75 vigorous | Strength and balance work |
| Chronic condition | 150 minutes moderate when able | Strength on 2 days |
| Pregnancy and postpartum | At least 150 minutes moderate, in chunks | Discuss limits with a clinician |

## Women, pregnancy and postpartum

Women runners follow the same aerobic and strength guidance as men. The CDC states activity is healthy and safe for most pregnant women; healthy pregnant or postpartum women need at least 150 minutes of moderate activity weekly, in smaller chunks, and should discuss restrictions with their clinician.

Postpartum recovery varies widely; pelvic-floor symptoms, persistent bleeding, wound problems, dizziness or pain need assessment. Breastfeeding demands and broken sleep also change tolerable load.

## Older runners

CDC guidance for adults 65 and older includes aerobic, strengthening and balance activities. Age alone is not a reason to stop, but balance, bone health, medication, previous injury and recovery speed belong in the plan. Strength, balance and gradual loading matter more than pace comparisons; swapping running for brisk walking or run-walk intervals buys recovery.

## Chronic conditions and medication

Adults with chronic conditions or disabilities can be active and gain benefits. When able, aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity weekly plus two days of strengthening; some activity beats none, and it can improve function, sleep, mental and cardiovascular health.

Medicines may affect heart rate, temperature regulation or blood glucose. Discuss intensity and monitoring with a clinician who knows your condition, and never change medication yourself.

## Weight, sleep and other factors

Weight is influenced by sleep, age, genes and medication, and active people can still gain weight if intake exceeds use. Treating running as the only weight tool invites overtraining when the scale stalls. For weight change tied to illness or medication, or stubborn weight loss, ask a health care provider; short sleep also affects appetite and recovery.

## How to put the guidance into one week, in three steps

1. Set the baseline: write down the minutes you can complete reliably; if it is under 150, start there.
2. Allocate types: split aerobic work across 2 to 5 sessions, fix 2 strength days, and add balance work for people 65 and older or with poor balance.
3. Set progression: change 1 variable every 2 to 4 weeks and record pain, sleep and fatigue.

## A worked example: splitting 150 minutes across a week (hypothetical)

Assume an adult can train 5 days a week. Plan 3 easy runs of 30 minutes, 90 minutes total, plus 2 brisk walks of 30 minutes, 60 minutes; together exactly 150 minutes. Put strength work on 2 of those days, 20 minutes each. This split is hypothetical, not the only option; a week with 90 minutes still beats no activity.

## Common misconceptions

- "The 150 minutes must be one session": it can be split into 10 to 30-minute blocks.
- "Pregnancy means no exercise": activity is safe for most pregnant women, but limits need clinician confirmation.
- "Getting older means stopping": balance, strength and recovery matter more than age.
- "Running guarantees weight loss": active people can still gain weight.

## Frequency, cycles and progression

Spread aerobic activity across the week rather than stacking it at the weekend. Keep strength on at least 2 days, away from the hardest runs. Use 2 to 4 weeks as one review cycle and change 1 variable at a time; if effort at the same pace keeps rising, make the next week a maintenance week. In pregnancy, postpartum and chronic-condition phases, involve a clinician.

## When to stop and when to seek help

Stop and seek advice for chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness, altered consciousness, persistent bleeding, pain after a fall, new joint swelling, or unusual pregnancy symptoms. With a chronic condition, pause during infection, fever or marked glucose swings. Do not mask signals that need assessment.

## Checklist

- The aerobic minutes you can complete reliably are written down.
- The week includes at least 2 days of strength training.
- People 65 and older or with poor balance have added balance work.
- Pregnancy, postpartum or chronic-condition limits have been discussed with a clinician.
- Progression changes 1 variable at a time, with a review after 2 to 4 weeks.

## Scope and limits

This page does not diagnose disease, prescribe individual exercise, or replace maternity, cardiology, endocrinology or other specialist care. Chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness, altered consciousness, persistent bleeding or marked deterioration need urgent medical help.
