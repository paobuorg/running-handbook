## Definition and scope

Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S) describes a syndrome of health and performance effects associated with low energy availability: after exercise energy is subtracted, the remaining energy cannot support normal physiology. This page is for female runners with menstrual cycles, high training loads or recent weight control. The 2023 International Olympic Committee consensus states that RED-S applies to both male and female athletes, and it is a framework for risk recognition and referral, not self-diagnosis. One typical case: training volume rising, weight falling and menstrual changes together call for professional assessment, not more training. It is not simply eating too little: rising training without matching intake, poor recovery or deliberate weight control can all contribute.

## Common signals

Female runners may notice menstrual changes, recurrent stress fractures, persistent fatigue, poor sleep, feeling cold, digestive complaints, low mood and declining performance; men may notice reduced sex hormones, libido and bone density. One symptom does not prove RED-S, but problems across several systems deserve attention, especially with long-term low body weight or rapid weight loss.

## How to: three steps toward assessment

1. Record: for four weeks, log training duration, weight change, menstrual dates, sleep, fatigue and stress-fracture history.
2. Seek care: take it to sports medicine, endocrinology or gynaecology; add dietitian and psychological support if eating behaviour is a concern.
3. Adjust: under guidance, reduce volume or intensity, secure energy rather than cut it, protect recovery and judge progress in weeks.

## Table: signal, meaning, next step

| Signal | Possible meaning | Next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Menstrual change or missed periods | Low energy or another gynaecological or endocrine cause | Clinical assessment, no self-attribution |
| Recurrent stress fractures | Bone health and energy availability may be affected | Sports-medicine review and bone density check |
| Persistent fatigue, falling performance | Load and intake | Log four weeks and reduce load |
| Feeling cold, digestion issues, low mood | Several systems may be involved | Whole-person assessment |
| Rapid weight loss | The energy gap may be widening | Pause weight goals, prioritise recovery |

## Worked example: a gap that "looks small" (hypothetical)

All figures are hypothetical. Assume 2200 kcal intake, 1800 kcal baseline needs and 500 kcal burned in training, leaving about −100 kcal that day; over 12 weeks with five sessions a week the gap reaches thousands of kilocalories. With weight down 3 kg and the cycle 6 weeks late, those signals warrant review, not another 60-minute run.

## Pregnancy and postpartum

The CDC states that physical activity is healthy and safe for most pregnant women, but restrictions should be discussed with their health care provider. The aim is at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week, split into smaller chunks, with weight-gain goals agreed with the provider. Running intensity and contraindications depend on gestation, complications, delivery and recovery. Seek urgent care for bleeding, abdominal pain, dizziness, chest pain, regular contractions or reduced fetal movement.

## Common misconceptions

- Treating amenorrhoea as proof that training works; it is more likely a signal to assess.
- Assuming a normal body weight rules out RED-S; energy availability is a balance, not an appearance.
- Answering declining performance with more training and less food.

## When to stop and seek care

Stop adding training and seek assessment for recurrent stress fractures, persistent amenorrhoea, rapid unplanned weight loss, fatigue affecting daily life, loss of control around food, or worsening depression. Chest pain, fainting, severe palpitations, unrelenting bone pain, or pregnancy bleeding or reduced fetal movement are emergencies.

## Frequency and review rhythm

Log training, sleep, weight and menstrual dates weekly; review with a clinician or dietitian every four weeks; assess bone health, menstrual recovery and load every three to six months; revisit target weight and race plans each 12-week cycle. Restore energy availability and health markers first, then volume, then intensity.

## Check yourself

- Menstrual dates for the last four weeks are recorded and changes shared with a clinician.
- Declining performance has not been answered with more training or less food.
- A plan for medical review and nutrition support exists.

## Scope and limits

This page does not diagnose RED-S, menstrual disorders, eating disorders or osteoporosis, and does not prescribe individual calorie or training targets. Persistent menstrual change, recurrent fractures, rapid weight change, eating-behaviour concerns, or pregnancy questions should go to a doctor, dietitian or sports-medicine team.
