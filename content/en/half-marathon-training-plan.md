## Definitions and scope

This page describes the **structure of a multi-week block for a 21.0975 km race**: how long the block should be, how many quality sessions per week, how long the long run should get, how to taper, and how to derive race pace from a recent result. It does not provide a pace chart with second-by-second targets.

Assumptions: you can already run continuously for 60 minutes, have had a stable weekly volume over the past four weeks (roughly 20 km or more), have no acute injury, are 10–16 weeks from the target race, and can run at least three times a week. If you are just starting, or your goal is simply to finish, use a beginner plan. If you are returning from injury, pregnant, or have cardiovascular, metabolic or respiratory disease, intensity should be set by a doctor or qualified coach and this page does not apply.

**A generic plan cannot replace individual coaching.** The same weekly structure means a different load for runners with different volumes, recovery capacities and life stress.

## Start with inputs: goal, baseline and recent result

| Input | How to obtain it | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| Race date | Count back from race day | Block length and taper start |
| Current weekly volume | Average of the last four weeks, excluding outliers | How much room there is to build |
| Recent result | A 10 km or half marathon in the past 6–8 weeks, run at full effort in similar conditions | Relative paces rather than guesswork |
| Available training days | The days you can actually run, not the ideal plan | Whether quality sessions and the long run can be separated |

## Verification result: what half-marathon research does and does not provide

| Available | Not available |
|---|---|
| A cohort of 556 half-marathon runners linking weekly volume and longest run to finish time (Fokkema 2020) | No official body publishes how many phases a half-marathon plan should have, or how long each should last |
| A taper meta-analysis and descriptive data from elite runners (Bosquet 2007; Haugen 2022) | No study compares 12-week and 16-week amateur plans |
| Verifiable predictors of half-marathon time (Cooper test distance, vVO₂max) (Alvero-Cruz 2019) | No unified number for quality sessions per week |

The block length, phase split and session counts below are therefore **site-authored examples**, not research findings. Where research gives no ratio, the text says unknown.

## Five structural parameters

| Parameter | Example in this page | Basis and status |
|---|---|---|
| Block length | 12 weeks; 10 weeks with a solid base, 16 weeks with a low base | Site example. Public sources give no universal length (unknown); Fokkema surveyed the preparation period but did not prescribe weeks |
| Phases | 4 weeks base + 6 weeks specific + 2 weeks taper | Site example. Haugen 2022 describes elite tapering starting 7–10 days out, not amateur phase ratios |
| Quality sessions | 1–2 per week, at least 2 days apart | Site guidance. Seiler 2010 observed about 20% of elite sessions at high intensity, roughly 2–3 per week; amateurs take fewer |
| Long run | Once a week, building towards but not requiring 21 km | Fokkema 2020: a longest run **>21 km** was associated with a faster finish (beta −3.87 min). This is an association, not a prescription to cover 21 km |
| Taper | Start 2 weeks out; reduce volume 41%–60%; keep intensity and frequency | Bosquet 2007 meta-analysis of 27 studies: a 2-week taper with an exponential 41%–60% volume reduction was optimal; Haugen 2022: elite start 7–10 days out |

The most common confusion is the taper: **volume comes down, intensity does not**. Bosquet's analysis found performance gains when intensity (effect 0.33) and frequency (effect 0.35) were unchanged. Cutting the quality sessions too changes the training, rather than tapering it.

## The role of the long run: how the half differs from 5K/10K

The practical difference between half-marathon and 5K/10K training is not the quality session; it is **whether a long run is necessary**.

- **5K/10K**: race duration is short; the long run builds aerobic base and running economy and does not need to approach race distance. The 8-week structure in the 5K/10K article only requires "the longest easy run of the week", with no distance target.
- **Half marathon**: the race lasts roughly 90 minutes to 2 hours, so glycogen, muscular fatigue resistance and pace maintenance become limiting. In Fokkema's half-marathon sample, runners whose longest run exceeded 21 km finished faster and faded less in the second half (beta −1.89 min).
- **Direction of the evidence**: this is observational. Runners who train more and longer may simply have more years of training and better fitness; the study did not show that extending a long run from 15 km to 22 km produces a 3.87-minute improvement.

**No percentage of weekly volume is given for the long run.** Public sources do not fix a universal ratio for half-marathon plans (unknown). This page only sets rules: the long run is the longest easy run of the week, separated from quality work by at least one day, and the 24-hour response decides whether volume rises next week.

## Deriving race pace from a recent result

Start with a recent race or time trial: pace = time ÷ distance. For example, if a runner covers 10 km in 50:00, the average 10 km pace is 5:00/km; this is an arithmetic example, not a prescription.

For cross-distance conversion, use the site's [race prediction tool](https://tools.paobu.org/en/tools/race-predict/): enter a recent result and a target distance to get an equivalent performance estimate. It relies on empirical formulas whose exponents differ — Blödorn and Döring (2025) found that a Riegel exponent of 1.07732 versus the commonly used 1.06 changes the result by about 10 seconds over 5 km, with the gap growing at longer distances. Treat the output as a **starting range**, not a race target.

There is also a verifiable field-test route: the Cooper test (12-minute distance). In 23 recreational male runners, Alvero-Cruz 2019 found Cooper distance correlated with half-marathon time at **r = −0.93**, better than laboratory vVO₂max at r = −0.84; the Cooper-based prediction had R² = 0.873 and a standard error of 3.78 minutes. The authors restrict extrapolation to recreational male runners.

In training, use **relative descriptions only**:

- **Easy running**: full sentences possible, singing not; about 40%–59% heart rate reserve (ACSM).
- **Threshold running**: just faster than lactate threshold, "comfortably hard", near 10 km pace or slightly slower.
- **Half-marathon pace**: the average pace of a recent half marathon, or a 10 km-derived equivalent run slightly slower.
- **Short accelerations / hill sprints**: fast and relaxed, no stopwatch target; the purpose is neuromuscular priming and form.

## A 12-week example structure

The table is an **illustrative assumption** showing structure, not a validated plan. Quality sessions are described by relative intensity, without paces.

| Week | Phase | Quality session | Long run | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Base | One short acceleration or hill session | Longest easy run of the week | Record only, no pace chasing |
| 2 | Base | One threshold session in short repetitions | Easy long run | At least one day after quality |
| 3 | Base | One threshold session with longer repetitions | Easy long run | Watch the 24-hour response |
| 4 | Base | Quality volume halved | Shortened or skipped | Lighter week, no testing |
| 5 | Specific | One threshold + one half-marathon-pace interval session | Easy long run | At least 2 days between quality sessions |
| 6 | Specific | One continuous half-marathon-pace run in 2–3 blocks | Longest run of the block so far | Do not attempt full race pace throughout |
| 7 | Specific | One threshold + one short interval session | Long run near the longest | Add no new variables |
| 8 | Specific | Quality volume halved | Shortened | Lighter week |
| 9 | Specific | One long block at half-marathon pace | Longest run of the block | Rehearse race pace and fuelling |
| 10 | Specific | One threshold session with shorter repetitions | Clearly shortened | Last full quality session |
| 11 | Taper | Short intervals, intensity retained | Shortened | Volume falls, intensity stays |
| 12 | Race week | 2–3 short easy runs with accelerations | None | No new shoes, fuel or course |

## Pace execution: even is steadier than fast-then-slow

A PLOS ONE 2026 analysis of 78,912 Boston Marathon finishers found that even pacing was the largest group (47.9%) and was associated with the fastest finish times; strong positive splitting accounted for 12.2% and was about 59 minutes slower on average than even pacing. Among runners under 3:00, 82.0% used even pacing, compared with 19.6% of casual participants.

These are marathon data and cannot be transferred directly to the half marathon, but the direction is useful: **time gained by running the first half faster than target is usually repaid, with interest, in the second half.**

## The most common execution errors

1. **Treating "over 32 km/week" as a threshold.** Fokkema's 32 km was a sample grouping cut-off, not a pass mark; jumping from 25 km to 35 km adds injury risk, not fitness.
2. **Never taking the long run near race distance.** A half-marathon block needs several 16–21 km runs, though not necessarily 21.1 km; the 5K/10K structure does not transfer.
3. **Stacking quality sessions back to back.** Two hard sessions on consecutive days become one poor session.
4. **Cutting intensity during the taper.** Bosquet's conclusion is lower volume with intensity and frequency retained.
5. **Racing the half at 5K pace.** Half-marathon pace sits near or slightly slower than threshold pace.
6. **Trying new shoes, fuel or breakfast on race day.** Every variable should have been tested in the week-9 long run.
7. **Adding volume without watching pace decline.** A marked second-half fade points to pacing or endurance limits, not willpower.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A half-marathon plan must be 16 weeks"**: no public source fixes a universal length; 12 weeks is a common template, not a research finding.
- **"The longest run must reach 21 km"**: Fokkema's >21 km figure is an association, not a requirement; most plans peak at 16–19 km.
- **"More volume is always better"**: higher volume was associated with faster finishes, and the same study found no association with injury; that does not make unlimited increases safe.
- **"Tapering means running less"**: taper volume, retain intensity and frequency.
- **"The Cooper test predicts my half marathon precisely"**: R² = 0.873 with a 3.78-minute standard error still leaves meaningful uncertainty, and the sample was recreational men only.
- **"The race prediction tool gives me a target"**: it gives an equivalent-performance estimate, not a guarantee for race day.

## Checklist

- [ ] Do I know my average weekly volume over the past four weeks?
- [ ] Is my recent result from the past 6–8 weeks, run at full effort in similar conditions?
- [ ] Is the long run separated from quality work by at least one day?
- [ ] Are quality sessions at least two days apart?
- [ ] During the taper, did I keep intensity and frequency while cutting volume?
- [ ] Is race pace derived from my own result and relative effort, not someone else's plan?
- [ ] Did I rehearse race shoes, fuel and breakfast in the week-9 long run?

## Limits of this page

This is training education, not an individual prescription. A generic plan cannot replace coaching; where a coach, doctor or physiotherapist is involved, their assessment takes precedence. Stop and seek medical care if chest pain, dizziness, unusual breathlessness or steadily worsening pain appears during training, or if resting heart rate is clearly elevated for two weeks alongside falling performance.
