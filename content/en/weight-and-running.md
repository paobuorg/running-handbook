## Definition and who this page applies to

Weight and running means understanding how body weight, energy intake and performance interact without damaging health or training quality; lighter is not automatically faster. It applies to runners who run regularly and are considering a weight change, and to people whose race and weight goals conflict. It holds when you are not pregnant, breastfeeding or in rapid adolescent growth, and have no history of disordered eating.

## Weight is not the only performance variable

Running lifts and decelerates body mass, so weight affects energy cost per kilometre, though performance also depends on training, pacing and economy. The CDC notes that weight is influenced by sleep, age, genes and medications, and that active people can still gain weight when intake exceeds expenditure. Its adult guidance recommends 150 minutes of moderate activity weekly plus two days of strength work.

## Risks of low energy availability

Chronic low energy availability is linked to RED-S. The 2023 International Olympic Committee consensus updated the female athlete triad framework to RED-S, which applies to male and female athletes and describes endocrine, bone, metabolic and psychological effects; it requires clinical assessment. Rapid weight loss, menstrual changes, recurrent stress fractures, persistent fatigue or loss of control around food are signals; long-term under-fuelling can reduce recovery, bone density and immunity.

## Race goals and weight

If a race goal involves weight change, do it slowly and only when it does not compromise training or health, with a doctor or registered dietitian. The CDC advises consulting a provider when illness or medication affects weight. Do not tie the race date to a weight-loss deadline; more training, less food and race pressure is one of the riskiest combinations.

## Weight-management cycle and progression

Observe weight over months, not weeks. Record training volume, whether intake covers training, sleep and perceived energy for four weeks. If you change, set one small monthly goal and review performance, menstruation where relevant, mood and injury signals. Persistent fatigue means pausing the goal.

## How to assess a weight goal in three steps

1. Write down training volume, race date and weight goal, and check for conflict; if they conflict, postpone the weight goal.
2. Record for four weeks whether food covers training days, plus sleep and energy; if performance falls, increase intake and reduce load.
3. If change is still wanted, assume a small monthly change and work with a doctor or dietitian, reviewing training, sleep, mood and signals.

## Energy and training table

| Situation | Energy focus | Training arrangement | Risk note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Easy run | Cover basic needs | Keep effort easy | A long deficit accumulates fatigue |
| Quality or long run | Enough carbohydrate | Fuel before and after | Fasting hard sessions adds strain |
| Pre-race taper | Maintain normal eating | Reduce volume, keep intensity | Do not diet at the same time |

## Worked example: eight weeks before a race

Assume a 60 kg runner covering 50 km a week plans a 2 kg change, about 1 kg per month, eight weeks before a half marathon. The first four weeks keep 50 km and log sleep; the last four weeks taper from 50 km to 30 km and stop weight loss while maintaining eating. This avoids stacking more training, less food and race pressure. If persistent fatigue appears in week three, pause the goal. Hypothetical example.

## Common misconceptions

- “Lighter always means faster.” Weight is one variable, and chronic low energy harms recovery, bone and immunity.
- “More running cancels out more eating.” Active people can still gain weight when intake exceeds expenditure.
- “Weight loss and tapering can run together.” More training, less food and race pressure is one of the riskiest combinations.

## When to stop and seek help

Stop the weight goal and seek assessment for menstrual change, recurrent stress fractures, persistent fatigue, feeling cold or worse sleep, rapid weight loss, loss of control around food, or declining performance. RED-S needs clinical assessment. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, adolescence, diabetes or disordered-eating history should be discussed with a clinician first.

## Checklist

- [ ] The weight goal is not tied to the race date.
- [ ] Training-day intake covers quality sessions and long runs.
- [ ] Only one small, sustainable goal is set per month.
- [ ] Sleep, mood and injury signals are logged.

## Scope and limits

This page does not provide a weight-loss plan, calorie target or body-fat standard, or diagnose eating disorders, RED-S or metabolic disease. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, adolescents, chronic disease or disordered-eating history should discuss goals with a doctor or dietitian.
