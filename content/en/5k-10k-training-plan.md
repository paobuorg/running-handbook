## Definition and scope

This page is about the **several-week training structure that prepares you for a 5 km or 10 km race**: how to arrange easy runs, quality sessions, the long run and the pre-race taper, and how to derive relative paces from a recent result rather than copying a table of exact splits.

Scope: you can already run continuously for 30–40 minutes, your weekly volume has been reasonably stable for the past four weeks, you have no acute injury, your target race is 6–12 weeks away, and you can run at least three times a week. If you are just starting, aiming only to finish, returning from injury, pregnant, or managing cardiovascular, metabolic or respiratory disease, use a beginner plan or have intensity set by a clinician or qualified coach. This page does not apply.

**A generic plan cannot replace individual coaching.** The same weekly structure means a different load for runners with different volume, recovery capacity, racing experience and life stress. This page gives a framework you can inspect, not a personal prescription.

## Set the inputs first: goal, starting point and recent result

A plan does not start from "how much everyone else runs". It starts from four inputs:

| Input | How to get it | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| Goal distance and race date | 5K or 10K; count back from race day | Cycle length and when the taper starts |
| Current weekly volume | Average of the past four weeks, dropping an obviously high or low week | How much room there is to progress; prevents copying elite mileage |
| Recent result | A 5K or 10K raced or time-trialed all-out within the past 6–8 weeks, in similar conditions | Relative paces, instead of guessing |
| Days available per week | The days you can actually run, not the ideal plan | Whether quality work and the long run can be separated |

**How to derive pace from a result.** Start with a recent race or time trial close to the target distance: pace = time ÷ distance. For example, assume a runner finishes 5K in 25:00; the average pace for that 5K is 5:00 per kilometre. This is a worked assumption, not a prescription for you. If you only have a 10K result and want a 5K estimate, or the reverse, use the site's [race prediction tool](https://tools.paobu.org/en/tools/race-predict/): enter the recent result and the target distance to get an equivalent-performance estimate. It is an estimate based on an empirical formula, not a guarantee that race day will match, and it cannot replace an actual all-out time trial.

Once you have the result, use **relative language** in training:

- **Easy**: conversational effort; you can speak in full sentences but cannot sing. Heart rate is roughly 40%–59% of heart rate reserve (HRR) under the ACSM definition.
- **Threshold**: slightly faster than lactate threshold, "comfortably hard", close to 10K pace or a little slower.
- **5K pace**: the average pace of your recent 5K race.
- **10K pace**: the average pace of your recent 10K race, usually slightly slower than 5K pace.
- **Short strides/hills**: fast and relaxed, not timed to the second; the purpose is neuromuscular wake-up and form.

## Five structural parameters

A plan really comes down to five decisions. The table separates the example value from its basis, so an editorial suggestion is not disguised as a research finding.

| Parameter | Example on this page | Basis and status |
|---|---|---|
| Cycle length | Start 8 weeks before the race | Site planning example, not a research finding; Haugen et al. 2022 describe the annual cycle of world-class runners and a 7–10 day pre-race taper, not an eight-week amateur trial |
| Quality sessions per week | 1–2 | Site planning suggestion; Seiler 2010 observed that elite athletes training 10–13 times per week did about 20% of sessions as high intensity, roughly 2–3 sessions; amateurs should take fewer according to recovery |
| Long-run share | No percentage given | Public sources do not give a single share of weekly volume that a long run should occupy in a 5K/10K plan (unknown); this page only specifies that the long run is the longest easy run of the week and is separated from quality work |
| Intensity distribution | Elite: ≥80% of running volume low intensity; amateur example: 4 easy runs out of 5 | Haugen et al. 2022 is descriptive data on world-class runners; "4 easy, 1 quality" is an example counted by sessions, not a research-proven ratio |
| Taper | Start 7–10 days before the race; if using a two-week frame, reduce volume by 41%–60% while maintaining intensity and frequency | Haugen et al. 2022 elite description; Bosquet et al. 2007 meta-analysis of 27 studies; PLOS ONE 2023 review of 14 studies |

The most important warning is **not to mix denominators**. Haugen's "≥80%" refers to the share of total running volume performed at low intensity; Seiler's "about 80%" refers to the share of sessions. Those are different fractions. Treating "80% of sessions" as "80% of time" or "80% of distance" can make a plan look compliant while the actual intensity is too high.

## An eight-week example structure

The table below is an **illustrative assumption** that demonstrates structure. It is not a validated plan. Quality sessions use relative intensity, not splits to the second; the long run has no percentage, only a placement rule.

| Week | Focus | Quality session (relative) | Long run | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Establish baseline | 1 short strides or hill session, fast and relaxed | The week's longest easy run | Record only; do not chase pace |
| 2 | Introduce threshold | 1 threshold session slightly faster than lactate threshold, broken into short reps | Easy long run | At least one day between quality work and the long run |
| 3 | 5K-specific | 1 session at 5K pace, 3–5 minutes per rep | Easy long run | For a 10K goal, replace this with 10K pace or threshold |
| 4 | Lighter week | Quality reduced to one short session | Cancel the long run or make it a short easy run | Use the 24-hour response to judge recovery |
| 5 | Threshold + specific | 2 quality sessions: one threshold, one at 5K pace | Easy long run | At least two days between the two quality sessions |
| 6 | 10K-specific / race simulation | 1 continuous run at 10K pace, or two blocks | Easy long run | Do not try to hold full race pace throughout |
| 7 | Taper begins | Quality shortened; keep a few short strides | Clearly shorter; relaxed only | Choose the elite 7–10 day pattern or the two-week frame |
| 8 | Race week | 2–3 short easy runs with strides | No long run | No new shoes, fuel or course |

The table specifies **order and spacing**, not kilometres. The reason is simple: the same eight weeks cannot use one set of distances for a runner doing 20 km per week and another doing 80 km per week, and public research does not give one long-run share that applies to all recreational runners. Leaving distance relative to your own baseline is safer than copying a number that looks precise.

## Quality sessions: three types and relative intensity

| Session type | Relative intensity | How to derive it | Purpose | Example dose (not a prescription) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Easy run | Conversational; about 40%–59% HRR | No pace needed; use the talk test | Aerobic base and recovery | 30–60 minutes, by daily feel |
| Threshold run | Slightly faster than lactate threshold; near 10K pace or slower | Around recent 10K pace, adjusted by feel | Sustained effort | 20–40 minutes, split into 2–4 blocks |
| 5K-pace intervals | Recent 5K pace | 5K time ÷ 5 km | Race-specific endurance and rhythm | 15–25 minutes of fast running, broken up |
| Continuous 10K-pace run | Recent 10K pace | 10K time ÷ 10 km | 10K-specific rhythm | 20–30 minutes continuous or in two blocks |
| Short strides/hills | Fast and relaxed, not gasping | No conversion needed | Neuromuscular wake-up and form | 8–10 × 20–30 seconds |

The "example dose" column is an editorial suggestion, not a research finding. Recovery decides the real dose: if pain, stiffness or fatigue at the same time the next day has not returned to the pre-session level, the next quality session should be shortened or postponed, not forced.

## How to place the week

A five-run example week:

- Monday: easy run;
- Tuesday: quality session (threshold or 5K pace);
- Wednesday: easy run;
- Thursday: easy run;
- Saturday: long run;
- The remaining days: rest or walking.

Three placement rules:

1. **Leave at least two days between quality sessions**, and at least one day between quality work and the long run. This is an editorial rule, not a research finding.
2. **Easy runs must actually be easy.** If every run is "moderately fast", you get neither low-intensity recovery nor high-quality high-intensity work; you just accumulate moderate fatigue every day.
3. **Change one variable per week.** If you add volume, do not add a quality session at the same time; if you add quality, hold volume. The site's [volume progression guide](https://learn.paobu.org/en/articles/training-volume-and-progression/) provides a four-week framework gated by the 24-hour response.

If you can only run three times a week, compress the structure to one quality session plus two easy runs, with one of the easy runs slightly longer. Do not put quality work and the long run back to back just to hit a session count.

## Taper and race week

The purpose of a taper is to remove fatigue while keeping the stimulus, not to stop training. Three verifiable anchors:

- **Bosquet et al. 2007** screened 182 studies and included 27 in a meta-analysis: the optimal taper was about **2 weeks**, with training volume decreased exponentially by **41%–60%**, while **intensity and frequency were maintained**.
- **PLOS ONE 2023** reviewed and pooled **14 studies**: a 41%–60% volume reduction, maintained intensity and frequency, and a taper lasting up to about **21 days** was associated with improved time-trial performance. A pre-taper overload produced a larger effect in some studies, but the authors note that individual adaptation is still required.
- **Haugen et al. 2022** describe world-class 5K/10K and marathon runners starting their taper **7–10 days** before the main competition.

These three do not fully agree: the elite 7–10 day description and the meta-analytic "about two weeks" are not the same thing. A practical reading: a 5K goal can use the shorter 7–10 day pattern; a 10K or a race preceded by a heavy block can sit inside the two-week frame. **There is no direct comparison showing one is better**, so do not claim that.

In race week, do only short easy running and a few strides; no long run, no extra quality session, no new shoes or fuel. If the taper leaves you more fatigued, with a clearly elevated resting heart rate or several poor nights of sleep, stop adding sessions and seek assessment.

## Traps for recreational runners

- **Copying elite mileage.** Haugen et al. 2022 describe world-class 5K/10K runners doing **130–190 km per week** across **11–14 sessions** in mid-preparation. That reflects an elite training history and recovery context, not an amateur starting point.
- **Three or more quality sessions a week.** The elite "about 20% high intensity" rests on a large base of low-intensity work and years of adaptation. Get easy running right before adding quality.
- **Treating a pace table as the plan.** A pace table assumes known conditions, fitness and course. Real races have wind, heat, hills and crowds. Relative effort plus a recent result is more robust than a table.
- **Back-to-back long run and quality work.** Two hard loads stacked together reduce quality and lengthen recovery.
- **Stopping entirely in race week.** The evidence supports a taper, not detraining; complete rest can blunt rhythm and coordination.
- **Top-up training in race week.** Extra sessions add fatigue and cancel the recovery the taper is meant to create.
- **Treating the eight-week example as a guarantee.** The example only demonstrates structure; no study validated this exact table for all recreational runners.
- **Pushing through pain to stay on the table.** The plan assumes no acute injury and a 24-hour recovery. If pain is still there the next day, stop or rewrite the plan.

## Evidence boundaries

What can be verified here is **structural parameters**, not "the best plan". Haugen et al. 2022 is a descriptive review of world-class athletes; it cannot establish that amateurs copying it will get the same result. Seiler 2010's 80/20 is an observation, not a prescription. Bosquet 2007 and PLOS ONE 2023 pool competitive athletes; extrapolating to recreational 5K/10K runners carries risk.

This page **does not** give: a weekly volume for everyone, a long-run percentage, exact pace tables, an optimal number of quality sessions, or a claim that one plan is proven superior. Direct comparative evidence for those questions is insufficient; a precise number would create false certainty.

## Checklist

- [ ] I know my race date and counted back the weeks from it.
- [ ] I derived relative paces from a result within the past 6–8 weeks, not from someone else's table.
- [ ] I have only 1–2 quality sessions this week, with at least two days between them.
- [ ] My easy runs really allow full sentences.
- [ ] I know the long run has no percentage here; it is simply the week's longest easy run.
- [ ] My taper keeps intensity and frequency while reducing volume.
- [ ] If pain, unusual fatigue or a clearly elevated resting heart rate appears, I will stop adding load and seek help.
