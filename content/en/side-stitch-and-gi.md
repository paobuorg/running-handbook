## What this page covers

A side stitch is called **exercise-related transient abdominal pain** (ETAP) in the literature: abdominal pain that appears during running or walking and usually settles after you stop. It is typically located in the mid-abdomen or one or both sides. It overlaps with, but is not the same as, needing the toilet, gastro-oesophageal reflux or discomfort from eating too much.

This page covers two common situations: **side stitch** and **running-related gut symptoms** such as nausea, bloating, urgency and diarrhoea. Most are not dangerous, but a minority of abdominal pain needs immediate medical care and must not be treated as a stitch. Recognising that boundary matters more than memorising any coping trick.

## How common it is

| Source | Population and finding |
|---|---|
| Morton 2003 review | More than 60% of runners reported a stitch in the previous year |
| Community event cited by Morton 2003 | In a 14 km run/walk, 31% reported symptoms and 42% of those said it affected performance |
| Baart 2023 survey | Of 1,993 runners, 1,139 (57%) had abdominal discomfort during or within 3 hours after running |
| Baart 2023 location | Upper abdomen 302 (15%), lower abdomen 1,115 (56%), both 278 (14%) |
| Baart 2023 impact | About one third of symptomatic runners said it affected their running |
| Baart 2023 associations | Female sex, younger age and higher intensity were positively associated with discomfort; prevalence across studies of distance runners ranges from 30% to 90% |

Two conclusions follow. First, running-related gut symptoms are common and are not a sign that your body is uniquely defective. Second, definitions and populations differ substantially, so a 30%–90% range should not be read as a precise "true prevalence".

## More like a stitch, or more concerning

| Feature | More like a stitch | More concerning |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Mid-abdomen or flanks, somewhat diffuse | One fixed, severe point, or persistent right-lower-abdominal pain that worsens |
| Timing | Appears during exercise and settles minutes to a few hours after slowing or stopping | Continues after stopping, or wakes you at night |
| Character | Cramping, aching or pulling when mild; sharp or stabbing when severe | Knife-like or tearing, with abdominal rigidity |
| Associated symptoms | Shoulder-tip pain possible; no fever, vomiting or black stool | Fever, repeated vomiting, blood or coffee-ground vomit, black or bloody stool, inability to pass urine, stool or wind, breathing difficulty, chest pain or fainting |
| Triggers | Repeated trunk movement, trunk extension, exercise after meals, hypertonic drinks | Recent trauma, known hernia, abdominal surgery, pregnancy |

Morton's review describes the typical pattern: pain can occur anywhere in the abdomen but is most common in the mid-abdomen, especially the lateral aspects; severe episodes are sharp or stabbing while milder ones are cramping, aching or pulling; repetitive trunk movement is the most common provocation and trunk extension aggravates it; symptoms are more common after meals and in younger people, and shoulder-tip pain may occur. These are **pattern recognition**, not diagnostic criteria.

## Mechanism: the old explanation has been challenged

For a long time, a stitch was explained as diaphragmatic ischaemia or traction on the inferior diaphragmatic ligaments. Morton 2003 notes that these traditional explanations have been challenged and proposes irritation of the parietal peritoneum as an alternative. The same review reports several trigger clues:

- Hypertonic or high-energy-density drinks provoke symptoms more than isotonic or hypotonic drinks;
- A hunched posture increases susceptibility;
- Palpation near the joint can reproduce the symptoms.

This does not mean the mechanism is settled. It means a stitch is not simply a diaphragmatic cramp, and "just breathe deeply" is not an adequate explanation for every case. The practical implication is to reduce known triggers rather than chase a definitive cause.

## What to do during a run

High-quality management studies are scarce. The following are low-risk, common field measures, not proven therapies:

1. **Slow down first.** Reduce pace to a level at which you can speak, and observe for 1–2 minutes.
2. **Change trunk posture.** Stop hunching and gently extend the trunk; if slowing and extending do not help, stop running or switch to walking.
3. **Adjust breathing.** Slow the breathing rate and avoid shallow, rapid panting; do not hold your breath and sprint through the pain.
4. **Do not add load while it hurts.** If the same pain appears at the same pace and duration each time, shorten or slow that session first and observe.
5. **Record the trigger.** What you ate and drank in the hours before, the drink concentration and whether you ran soon after a meal are more useful than "it happened again".

If the pain persists after stopping, intensifies, or comes with any red flag below, do not keep calling it a stitch.

## Common triggers for gut symptoms

Baart 2023 found associations with female sex, younger age and higher intensity. These are associations, not causes. Variables worth testing one at a time include:

| Variable | What to try |
|---|---|
| Drink concentration | Hypertonic drinks provoke stitches more than isotonic or hypotonic ones; try a lower concentration or separate carbohydrate from fluid |
| Timing of food | Adjust the size of the meal 1–4 hours before running and the gap between eating and starting |
| Food type | High-fibre, high-fat, spicy and dairy foods cause problems for some runners; change one variable at a time |
| Supplements and caffeine | Caffeine, gels and sweeteners affect some people; record rather than guess |
| Intensity and dehydration | Higher intensity and greater dehydration reduce gut blood flow; this is not a simple "drink more and it will resolve" relationship |
| Race anxiety | Anxiety changes gut motility and eating rhythm; a fixed race-day routine is more useful than a last-minute change |

These checks are practical experience, not research conclusions. Their value is turning "it happens every time" into "I know which variable is responsible". People with irritable bowel syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, lactose intolerance or gastro-oesophageal reflux should manage those conditions first rather than relying only on race-day adjustments.

## Red flags that need emergency care

NHS lists the following emergency signals for abdominal pain:

- Sudden or very severe abdominal pain;
- Pain when the abdomen is touched;
- Vomiting blood or material that looks like coffee grounds;
- Black or bloody stool;
- Being unable to pass urine, stool or wind;
- Difficulty breathing;
- Chest pain;
- Vomiting in a person with diabetes;
- Collapse or fainting.

Any of these should be treated as an emergency. Do not continue running, do not drive yourself, and do not mask the symptoms with painkillers.

## When to seek urgent medical advice

NHS also lists these reasons to seek urgent advice:

- Pain that is clearly getting worse;
- Pain that lasts or keeps returning;
- Difficulty swallowing;
- Abdominal pain during pregnancy;
- Unintended weight loss;
- A sudden change in urination;
- Blood in the stool;
- Unusual vaginal bleeding;
- Diarrhoea that does not settle after several days.

Runners should also pay attention to abdominal pain that **persists for hours after stopping exercise**, **wakes you at night**, **comes with fever**, **is sharply focal**, or **comes with rebound tenderness or abdominal rigidity**. Seek medical assessment rather than waiting for the next race.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A stitch is a diaphragm cramp, so deep breathing fixes it"** — the traditional mechanism has been challenged, and deep breathing is not a universal solution.
- **"Push through the pain to prove toughness"** — persistent or worsening pain needs you to stop and assess it; pushing through does not remove the cause.
- **"All running stomach pain is from eating too much"** — food is only one trigger; intensity, dehydration, drink concentration, anxiety and individual differences all contribute.
- **"Sports drinks are always better"** — hypertonic or high-energy-density drinks are more likely to provoke stitches; concentration and tolerance need practice.
- **"Take a painkiller and keep running"** — this page gives no medication advice; painkillers can mask emergency signals when the cause is unknown.
- **"Young women get stitches more often, so it is nothing to worry about"** — surveys do find higher frequency in women and younger runners, but common does not mean safe.

## Checklist

- [ ] Does the pain fit the stitch pattern of diffuse abdominal pain during exercise that settles after stopping?
- [ ] Is there sudden severe pain, tenderness, blood or coffee-ground vomit, black stool, inability to pass wind or stool, breathing difficulty, chest pain or fainting?
- [ ] Did the pain settle within hours of stopping?
- [ ] Have I recently changed drink concentration, meal size, the gap after eating, or intensity?
- [ ] Did I change only one variable and record the result?
- [ ] If the same pattern keeps recurring, have I arranged medical assessment instead of pushing through?

## Boundaries

This page is health education. It does not diagnose and gives no medication or supplement advice. The mechanism of stitches and gut symptoms remains debated, and field management lacks high-quality trials; separating low-risk measures from red flags is intended to prevent emergencies being treated as stitches. Pregnant and postpartum runners, people with abdominal surgery, hernia, inflammatory bowel disease or diabetes, and people taking anticoagulants should seek medical assessment earlier. Children and adolescents with persistent abdominal pain should be seen with a guardian.

## Example: one stitch in a 10 km run (hypothetical)

Suppose a sharp pain on the right appears at 4 km. Slow down for 20–30 seconds, slow the breathing and lengthen the exhale, and press lightly on the painful spot; if it eases within 1–2 minutes, continue at about nine-tenths of the original pace and take a small drink at the next aid station.

Stop and seek medical review if it recurs three times on the same stretch, or comes with cold sweat, nausea or chest tightness. This example shows the order of decisions and the stopping rules only; not everyone eases the same way, and it does not diagnose the cause of abdominal pain.
