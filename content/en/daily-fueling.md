## Definition and who this applies to

Everyday fuelling is the whole pattern of energy, carbohydrate, protein, fluid and supplement choices across training and rest days, not one race-day routine. Its core is matching intake to training load over time, with supplements only when the base diet is short or evidence supports them.

This applies to amateur runners whose goal is health, finishing or improvement. The condition: anyone in clinical nutrition therapy, pregnant or breastfeeding, or managing kidney, heart, metabolic or eating-related conditions should treat this page as background only.

## Start with the daily total

Recovery, daytime energy and race performance depend first on adequate daily energy. One low day will not ruin a block, but chronic under-eating can bring persistent fatigue, repeated injury or poor sleep. Check total intake and recovery before supplements; watch weight trends and hunger rather than calories.

## Carbohydrate and protein

Carbohydrate is the main endurance fuel; grains, fruit, legumes and potatoes supply it, and intake usually rises with volume. The ISSN position stand considers 1.4–2.0 g/kg body weight per day sufficient for most exercising people, with servings of about 0.25 g/kg or 20–40 g every 3–4 hours. These are population ranges, not exact targets. Protein powder is a convenience, not a requirement; protein may offset muscle damage but does not replace carbohydrate.

## Weight management is not a race-week project

Weight is influenced by sleep, age, genes and medications. The CDC notes active people can still gain weight when intake exceeds expenditure, and that suddenly eating much less before a race is not reliable. Make change gradual, preferably at lower training load, and avoid extreme diets before a key race.

## Caffeine

The ISSN review reports that 3–6 mg/kg body mass consistently improved performance in many studies, while very high doses bring more side effects. A common timing is about 60 minutes before exercise, depending on source. Responses vary; later intake can disturb sleep or worsen anxiety.

## Supplements and regulation

Supplements are not drugs. The FDA regulates them differently from foods and medicines: manufacturers evaluate safety and labelling before marketing, and the FDA can act after marketing against adulterated or misbranded products. Most runners with an adequate diet do not need supplements; if considering one, check ingredients, dose and testing, and discuss it with a clinician or dietitian.

## How to stabilise your fuelling variables in three steps

1. **Record a seven-day baseline.** Note staple portions, protein foods, training duration and weight trend.
2. **Change one variable at a time.** Adjust only breakfast protein, or add one carbohydrate serving after an easy run, for 2–3 weeks.
3. **Review and keep an exit.** If gut discomfort, persistent fatigue or unusual weight change appears, return to the previous plan and seek advice.

## Reference table for daily and training days

| Situation | Carbohydrate | Protein | Fluid and notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rest day | Meals matched to appetite and activity | 1.4–2.0 g/kg per day, every 3–4 hours | Aim for clear pale yellow urine |
| Before training | Eat normally; avoid chronic fasting | A 20–40 g serving | Drink to thirst |
| After training | Prioritise carbohydrate | A 20–40 g serving with carbohydrate | Drink according to thirst |
| Long or hot session | Calculate actual intake per hour | Keep the daily total first | More fluid may be needed |

## Worked example: ranges for a 70 kg runner (assumed)

For a 70 kg runner, protein is 70 × 1.4 = 98 g/day to 70 × 2.0 = 140 g/day; at 20–40 g per serving that is roughly 3–5 servings, spaced 3–4 hours apart. Caffeine at 3–6 mg/kg would be about 210–420 mg, commonly about 60 minutes before exercise. The weight and servings are hypothetical, not a prescription.

## Common pitfalls

- **Treating supplements as the base.** They cannot replace short energy, carbohydrate or protein.
- **Trading carbohydrate for protein.** Carbohydrate is the main endurance fuel.
- **Copying a caffeine dose.** Body mass, tolerance, sleep and anxiety differ.
- **Crash dieting before a race.** A sudden large cut may affect recovery.

## Frequency, cycle and progression

An illustrative rhythm: in a base phase, hold a fixed 10-minute weekly review, adjusting protein or timing every 3–4 weeks. In the last 8–12 weeks before a race, keep a tested plan rather than starting an extreme diet.

## When to stop and seek help

Seek a clinician or dietitian for persistent fatigue, repeated injury, feeling cold, hair loss, menstrual changes, marked weight change or a difficult relationship with food. Stop exercising and seek medical help for chest pain, fainting, unusual palpitations, or persistent vomiting or diarrhoea. The CDC notes healthy pregnant or postpartum women need at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, a population prompt rather than an individual plan.

## Checklist

- [ ] Daily energy roughly covers training and everyday expenditure.
- [ ] Carbohydrate rises with volume and is not squeezed out by protein.
- [ ] Protein is distributed by body-weight range and 3–4 hour spacing.
- [ ] Caffeine dose and timing were tested and do not disturb sleep.
- [ ] Any supplement has ingredient, dose and testing information.

## Scope and limits

This page does not prescribe calorie, weight-loss or supplement plans, or diagnose eating disorders, anaemia or endocrine problems. Persistent fatigue, marked weight change, menstrual changes, repeated stress injuries or food-related distress deserve professional help. The advice does not apply to pregnancy, breastfeeding or chronic disease without review.
