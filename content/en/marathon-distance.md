## Definition and scope

The marathon is a road running event over 42.195 kilometres, also expressed as 26 miles 385 yards. It is for runners preparing a first marathon, people checking results and courses, and readers of race archives. It applies to the standard road marathon, not an ultramarathon, trail event or self-measured route, with pace and results in one unit system. One case where it holds: a race measured over the standard distance with official results, where 42.195 km is the correct denominator for pace.

## Distance and units

The two expressions describe the same distance in different units. Training plans and pace tables should use the standard figure unless an event explicitly offers another distance. Writing "42 km" or "26.2 miles" is usually shorthand; pace calculations should use 42.195 km, otherwise a four-hour finish gains or loses minutes.

## How to: three steps to check distance and pace

1. Confirm the distance: check the official race page and confirm the standard marathon, not a category described as "about 42 km".
2. Unify units: keep time and distance in one system, using 42.195 km or 26 miles 385 yards.
3. Leave variables: write start congestion, aid stops, hills and weather into the plan, using the average as a baseline and split targets for execution.

## Worked example: splitting 3:30 into segments (hypothetical)

All figures are hypothetical. Assume a 210-minute target over 42.195 km: average pace about 4:59 per km and average speed about 12.1 km/h. For a slower first half, set the first 21 km at 5:05 per km, about 106 minutes 45 seconds, and the remaining 21.195 km at 4:53 per km, about 103 minutes 30 seconds; the total is about 3 hours 30 minutes 15 seconds. If start congestion costs 3 minutes and aid stops 2 minutes, recover about 7 seconds per kilometre on controllable sections.

## Common misconceptions

- Treating "42 km" as the exact distance for pace; the error accumulates to minutes.
- Treating the half marathon as "half the load"; distance halves, but fuelling and fatigue do not.

## Course, measurement and records

World Athletics publishes records by event and category; road tables distinguish mixed-gender and women-only races and mark records pending ratification. Listing is governing-body recognition, not simply the fastest time reported anywhere. A correct distance is only the first step: certification and measurement, start and finish layout and elevation rules all affect whether a result can count.

## Race rules and bibs

The BAA 2026 Boston Marathon rules state that bibs are non-transferable, participants may not run with anyone not registered on any part of the course, headphones are allowed only with awareness of surroundings, professional, wheelchair and para divisions have stricter rules, and all must follow the code of conduct. Transferring a bib misaligns medical and identity information and removes the result's validity.

## When to stop and seek care

Stop and seek help during a race for chest pain, fainting, confusion, inability to stand, persistent palpitations or severe breathlessness; these do not become safe because only 5 km remain. After the race, persistent vomiting, fever, markedly reduced urine output, altered consciousness or obvious swelling warrant prompt assessment.

## Frequency and progression

Plan by season: check distance and rule updates once a season; set one main goal race per cycle, for example every 12 to 16 weeks; archive results within 48 hours. Progress by running a solid half marathon, then extending long runs, and only then setting a full-marathon time goal.

## Checklist

- Event distance confirmed as 42.195 km from the official page.
- Pace and splits use one unit system, with no mixing of miles and kilometres.
- Start congestion, aid stops and weather are in the time budget.

## Scope and limits

This page does not decide whether a result can be ratified as a record, and does not provide entry eligibility or a finish-time promise. Measurement requirements, record categories and race rules are governed by the latest World Athletics, organiser and technical-official documents.
