## Definition and who this applies to

Race and shared-path etiquette is the set of actions that let entrants, pedestrians and other users coexist safely: bib use, pacing, passing, aid-station flow, litter, visibility and trail access. It applies to races with published rules and to public paths, where site-specific rules take priority. It holds when predictability comes first and your actions do not force others to swerve or stop. Race rules are entry conditions, not optional advice.

## Race rules are entry conditions

The Boston Marathon rules state that bibs are non-transferable; participants may not run with anyone not registered as an official participant on any part of the course; portable music devices may be used with headphones only while maintaining awareness, with stricter rules for professional, wheelchair and para divisions; and all must follow the event code of conduct and competition rules. Bib swapping, unofficial pacing, bypassing check-in, moving against aid-station flow or cutting across the course can affect safety and lead to disqualification.

## Start, passing and aid stations

Start in your assigned corral and do not weave sideways or stop for a photo without warning. Pass on the left or the specified side, giving space early. When you hear “on your left”, do not change line suddenly. Signal before an aid station, take what you need, then move forward before slowing. Use designated litter zones; if you must walk, move to the side. Predictable movement reduces collisions.

## Shared paths and trail boundaries

On pavements, greenways and park paths, keep to your side, warn before passing, do not take the full width as a group, and slow around children, older people, dogs and cyclists. Right-of-way rules vary; follow signs and local law. At night, be visible and slow at corners and blind spots. On trails, follow land-manager rules, do not cross closures or cut switchbacks, and do not disturb wildlife.

## How to do it

1. Read the race rules and note three boundaries: bib transfer, unregistered pacing, and headphone and aid-station conditions.
2. Warn before passing: assume 10 metres ahead and 1.5 metres of lateral space, not a sudden cut.
3. Signal at aid stations, assume 20 metres past the table before slowing, and use designated litter zones.

## Actions by situation

| Situation | Predictable action | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start corral | Stand in your zone, walk forward | Sudden weaving or photo stops |
| Passing a runner | Warn early, pass on the specified side | Cutting close or changing line |
| Aid station | Signal, take what you need, move on | Stopping at the table or moving back |
| Shared greenway | Keep to your side, slow near others | Taking the full width as a group |

Directions are examples; the race manual, signs and local law govern specifics.

## Frequency, cycle and progression

Practise in training: assume one of three weekly runs is on a shared path, making warnings, moving aside and litter handling default actions. In a race cycle, rehearse the start corral and aid-station flow two weeks before the event. Change one action at a time. Once habitual, crowded races need less decision-making.

## Worked example (assuming a 10 km run and a two-hour race)

Assume a 10 km greenway run, three times weekly for 50 minutes; warn 10 metres before passing, leave 1.5 metres of lateral space, and slow near children and dogs. Assume a two-hour race with an aid station every 5 kilometres, four stops in total; walk 20 metres past the table before slowing. These numbers are assumptions; actual layout follows the current event.

## Common misconceptions

- “Etiquette is just politeness.” Sudden line changes, stops or wrong-way movement create collision risk.
- “A bib can be lent.” Bibs are non-transferable; unofficial running can affect insurance, medical records and eligibility.
- “Unregistered pacers are harmless.” The rules prohibit them on any part of the course.
- “Headphones let you hear enough.” Use depends on race rules; awareness of surroundings remains required.

## Conflict, risk and seeking help

If harassed, followed or in a collision, move to a lit, populated place and contact police or emergency services; do not confront anyone. In a race, move aside and seek medical help rather than collapsing in the course.

On remote trails, leave a trip plan with route, contacts, arrival and return times and companions. Coverage may be absent; consider a locator beacon and conserve battery. Check weather, prepare for lightning and flash flooding and keep a Plan B. Carry enough water and turn back before half is gone. Carry a map, compass, headlamp with spare batteries, whistle, first aid and extra layers; GPS can be unreliable.

## Scope and limits

This page is not the full race rulebook, traffic law or land-management rule. The race manual and technical meeting govern a race; local law, signs and the land manager govern everyday routes.
